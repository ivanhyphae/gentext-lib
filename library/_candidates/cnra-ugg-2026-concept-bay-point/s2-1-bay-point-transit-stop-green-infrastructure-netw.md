that reimagines bus stops as community infrastructure capable of addressing extreme heat, improving public health, and supporting neighborhood resilience. The project will focus on priority transit corridors in the frontline underserved community of Bay Point, including areas such as Willow Pass Road where riders are regularly exposed to heat, traffic, and a lack of basic amenities while waiting for transit. Today, many bus stops consist of little more than a sign surrounded by pavement. During hot weather, these locations can become uncomfortable and, at times, unsafe for riders who have no choice but to wait outdoors. The project will retrofit selected transit stops and surrounding public spaces with green infrastructure designed to create cooler and healthier environments. Improvements may include shade trees, native and climate-adapted planting areas, green stormwater features, and pedestrian-focused enhancements that make transit stops more welcoming and functional. Rather than treating individual bus stops as isolated pieces of infrastructure, the project will create a connected network of climate-resilient transit stop improvements along the corridor. The network will include a range of intervention types tailored to site conditions and community needs. Depending on the location, improvements may include shade trees, green stormwater infrastructure, expanded planting areas, pedestrian safety enhancements, environmental monitoring equipment, and other climate-resilient features. This tiered approach allows the project to maximize environmental and community benefits while responding to the unique opportunities and constraints of each site. Before design begins, the project anthropologist will develop and carry out a focused humanistic research plan combining structured observation with brief, voluntary conversations with transit riders. Conducted at different times of day and under varying heat and shade conditions, the research will document where people wait, how they seek shade, use seating, move through the site, maintain sightlines, and manage accessibility needs or personal belongings. Conversations with willing riders will help explain these observed patterns rather than relying on assumptions about behavior. Findings will be summarized through an annotated site-use map and concise design recommendations. They will be brought into the project's community co-design process, where riders and neighboring residents can interpret, challenge, and validate the findings and help determine how they should influence trees, planting, shade, seating, circulation, accessibility, and visibility. A limited post-installation study will assess changes in site use, comfort, and shade-seeking behavior and inform recommendations for other Bay Point transit stops. Collectively, the flow of these segments are: 1) observation and rider conversations, 2) community interpretation and co-design, 3) design response, community validation, and 4) post-installation evaluation. This multi-benefit project will also have a workforce development component, with cohort participants comprised of local unhoused community members, and will be incorporated into project design and implementation. Participants, led by our partner SOS Richmond, will receive paid, hands-on experience helping establish and steward project improvements. Rather than creating a stand-alone training program, the project will use real construction, planting, and maintenance activities as opportunities for workforce development. The project will also explore the use of environmental monitoring equipment at selected transit stop locations to better understand localized conditions along the corridor. Sensors may be used to track air temperature, surface temperature, and air quality before and after project implementation. This information will help evaluate the effectiveness of shade trees, green infrastructure, and other project improvements while providing valuable data to support future planning and climate adaptation efforts
