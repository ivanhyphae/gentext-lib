Fresno County faces a growing public health crisis from extreme heat, with 64 days in 2024 exceeding 100°F. On October 3, 2024, the county recorded a scorching 105°F—the highest October temperature in Fresno's history. Climate projections indicate this trend will worsen, with annual average maximum temperatures rising by 5.3°F to 8.7°F by the end of the century. The number of "extreme heat days," defined as those surpassing 94.3°F, is expected to increase from the historical average of five per year to as many as 66 under high emissions scenarios. These rising temperatures exacerbate existing disparities. Over 55% of Fresno County census tracts rank above the 75th percentile on CalEnviroScreen 4.0, reflecting burdens such as poverty, air pollution, and health inequities like asthma and cardiovascular diseases. Many residents in low-income communities lack access to cooling resources, such as air conditioning, shaded pedestrian routes, or energy-efficient housing, leaving them disproportionately exposed to heat-related illnesses. However, in Fresno County census tracts are typically spatially large enough that their attributes tell us little about the relationship between built environment parameters such as shade from trees, and heat stress impacts. Disadvantaged communities, particularly in South Fresno, suffer from shade inequity; research using high resolution geospatial LiDAR data and metrics like Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT) reveals that low-income neighborhoods experience MRT levels as high as 122.7°F, compared to 115.7°F in wealthier areas. This disparity intensifies health risks for agricultural workers, who endure prolonged exposure to extreme heat with minimal shade
