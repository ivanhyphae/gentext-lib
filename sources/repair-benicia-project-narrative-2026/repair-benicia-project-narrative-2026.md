STRATEGY ALIGNMENT (10 points)

Provide a short summary (1-2 paragraphs) that outlines this project.

This project is a community-driven pollution monitoring and modeling initiative targeting the heavily industrialized Carquinez Strait corridor; focusing on Rodeo, Crockett, Vallejo, Benicia, Port Costa, and Martinez, where many residents face disproportionate pollution exposure and health burdens. We propose to focus on these communities due to existing partnerships with and support from local organizations and leaders, as well as community-identified needs for expanded air monitoring capabilities and public education around air quality. The project expands geographic coverage from past and ongoing monitoring initiatives further eastward into Contra Costa County to include residents in and around Port Costa and Martinez, and adds technical capabilities through a coordinated multi-tier monitoring system and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling. Multi-pollutant monitoring capabilities and an atmospheric modeling framework will be integrated with community sensor and wind pattern data. The project's signature contribution is its multi-scale CFD modeling framework, which integrates sensor data, wind patterns, and multi-pollutant measurements to reveal pollution pathways and test intervention scenarios (e.g., urban greening, traffic adjustments, emission reductions); quantifying potential air quality benefits and building an evidentiary foundation to inform future actions and community investments. Community monitoring is scoped to fill gaps in existing air district coverage: stationary nodes will be sited based on analysis of existing BAAQMD data, and the mobile platform will be deployed primarily in response to odor complaints and acute events.

Another key feature of this project is community involvement and participation throughout the project term, with multiple rounds of public meetings and outreach, and a workplan component to develop a core group of trained citizen scientists throughout the Carquinez Strait region, as a step toward a future Community Environmental Response Program. Through this project, we intend to build toward a more interconnected and collaborative community-led regional air quality monitoring network, with significantly expanded technology and training. Building on our organizations’ past and ongoing initiatives in this region, we will ensure that this project’s monitoring plan, equipment placements, and program development reflect community knowledge and needs. Through the integration of wind data, multi-pollutant monitoring, and modeling outputs, our project team will make local air quality data more accessible to community members living in the Carquinez Strait region. Our goal is to work toward closing data gaps often that leave communities without key information to better understand the impacts and movements of air pollution where we live and work, protect our health, and engage meaningfully with our neighbors and local officials.

Briefly describe how the project aligns with at least one of the Local Community Benefit Fund’s strategies:

Reduce air pollution and improve health outcomes

Build economic resilience for a just transition

Reduce air pollution or mitigate its impacts, improve public health outcomes, and build economic resilience for a just transition away from the harmful effects of a fossil fuel-based economy.

Advance integrated projects to holistically meet community needs.

Strengthen community-driven and collaborative solutions.

This project aligns with the Local Community Benefit Fund's (LCBF’s) core strategy of reducing air pollution and improving health outcomes in communities that have been most affected by refinery and industrial emissions. By establishing a community-accessible network for monitoring pollutants: particulate matter/PM2.5, ultrafine particles (UFPs), black carbon, toxic air contaminants (TACs), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and other industrial pollutants, and wind patterns across Carquinez Strait communities, we will empower residents with the data they need to better understand personal pollution exposure and take steps to protect their health. This multi-tiered project involving monitoring, modeling, researching environmental response programs will also help to mitigate the impacts of air pollution by informing future design interventions to reduce community exposure.

This project will also advance integrated projects to holistically meet community needs by expanding upon existing air pollution monitoring networks that have been and are being installed in this region through other community based organizations’ initiatives. Our project team has been deeply involved in past and ongoing efforts to improve local air quality monitoring, and we are in regular communications with community leaders, air quality experts, and other organizations in this area. We seek to integrate and expand upon progress that has already been made with air monitoring systems and data access, and strengthen connections between our neighbors across the Carquinez Strait area.

This project also aligns with the LCBF's strategy of strengthening community-driven and collaborative solutions by addressing several interrelated needs that have already been identified by community members: real-time monitoring, incident response, and predictive modeling; while also positioning community members not just as recipients of data, but as producers and designers of it. Community input and feedback throughout the project term will shape what gets measured and modeled, and data being gathered will be structured to be informative to residents and officials alike. By prioritizing outreach, coalition building and communications, resident participation and input, we seek to build a durable foundation for ongoing community participation and collaboration long after the grant period concludes.

COMMUNITY IMPACT (20 points)

Describe the location of your project. If applicable, please include an address. If multiple locations or neighborhoods are served by your project, please list all.

The project has no single fixed address, monitoring, engagement, and pollution monitoring activities are distributed across the full corridor. We will engage with communities across the Carquinez Strait refinery corridor in Contra Costa and Solano Counties, organized around five geographic hubs corresponding to areas of highest industrial activity and community exposure risk:

Rodeo: Phillips 66 Rodeo Renewed Refinery, Sunoco (former Nustar) tank farm and terminal

Crockett: industrial facilities, centrally located between refineries and traffic corridors, with sparse monitoring equipment and complex terrain

Vallejo: Port of Vallejo, industrial facilities, I-80/I-780 corridor, state highways 29 & 37, with existing community air monitoring network and capabilities

Benicia: centrally located between refineries, industrial facilities, and traffic corridors, with existing community air monitoring network and capabilities

Martinez: home to Martinez Renewable Fuels Refinery (Marathon) and Martinez Refining Company, with sparse air monitoring

Describe community engagement efforts related to the project. Describe the methods used to identify community needs and priorities, and how community-based organizations, residents and other key stakeholders were meaningfully involved in the determination of community needs.

Community engagement will inform every aspect of this project, as past community engagement has already informed each workplan element we are proposing. The project builds directly on years of community-led environmental organizing in the Rodeo - Martinez refinery corridor, as well as community-informed air monitoring plans currently under development. Our project design also reflects the priorities, knowledge, and lived experience of local residents who have been advocating for improved data collection and access for years.

This project does not start from scratch, but builds upon existing air quality monitoring networks and several related community-informed initiatives recently completed or currently in progress, detailed below in the ECOSYSTEM ALIGNMENT section of this application. Co-applicant organization Citizen Air Monitoring Network (CAMN) has already installed low-cost particulate matter sensor networks in Vallejo and is currently working with our other co-applicant Hyphae Design Labs to expand that monitoring network into Rodeo and Crockett, while also incorporating wind and PM2.5 modeling led by Hyphae. Our Children’s Earth Foundation (OCE) has been supporting this ongoing CARB-funded effort, and is now stepping into this project as a lead applicant to bring its organizational infrastructure as well as additional outreach capacity through its general environmental and civics education programs.

Developing a framework for the Community Environmental Response Program (CERP) is also a direct result of years of feedback from community members, who have consistently expressed frustration about the lack of real-time monitoring or accessible data during and after known pollution release events, such as the Thanksgiving 2023 24-ton spent catalyst release at the Martinez Refining Company, and the Phillips-66 Nigerian oil tanker spill incident in 2016 that sent 120 Vallejo residents to the ER with respiratory distress. These events underscored the interconnectedness of communities throughout the Carquinez Strait region, who are all impacted by air pollution from nearby sources depending on how the wind travels through our neighborhoods when pollution releases occur.

With all of this community-informed knowledge and experience in the background, we plan to proceed with community engagement through regular public meetings, online communications, interactions with local community leaders and citizen science enthusiasts. and continue some of the ongoing activities already being conducted such as participatory mapping to document pollution and odor hotspots. Public meetings will be held across corridor communities at accessible times and locations, with bilingual (English/Spanish) outreach ensuring equitable access and participation, and we will conduct general outreach throughout the project term to solicit input and feedback on monitoring priorities and monitoring plan design.

Describe how your project addresses the community needs identified in the previous question, including the specific communities your project will benefit. Describe the environmental justice, public health and/or just transition challenge(s) that your application seeks to address. Describe how the measurable outcomes achieved through your application will demonstrate progress towards achieving community impact.

The Carquinez Strait is home to some of the most environmentally overburdened communities in California according to CalEnviroScreen (5.0), with south Vallejo and Rodeo being in the 99th and 93rd percentile for asthma rates in the state, respectively. Many low-income residents here live near active industrial facilities and transportation corridors in this region, and experience elevated rates of cancer, cardiovascular disease, and adverse birth outcomes, in addition to asthma. Many of the most heavily burdened neighborhoods remain significantly under-monitored.

Several Carquinez Strait communities, including Rodeo, Crockett, Vallejo, and East Contra Costa County appear on CARB's official Consistently Nominated AB 617 Communities list, meaning they have been repeatedly recommended for inclusion in the Community Air Protection Program but have not yet been selected for funding and support under the AB 617 programs. Existing regulatory fenceline monitors sit below stack height, leaving plumes that rise above them and settle into neighborhoods undetected. Regional monitoring networks are too sparse to reveal differences in exposure at the neighborhood level. And when flaring events or accidental pollution releases occur, no independent community-informed capacity currently exists to measure what was released and where it went. Existing low-cost and grant-supported air monitoring networks need to be expanded further into East Contra Costa County, and additional monitoring capabilities beyond PM2.5 are also needed.

The recent transitions of the Phillips 66 Rodeo refinery and the Marathon Petroleum refinery to renewable diesel and sustainable aviation fuel biofuel production adds an additional layer of environmental justice concern. Renewable refining produces a distinct and inherent profile of potential air contaminants, such as formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, other aldehydes, and reactive VOCs resulting from lipid breakdown products that differ significantly from traditional refinery emissions, that have unique smells, possess known health risks and are not currently monitored at the community scale. Without independent monitoring, community members have no way to verify whether this transition is improving or worsening local air quality.

Our project will address these identified community needs in several ways:

Data gap: operating multi-pollutant sensors throughout Carquinez Strait communities will provide independent, real-time, neighborhood-scale air quality data in areas that currently have none

Monitoring during incidents: The CERP scoping will identify opportunities for environmental sampling during and after acute events that pose the greatest health risks

Renewable fuel transition: our dual chemistry monitoring framework and focus on toxic air contaminants provide independent community-informed data on the environmental health implications of renewable fuel refining

Expanded access to data: our community outreach and trainings will educate residents on how to access monitoring data, incident reports, modeling, BAAQMD’s air quality complaint portal, and other relevant information

Our project’s milestones and measurable outcomes will achieve meaningful progress toward community impact:

Outcome 1 demonstrates expanded monitoring coverage through the deployment of 16 stationary multi-pollutant nodes and 1 mobile response unit across previously unmonitored communities, each measuring a suite of pollutants including UFPs, PM2.5, VOCs, NO2, CO, H2S, formaldehyde, and wind. Note: A detailed description of the monitoring network design, instrument selection, siting methodology, and quality assurance protocols is provided in the Supplementary Monitoring Plan document.

Outcome 2 demonstrates progress toward improved data access by prototyping a community-informed app that will integrate existing datasets with CFD modeling data, and incorporate options to report pollution and odor complaints to local officials

Outcome 3 advances understanding of pollution sources, pathways, and exposure hotspots across the Carquinez corridor through a multi-scale CFD atmospheric modeling framework. Unlike regulatory-grade Gaussian dispersion models such as AERMOD and CALPUFF, which assume steady-state plume behavior and cannot resolve terrain-channeled flows, building wake effects, or street-level concentration gradients, the CFD framework directly simulates the physical mechanisms that govern pollution transport in this complex coastal and industrial corridor. Modeling results characterize existing conditions and, where relevant, community-selected intervention scenarios, informing future actions. NOTE: A detailed description of the modeling framework is provided in the Supplementary Modeling Plan document.

Outcome 4 demonstrates progress toward developing a community-led response capacity to gather data and engage with officials during and after pollution release events

Outcome 5 demonstrates progress toward meaningful community engagement with regulatory officials regarding project findings and insights

Together, these outcomes build toward the project's ultimate goal of closing data gaps that have left frontline and downwind communities without key information they need to protect their health, hold industrial polluters accountable, and engage meaningfully with complex governmental processes that shape their lives.

COMMUNITY ENGAGEMENT (10 points)

Describe the methods that will be used to engage community members on implementation progress and project benefits available to community members. Describe the methods that will be used to solicit community feedback during grant implementation, and how that feedback will be considered.

Community engagement is a fundamental aspect of this project, not just an add-on. The design, governance, and implementation of the project revolve around a multi-layered engagement strategy that ensures residents are informed, consulted, and empowered at every stage. Engagement will begin with targeted and personalized outreach to identified community advocates and citizen scientists throughout the Carquinez Strait region. The co-applicants for this project already have contacts, networks, and lists of people to help with this engagement, as well as identified ally organizations in East Contra Costa County who have expressed willingness to support our team and the project if it is funded. Direct community engagement will be supported by broader outreach efforts using social media, earned media, newsletters, in-person participation and tabling at community events and meetings, and word-of-mouth networking. All outreach materials will be produced in both English and Spanish to increase equitable access across the region's diverse communities, and remote/online participation options will be provided.

Instead of presenting residents with a completed project plan, this initiative will adopt an iterative design process that invites community members to provide ongoing input. Community input will shape monitoring priorities, sensor placements, and intervention scenarios modeled under proposed conditions. This participatory approach is especially important for residents from historically under-resourced areas who often feel ignored and excluded from environmental planning processes. Our outreach plan will incorporate public outreach and meetings in specific neighborhoods with high overall CalEnviroScreen percentiles reflecting overlapping health, pollution, and economic burdens.

Public meetings will be held throughout Carquinez Strait communities at accessible times and locations, bringing together technical experts and residents to share insights transparently and build coalition momentum. Multi-pollutant air quality data, pollution pathway modeling, and community odor and incident reporting will be made publicly accessible online, giving residents access to monitoring results and information about air quality in their communities. Community feedback will also be summarized and reflected in project reports and public-facing communications throughout the project term.

Beyond the grant period, our project aims to strengthen community ties through increased connectedness and empowerment. Community members engaged through monitoring and improved technical literacy as a result of our educational outreach will be better equipped to address future air quality challenges together. Every aspect of our project will be developed with long-term community ownership in mind — building upon existing foundations for community environmental monitoring, and extending beyond the 3-year grant period.

MEASURABLE OUTCOMES PLAN (30 points)

Provide a summary of the major components of this project, including the overall project vision, measurable outcomes, role of the Applicant and any additional partners.

  
The overall project vision and goal is to use community-informed data collection to empower residents of the Carquinez Strait region with information they request and need to protect themselves and their families from the health impacts of pollution.

Monitoring and modeling activities will inform future actions to improve local air quality and reduce pollution exposures. We will work with community members throughout the project to ensure that the data being collected reflects their priorities, and to help them navigate and use data to further their own goals for improved community health and wellbeing. The project also prioritizes forging and strengthening communities in the Carquinez Strait region that may be separated from one another by waterways, toll bridges, and municipal boundaries, but share much in common in terms of living conditions and pollution exposures in one of the most industrialized and environmentally overburdened regions in the Bay Area. By leveraging existing air monitoring networks and expanding the geographic footprint of the local air and wind sensor network, while also adding significantly to the technical capabilities of the network, we will vastly increase the amount and quality of data being made available to residents of the Carquinez Strait region.

Public outreach, meetings, and general education throughout the project term will help community members to access and navigate air quality data, while giving the project team consistent feedback and input as to project design and implementation, as discussed above. Community participation will also shape the research and scoping of a frontline community response program. Prioritizing public engagement throughout the project will ensure residents drive monitoring priorities and that impacted communities are consulted and engaged throughout project implementation.

The prototype app and CERP are key components of the project that arose from many rounds of community input over the years. We propose to scope and prototype these resources during this project, as opposed to fully develop and deploy them, due to anticipating time and budget constraints that may come into play, and recognizing the need for ongoing community input to ensure these resources will be truly helpful to community members when they are deployed in the future.

Finally, maintaining an open line of communication with the Bay Air Center will be a huge help to our team as we interface with community members and our technical team, and work towards developing the most robust monitoring system possible with the tools we are proposing to add to our local community’s air monitoring arsenal. Having access to advice, perspectives, and insights of the Bay Air Center’s team is enormously valuable for communities like ours, which are not yet included in the AB 617 programs and broadly lack a strong or established history of local activism or well-established community advocacy organizations. Most of the community-based organizations here are relatively new, or at least, relatively new to engaging in matters of environmental and public health. We enthusiastically welcome access to and support from the Bay Air Center as we work to build up local programs and capacities around air quality.

CAPACITY (10 points)

Describe the Project Lead’s experience managing and implementing projects of similar scope and size.

Our Children’s Earth Foundation (OCE) was founded in San Francisco nearly 30 years ago with environmental justice at the core of its mission. Our impact model relies on community support and partnerships, and as a relatively small organization, we focus our efforts where we believe our team can add the most value. Often, our value comes from our team’s management experience as much as our subject matter expertise in matters of environmental health and policy. We have demonstrated this value through participation in past and ongoing environmental initiatives in the Carquinez Strait region (described below), as well as through our community education and advocacy efforts throughout the United States, particularly in the Southeastern U.S. region.

For example, over the past decade, we have been involved with a multi-pronged campaign to improve water quality in the Tampa and Sarasota Bay areas of Florida’s Gulf Coast, partnering with multiple Florida Waterkeeper organizations to expand water sampling and hold major polluters accountable. These efforts generated multi-million dollar investments in water treatment infrastructure and ecosystem restoration projects. Our staff conducted public outreach, media engagement, strategic communications support, pollution documentation, and site investigations. Along the way, we also managed complex budgets and grant funding comparable to what we are requesting from the Bay REPAIR LCBF, covering sampling and analysis, consultant and contractor fees, and managing legal details such as MOUs and contracts for multi-party litigation in five municipalities throughout the Tampa and Sarasota Bay region.

More recently, from 2022-2025, we served as an active fiscal sponsor for Public Square Films’ Free Leonard Peltier documentary as well as two other film projects still in development, raising and managing more than $2.3 million, including over $1.7 million for the Peltier film alone through its production and impact campaign. Our fiscal sponsorship role involved managing contracts, grant and vendor agreements, reporting to multiple funders, and managing large teams of contractors and partners. Closer to home for our Executive Director and organizing team, we’ve been involved and supportive as we and other community-based organizations in the Carquiez Strait region have conducted other grant-funded projects described above in the COMMUNITY IMPACT and ECOSYSTEM ALIGNMENT sections. Describe how operations and maintenance of any physical infrastructure will be assured during and after the grant term. Describe any funding or financing sources that will be utilized for operations and maintenance expenses.

During the grant term, operations and maintenance will be governed by a Quality Assurance Project Plan (QAPP) developed in the initial project phase, which will prescribe sensor maintenance and calibration protocols, schedules, and acceptance criteria. Calibration and reference equipment maintained by Hyphae at its Oakland facility will support these protocols, including a 2B Technologies Model 306 ozone calibrator, a 2B Technologies POM ozone monitor, a Monitor Labs 9830B CO analyzer with calibration gas regulators, an ACD CAL2000 NO₂ source, and a Teledyne T200U NOₓ analyzer, along with associated calibration protocols and software tools. The deployment plan includes training community members to perform routine calibration and maintenance using this equipment, supported by Hyphae technical staff.

After the grant term, Hyphae will continue to host and maintain this calibration and reference equipment at its Oakland facility and keep it available to community members for ongoing project sensor maintenance. Routine post-term O\&M costs such as calibration labor, consumables, gas, and periodic sensor replacement will initially be covered through Hyphae's organizational overhead, ensuring continuity of operations independent of grant funding.

Separately, the mobile response component is designed as a prototype for a more comprehensive community-based emergency response network. Hyphae and its partners will leverage the project's demonstrated outcomes and data to pursue additional funding to sustain and expand the network beyond its initial scope.

Past experience in the Carquinez Strait region has taught us that sustained participation across multi-year project periods can be a challenge, which is one reason that we’re proposing to conduct bi-annual public meetings instead of more frequent meetings. We will also adapt our engagement model to incorporate community feedback and ensure multiple, easy entry points for participation, such as hosting events in different locations, providing remote access options, and providing meeting materials for later review. Participation trends and feedback will be reviewed regularly to identify barriers and work toward improving outreach and engagement strategies.

Providing food at community meetings and stipends for sensor hosts are proven strategies for encouraging community participation and demonstrating our appreciation for community members at the same time. Language access is also a potential barrier for some community members. OCE will provide bilingual English and Spanish outreach materials and translation services at public events throughout the project term.

We have also identified potential challenges involving community-led, participatory program development processes, which may result in timeline extensions for project components such as the CERP program and community platform app. To account for these challenges, we are proposing to scope and prototype these programs instead of promising full deployment, to allow for a flexible and community-responsive process while also maintaining overall project goals and deliverables.

This adaptive approach is a core strength of the project and will help ensure that our Carquinez Monitoring Initiative remains genuinely community-driven throughout project implementation.
