**PROPOSED WORK DESCRIPTION**
*Describe the proposed project. Include a detailed description of the proposed activities, including location of the work, frequency, and duration of interaction with proposed beneficiaries, the timeline for the entire project, and the number and demographics of beneficiaries/participants expected to be served.**** ****If the proposed project involves a partnership with other organizations, describe how the organizations will collaborate and the complementary roles each partner will play.*
The proposed fifteen-month project aims to develop an actionable, neighborhood-scale, multi-benefit greening plan for a section of Southwest Philadelphia’s emerging Africatown, with a focus area along Woodland Avenue between South 61st and South 68th Streets. The revitalization of this commercial corridor is the first stage in our larger vision to make Africatown a thriving economic and cultural hub for the global African diaspora—one that fosters business ownership, job creation, and environmental resilience while positioning the neighborhood as a destination for cultural tourism and investment.
Community-led from start to finish, this plan will employ an innovative, evidence-based framework that integrates environmental and social data collection, analysis, design, and modeling to identify strategic green infrastructure, tree planting, and streetscape improvements. These interventions will mitigate urban heat, manage stormwater runoff, and improve air quality while reinforcing Africatown’s identity through culturally specific placemaking strategies.
As shown in the attached PDF, the Philadelphia Department of Public Health has identified Southwest Philadelphia as having some of the city's highest surface temperatures, exacerbated by a limited tree canopy and excessive paved surfaces. Expanding the tree canopy is a proven economic driver, attracting foot traffic, reducing litter, and creating a more welcoming business environment. ACANA’s successful greening efforts along Chester Avenue, where 30 new trees revitalized the commercial corridor, demonstrated how tree-lined streets increase commercial activity and improve community perception. Applying these lessons to Woodland Avenue will reinforce Africa Town as a thriving economic corridor, boosting small businesses, supporting job creation, and enhancing cultural tourism. 
ACANA recognizes, however, that greening investments must be implemented in ways that prevent displacement and ensure long-term benefits for existing residents and businesses. To that end, ACANA is actively supporting homeownership initiatives to help renters transition into property ownership, providing financial counseling and advocating for affordability measures that protect low-income residents from speculative displacement. By partnering with local financial institutions, housing agencies, and community organizations, ACANA is helping families build generational wealth and maintain cultural continuity in Southwest Philadelphia, ensuring that the benefits of economic and environmental improvements remain with those who have long called this neighborhood home.
 Additionally, ACANA is committed to a community-led planning process, ensuring that greening interventions align with the neighborhood’s needs while reinforcing Africatown’s identity as a hub for cultural and economic empowerment. By supporting this project, the William Penn Foundation will help ACANA develop Africa Town into a regional economic engine that balances environmental sustainability with equitable development, leveraging Southwest Philadelphia’s existing network of 500 immigrant-owned businesses and positioning the corridor as a premier destination for the global African diaspora and other visitors.
To that end, the proposed Greening Africatown initiative aligns with ongoing funding streams, planning frameworks, and revitalization efforts already taking place in Southwest Philadelphia. This ensures that the project builds on existing investments while advancing equitable urban development.
Proposed Activities
1\. Geospatial Data Collection and Analysis
Hyphae Design Lab will begin by analyzing geospatial data to assess heat risks along the corridor. This analysis will identify sensitive receptors, such as trolley stops, community gathering spaces, and areas frequented by vulnerable groups (e.g., seniors and children). A key focus will be the 3.39-acre parking lot between 61st and 62nd Streets, identified as a major opportunity site for intervention. The data collected will serve as the foundation for meaningful community discussions, ensuring that proposed solutions align with local needs and environmental challenges.
2\. Community Groundtruthing and Initial Outreach
ACANA will convene the first of three community meetings to share the initial findings and validate them with residents' lived experiences. Stakeholders will help identify hotspots and prioritize greening opportunities. Hyphae will present a range of potential interventions, including:
  - Planters and street tree plantings along the corridor.
  - Bump-outs, bioswales, and rain gardens for stormwater mitigation to relieve Philadelphia’s combined sewer system, where stormwater runoff can overwhelm infrastructure and contribute to sewer overflows.
  - Pocket parks and parklets
  - Depaving opportunities to create additional tree wells.
  - Conversion of a section of the parking lot into a community park.
  - Trellises, canopies, street furniture, and other innovative cooling solutions at bus stops and key outdoor community gathering spaces. 
This meeting will ensure that community voices drive the project’s direction, creating an equitable and contextually relevant framework for interventions. Additionally, these improvements will enhance economic activity by increasing foot traffic to local businesses, creating more communal spaces, and promoting neighborhood pride and safety.
3\. Installation and Operation of Environmental Sensors
To capture real-time thermal comfort data, Hyphae will install heat sensors along the corridor to measure air and radiant temperatures, wind, and humidity—key factors influencing how heat is experienced in urban environments.
Two stipended local technicians will be recruited and trained to maintain the sensors and collect data, ensuring community ownership of environmental monitoring efforts. The sensors will stream measurements every 20 minutes to a cloud dashboard, where community members can access and analyze data as it is gathered. These readings will provide pre- and post-intervention data, allowing for direct evaluation of the impact of greening efforts on community heat exposure.
4\. Design Charrette & Selection of Two Priority Interventions
ACANA will then host the second of three community meetings, a design charrette, where residents will refine and select two actionable greening interventions from a set of proposed strategies. Hyphae Design Lab will facilitate this hands-on workshop using visual modeling and scenario-based analysis to help participants assess feasibility, effectiveness, and long-term benefits.
By anchoring decisions in both community priorities and scientific data, this session will ensure that the selected interventions are the most impactful for reducing urban heat and stormwater issues while supporting cultural and economic vibrancy.
5\. Greening Plan Development
The project will culminate in a comprehensive greening plan by the end of the fifteen-month timeline. This plan will summarize key findings, thermal comfort maps, and community feedback, and it will document the two community-selected interventions in detail.
Hyperlocal heat simulations will be used to refine these interventions, modeling their impact on thermal comfort, optimizing tree canopy placement, shade structures, and surface materials to maximize cooling benefits. This data-driven approach will ensure that selected designs achieve the greatest possible reduction in urban heat and stormwater runoff while aligning with community priorities.
These interventions will include:
  - Pre- and post-project environmental monitoring plans to track improvements in cooling benefits.
  - Site-specific designs for tree planting and greening to mitigate urban heat, improve air quality, and enhance stormwater management.
  - A comprehensive stewardship plan to ensure long-term maintenance through community engagement, local ownership, and partnerships with city agencies.
  - Recommendations for scalability and replication, developing a framework for future greening and stormwater interventions along ACANA-managed corridors, including Chester, Baltimore, and Elmwood Avenues, as part of their Africatown masterplan to promote climate resilience and environmental justice.
This plan will build on ACANA’s previous storefront beautification and Green Stormwater Infrastructure work, funded by the William Penn Foundation in 2023. Future phases of Africa Town will include additional infrastructure to support economic growth and increased visitor activity, ensuring that Southwest Philadelphia is both a cultural and economic destination point. These greening efforts align with the City of Philadelphia’s Green City, Clean Waters initiative, the Philly Tree Plan to which ACANA has already participated on its Chester Avenue corridor, and HUD funding administered by the Philadelphia Housing Authority, which has already allocated funding for ACANA under the $50 million Choice Neighborhoods Implementation Grant. Additionally, this work complements SEPTA’s Trolley Modernization Project, which will bring accessibility improvements and streetscape enhancements to Woodland Avenue, as well as the City of Philadelphia’s REBUILD initiative, which is investing in nearby community assets like Paschalville Library and Francis J. Myers Recreation Center. Philadelphia’s Vision Zero Action Plan has already identified 65th Street as a high-injury corridor, necessitating traffic calming measures, while Philadelphia’s Complete Streets Design Handbook prioritizes Woodland Avenue for pedestrian-friendly enhancements, ADA accessibility, and bike infrastructure.

These systems informed the equitable prioritization of streets and sidewalks for greening, focusing on heat mitigation. NSF SBIR Phase I and II Grants: AdaptOS Development (2020–Present)
Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform, developed with funding from NSF SBIR Phase I and II grants, enables community-based organizations (CBOs) and municipal partners to address environmental risks such as extreme heat, air pollution, and flooding with precision and scalability. This platform bridges gaps in traditional environmental planning by combining hyperlocal data collection, modeling, and design to ensure interventions are both cost-effective and impactful.
The AdaptOS framework is iterative, cycling through measurement, simulation, implementation, and assessment. Modeling is at the core of this process, serving two essential functions. First, it optimizes sensor placement by predicting pollutant dispersion patterns, ensuring accurate monitoring and data collection. Second, it allows communities to simulate the effects of proposed interventions—such as urban greening, truck rerouting, and shade structures—before implementation, providing clarity on which solutions will yield the greatest impact and benefit-to-cost ratio.
This approach addresses a persistent challenge faced by many environmental initiatives, as exemplified by Hyphae’s work on the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan (WOCAP). While WOCAP mandated significant regulatory concessions and outlined 89 pollution mitigation strategies, decision-makers lacked the empirical tools to determine which interventions would have the most significant impact at the hyperlocal level. AdaptOS fills this gap, demonstrating how interventions like tree canopy, vegetation, and traffic patterns can be configured to protect environmental health effectively.
By combining rigorous modeling with actionable design strategies, AdaptOS transforms environmental data into solutions tailored to community needs, ensuring measurable public health and environmental benefits. Hyphae has successfully deployed this platform in projects across Oakland, Stockton, and Dallas, delivering scalable frameworks that can guide future environmental resilience efforts.
The Green Heart Project: Pioneering Urban Greening for Public Health (2018 – Present)
The Green Heart Project began in 2018 as a collaborative effort between the University of Louisville, The Nature Conservancy, and multiple local and national partners, including Hyphae Design Lab. With funding from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and support from The Nature Conservancy, the Green Heart Project is the first clinical trial to study the effects of urban greening on air quality and its direct impact on cardiovascular and systemic health.
Impact:
Participants in neighborhoods receiving greenery saw 13–20% lower levels of hsCRP, corresponding to a 10–15% reduction in risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes.
Significant reductions in particulate matter (PM2.5) levels and urban noise pollution were documented, highlighting the multi-dimensional benefits of increased urban greenery.
Scalability and Future Research:
The Green Heart Project recently received $4.6 million in renewed funding from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) to expand its research over the next five years. The project will delve deeper into the long-term benefits of urban greening, exploring how increased tree cover affects heat stress, chronic diseases, and healthcare costs.

The proposed fifteen-month project aims to develop an actionable, neighborhood-scale, multi-benefit greening plan for a section of Southwest Philadelphia’s emerging Africatown, with a focus area along Woodland Avenue between South 61st and South 68th Streets. The revitalization of this commercial corridor is the first stage in our larger vision to make Africatown a thriving economic and cultural hub for the global African diaspora—one that fosters business ownership, job creation, and environmental resilience while positioning the neighborhood as a destination for cultural tourism and investment.
Community-led from start to finish, this plan will employ an innovative, evidence-based framework that integrates environmental and social data collection, analysis, design, and modeling to identify strategic green infrastructure, tree planting, and streetscape improvements. These interventions will mitigate urban heat, manage stormwater runoff, and improve air quality while reinforcing Africatown’s identity through culturally specific placemaking strategies.

NSF SBIR Phase I and II Grants: AdaptOS Development (2020–Present)
Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform, developed with funding from NSF SBIR Phase I and II grants, enables community-based organizations (CBOs) and municipal partners to address environmental risks such as extreme heat, air pollution, and flooding with precision and scalability. This platform bridges gaps in traditional environmental planning by combining hyperlocal data collection, modeling, and design to ensure interventions are both cost-effective and impactful.
The AdaptOS framework is iterative, cycling through measurement, simulation, implementation, and assessment. Modeling is at the core of this process, serving two essential functions. First, it optimizes sensor placement by predicting pollutant dispersion patterns, ensuring accurate monitoring and data collection. Second, it allows communities to simulate the effects of proposed interventions—such as urban greening, truck rerouting, and shade structures—before implementation, providing clarity on which solutions will yield the greatest impact and benefit-to-cost ratio.
This approach addresses a persistent challenge faced by many environmental initiatives, as exemplified by Hyphae’s work on the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan (WOCAP). While WOCAP mandated significant regulatory concessions and outlined 89 pollution mitigation strategies, decision-makers lacked the empirical tools to determine which interventions would have the most significant impact at the hyperlocal level. AdaptOS fills this gap, demonstrating how interventions like tree canopy, vegetation, and traffic patterns can be configured to protect environmental health effectively.
By combining rigorous modeling with actionable design strategies, AdaptOS transforms environmental data into solutions tailored to community needs, ensuring measurable public health and environmental benefits. Hyphae has successfully deployed this platform in projects across Oakland, Stockton, and Dallas, delivering scalable frameworks that can guide future environmental resilience efforts.
The Green Heart Project: Pioneering Urban Greening for Public Health (2018 – Present)
The Green Heart Project began in 2018 as a collaborative effort between the University of Louisville, The Nature Conservancy, and multiple local and national partners, including Hyphae Design Lab. With funding from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and support from The Nature Conservancy, the Green Heart Project is the first clinical trial to study the effects of urban greening on air quality and its direct impact on cardiovascular and systemic health.
Impact:
Participants in neighborhoods receiving greenery saw 13–20% lower levels of hsCRP, corresponding to a 10–15% reduction in risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes.
Significant reductions in particulate matter (PM2.5) levels and urban noise pollution were documented, highlighting the multi-dimensional benefits of increased urban greenery.
Scalability and Future Research:
The Green Heart Project recently received $4.6 million in renewed funding from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) to expand its research over the next five years. The project will delve deeper into the long-term benefits of urban greening, exploring how increased tree cover affects heat stress, chronic diseases, and healthcare costs.
