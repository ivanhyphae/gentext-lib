#### Program Funding and Purpose
The Governor's Office of Land Use and Climate Innovation (LCI) announced $27.5 million in competitive infrastructure grant funding for Round 2 of the Extreme Heat and Community Resilience Program.
Funded by the Climate Bond (Proposition 4) and Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund, the program supports infrastructure projects that mitigate extreme heat impacts and protect vulnerable populations.
#### Applicant Eligibility and Co-Applicant Requirements    
Eligible entities include local/regional public entities, California Native American Tribes, community-based organizations (CBOs), public higher academic institutions, and coalitions.
Most applicants must partner with a Co-Applicant to ensure adequate expertise and community participation, with specific pairing requirements based on the Lead Applicant's entity type.
#### Grant Types and Available Funds
**Early Infrastructure Projects:** Allocates $7.5 million for awards between $750,000 and $1.2 million to develop heat-related planning documents supported by a demonstration project completed within 30 months.
**Advanced Infrastructure Projects:** Allocates $20 million for awards between $3 million and $4.5 million to implement infrastructure projects informed by prior planning, requiring a Future Implementation Playbook and completion within 48 months.
##### **Application Process and Deadlines**
Applicants **must** first submit an **online Pre-Application Interest Form**, which features two submission windows: **Window 1 (closes July 21, 2026**, with technical assistance available) and **Window 2 (closes August 11, 2026,** without technical assistance).
Feedback and a link to the Full Application Form will be provided to applicants, with **final completed applications due no later than October 13, 2026.**
#### Scoring and Selection Process    
Applications are scored out of 90 points based on the project narrative (55 points), benefits to Funding Priority Groups (15 points), and the workplan and budget (20 points).
Top-scoring applicants will participate in an interview or site visit worth 10 points, bringing the total potential score to 100 points before final award recommendations are made.
####  Appeals Frameworks
Applicants can submit a formal letter to appeal determinations regarding Funding Priority Points within one week of notification.
Decisions regarding eligibility, incomplete submissions, or final application disqualifications must be appealed within 15 days of the determination letter.
##### **Support Resources and Inquiries**
Technical Assistance through a third-party provider** is available to selected eligible applicants who submit their Pre-Application Interest Form during Window 1.**
Additional support including informational webinars, workshops, and office hours will be provided, and written questions can be submitted via email.
#### Legal and Administrative Terms
Funded projects are subject to State prevailing wage laws unless specific statutory exceptions are met.
Submitted application materials become public records under the Public Records Act, and LCI retains the sole right to modify or suspend the provisions of the NOFA.

### **2024/2025 Round 1 Report (for reference)**
#### [20250529-EHRGPRound-1-AwardsList.pdf](https://drive.google.com/open?id=1GU3mXb1HDFUDZ8SwBdAhxEAy5Sard_HO)
The Extreme Heat and Community Resilience Program funds local, regional, and tribal efforts to mitigate extreme heat impacts, particularly in highly heat-burdened communities.
Round 1 (FY 2024-25) distributed nearly $32 million across 46 projects, while future Round 2 implementation will be supported by the Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund, Prop 4, and the Aliso Canyon Recovery Settlement.
The primary purpose of the Extreme Heat and Community Resilience Program Round 1 is to support local, regional, and tribal efforts to mitigate the impacts of extreme heat. The program prioritizes the state's most heat-burdened communities by providing funding, technical support, and evaluation for projects that build resilience.
Total Funding and Awards: Nearly $32 million was awarded across 46 individual projects for **the FY 2024-25 cycle.**
**Target Population:** The program specifically targets highly heat-burdened and disadvantaged communities, with over 95% of awarded projects representing a disadvantaged community.
**Eligible Entities:** Funding was awarded to California Native American Tribes, local and regional public entities, community-based organizations, and academic institutions.
##### Project Categories: The scope of work is divided into four distinct grant tracks:
**Large Implementation:** Funding physical infrastructure such as community resilience centers, cooling amenities in parks, and tribal microgrids.
**Small Implementation: **Focused on localized improvements like in-home cooling technology, HVAC upgrades for community centers, and heat-protective apparel for workers.
**Large Planning:** Supporting the development of comprehensive regional strategies, such as countywide Heat Action Plans and urban forest management plans.
**Small Planning:** Funding community-centered assessments, local heat response plans, and the creation of adaptation toolkits.
#### Large Implementation Grants
A total of $15,765,669 awarded to fund physical cooling infrastructure and localized emergency response upgrades.
Funded initiatives include developing community resilience hubs, installing spraygrounds, expanding park cooling amenities, and constructing tribal microgrids to ensure reliable cooling.
#### Small Implementation Grants
This category distributed $4,133,098.09 across smaller-scale neighborhood and facility improvements.
Projects include installing in-home cooling technology, upgrading community center HVAC systems, adding schoolyard shading, and providing heat-protective apparel for agricultural workers.
#### Large Planning Grants    
A total allocation of $7,741,170 supports regional strategy development and comprehensive heat action frameworks.
Activities focus on drafting city and countywide Heat Action Plans, designing school-based renewable energy microgrids, and forming urban forest management plans to increase tree canopy coverage.
#### Small Planning Grants
This track provides $4,294,225.30 for highly localized, community-driven climate assessments and toolkit creation.
Efforts center on creating localized heat response plans, conducting community-based participatory research, mapping natural cooling assets, and planning green schoolyards.

This ongoing project is led by the West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project with Hyphae support. Part of the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan (WOCAP) under AB 617, the project received initial funding from a $150,000 Transformative Climate Communities grant by the California Strategic Growth Council. Additional funding includes $300,000 from the Metropolitan Transportation Commission's Priority Conservation Areas program, matched with $600,000 from Alameda County Transportation Commission, $50,000 from the Port of Oakland, and $100,000 from CalFire. This multi-year initiative, funded through AB 617 and California’s Transformative Climate Communities (TCC) program, focuses on enhancing Stockton's green infrastructure to mitigate air pollution and urban heat impacts. Key activities include planting and maintaining over 1,750 trees within disadvantaged neighborhoods, creating vegetative barriers to shield residents from pollution, and implementing a prioritization plan for future greening efforts. Collaborating with Little Manila Rising (LMR), the project integrates advanced modeling techniques, such as wind flow and heat island simulations, with community-led outreach to identify sensitive receptors and optimize planting locations. The project’s deliverables include a long-term stewardship master plan and policy recommendations to ensure sustainability beyond the grant period​
  - **LA Depave Plan**  
      
    Partnering with Los Angeles County, Hyphae sourced and compiled data from over 20 agencies to create a countywide green infrastructure strategy. This comprehensive plan integrated GIS layers for land use, transportation, and climate factors to prioritize multi-benefit solutions addressing urban heat mitigation, stormwater management, and depaving opportunities. By overlaying environmental and social vulnerability data, the strategy emphasized interventions in heat-vulnerable areas to maximize cooling effects and enhance community resilience. The initiative was part of a larger Community Forest Management Plan funded by CAL FIRE’s Urban and Community Forestry Program, with an award of $1.5 million.
  - **Oakland STEP Project** (2023–2026, STEP Grant)  
      
    The Oakland STEP project integrated high-resolution parcel-level and meter-scale analyses, synthesizing remote sensing and political data to prioritize interventions for mitigating urban heat. Streetlight data for vehicle and active transportation guided the development of weighting systems that normalized diverse datasets and incorporated environmental justice and community feedback. These systems informed the equitable prioritization of streets and sidewalks for greening, focusing on heat mitigation. Hyphae collaborated with city DOT staff to improve engineering workflows for sidewalks and streetscapes, developing novel tree planting guidelines and protocols for tree well cutouts to enhance cooling and shading in historically neglected areas.  
  - **Southwestern Medical District Streetscape** (Dallas, TX)  
      
    Collaborated with the Texas Trees Foundation to transform the Harry Hines corridor into a multimodal, tree-lined parkway. Using a combination of environmental sensors and agent-based modeling, the project assessed heat stress and air quality impacts. Design solutions prioritized pedestrian thermal comfort and health-driven outcomes, validated through community engagement and site-specific data. Funded by multiple streams, including Texas Trees Foundation and other local agencies, the project integrates evidence-based methodologies to optimize urban resilience and public health benefits
  - **NSF SBIR Phase I and II Grants: AdaptOS Development **(2020–Present)  
      
    Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform, developed with funding from NSF SBIR Phase I and II grants, enables community-based organizations (CBOs) and municipal partners to address environmental risks such as extreme heat, air pollution, and flooding with precision and scalability. This platform bridges gaps in traditional environmental planning by combining hyperlocal data collection, modeling, and design to ensure interventions are both cost-effective and impactful.
The AdaptOS framework is iterative, cycling through measurement, simulation, implementation, and assessment. Modeling is at the core of this process, serving two essential functions. First, it optimizes sensor placement by predicting pollutant dispersion patterns, ensuring accurate monitoring and data collection. Second, it allows communities to simulate the effects of proposed interventions—such as urban greening, truck rerouting, and shade structures—before implementation, providing clarity on which solutions will yield the greatest impact and benefit-to-cost ratio.
This approach addresses a persistent challenge faced by many environmental initiatives, as exemplified by Hyphae’s work on the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan (WOCAP). While WOCAP mandated significant regulatory concessions and outlined 89 pollution mitigation strategies, decision-makers lacked the empirical tools to determine which interventions would have the most significant impact at the hyperlocal level. AdaptOS fills this gap, demonstrating how interventions like tree canopy, vegetation, and traffic patterns can be configured to protect environmental health effectively.
By combining rigorous modeling with actionable design strategies, AdaptOS transforms environmental data into solutions tailored to community needs, ensuring measurable public health and environmental benefits. Hyphae has successfully deployed this platform in projects across Oakland, Stockton, and Dallas, delivering scalable frameworks that can guide future environmental resilience efforts.
  - **The Green Heart Project: Pioneering Urban Greening for Public Health** (2018 – Present)  
      
    The Green Heart Project began in 2018 as a collaborative effort between the University of Louisville, The Nature Conservancy, and multiple local and national partners, including Hyphae Design Lab. With funding from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and support from The Nature Conservancy, the Green Heart Project is the first clinical trial to study the effects of urban greening on air quality and its direct impact on cardiovascular and systemic health.
Key aspects of the project included planting over 8,000 mature evergreen trees and shrubs across a designated four-square-mile area in Louisville, Kentucky. This effort targeted neighborhoods with historically low tree canopy coverage and high exposure to environmental stressors, including air pollution and urban heat.
The project implemented a **quasi-experimental design**:
  - Baseline (2018-2019): Collection of extensive health and environmental data from 745 residents, including biomarker analyses (e.g., high-sensitivity C-reactive protein or hsCRP), air quality measurements, and noise pollution monitoring.
  - Intervention (2019-2020): Large-scale greening, focusing on high-traffic corridors and residential zones.
  - Post-intervention Assessment (2020-Present): Continued monitoring of participants to evaluate health improvements linked to reduced systemic inflammation and improved air quality.
**Impact**:
  - Participants in neighborhoods receiving greenery saw 13–20% lower levels of hsCRP, corresponding to a 10–15% reduction in risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes.
  - Significant reductions in particulate matter (PM2.5) levels and urban noise pollution were documented, highlighting the multi-dimensional benefits of increased urban greenery.
Scalability and Future Research:
The Green Heart Project recently received $4.6 million in renewed funding from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) to expand its research over the next five years. The project will delve deeper into the long-term benefits of urban greening, exploring how increased tree cover affects heat stress, chronic diseases, and healthcare costs.
