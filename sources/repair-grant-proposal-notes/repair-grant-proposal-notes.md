# **BAAQMD REPAIR Grant **
  - Grant details: [Community Investments Office](https://www.baaqmd.gov/en/community-health/community-investments-office?fbclid=IwT01FWAQE5NRleHRuA2FlbQIxMABzcnRjBmFwcF9pZAwzNTA2ODU1MzE3MjgAAR7g5s_X2YU_JIyoBsX_PFZraW5TcIjqea8PJUC5rhyi18W5wCShigGGvD0JVg_aem_9037gUxn1gfm7st6e8R54Q)
  - **Application deadline: May 29, 2026** (Benicia Catalyst: May 1)
      - **Lead Applicants** must be a nonprofit, local government, school, tribe, or union. Hyphae participate as Co-Applicant
      - **Objective:** Reinvest air quality penalty funds directly into refinery corridor communities to reduce air pollution, improve public health, and build economic resilience for a just transition away from fossil fuels
      - Eligible projects include air pollution monitoring, emissions reduction, indoor air quality, urban greening, public health programming, and just transition workforce development
      - Three grant tiers: Seed ($100K–$200K), **Opportunity ($500K–$5M)**, Catalyst ($10–$40M)
      - **Strong emphasis on multi-stakeholder partnerships with deep community roots (scored heavily)**

### **Scope Decisions**
**1. Monitoring + Modeling + Prototyping — Not a Full Program Launch  
** The grant will fund development of the core technical infrastructure: multi-pollutant monitoring, CFD/atmospheric modeling, and a prototype platform. We are explicitly *not* committing to standing up a full emergency response program or launching large-scale volunteer coordination in the grant period. This is the right call — it makes the proposal realistic and improves our competitiveness.
**2. App Positioned as Prototype  
** The platform deliverable is a working prototype demonstrating: real-time air quality monitoring maps, modeling overlays, and reference materials. Features like rapid-responder volunteer coordination are deferred. The current contract covers scoping and specification — not building — and that framing should be reflected consistently in all documents.
**3. Rapid Response = Scoping Phase Only  
** The grant will fund research into how emergency response currently works in the county, stakeholder mapping, and community-informed design of what a future program could look like. We describe the vision and the path to building it — we do not promise to run it.
**4. Monitoring is designed to validate and feed the models — not just collect data  
** The core framing is that monitoring and modeling are coupled: sensor choices should be driven by what the CFD needs for validation, not coverage area alone. PurpleAir-type sensors can't measure VOCs — a critical gap for refinery monitoring. The network needs to measure a multi-pollutant gradient: PM, UFPs, VOCs, black carbon, ozone, NOx, and methane. Passive samplers are the right tool for VOC monitoring at scale — community-deployable, lab-processed, and their time-averaged output is compatible with CFD's Reynolds-averaged equations. This framing — monitoring in service of modeling, not as an end in itself — is one of the proposal's differentiators and should appear early.

## **1. Multi-Scale Modeling Framework**
The proposed modeling approach employs a nested, multi-scale framework that links regional atmospheric dynamics to neighborhood-level pollutant exposure. At the mesoscale, the team will leverage the HRRR (High-Resolution Rapid Refresh) model — a NOAA-backed forecasting system — as the foundation for initializing regional wind and weather conditions. This provides a physically consistent starting point that captures synoptic and seasonal variability across the San Francisco Bay Area and Carquinez Strait corridor.
From the mesoscale, the approach nests downward into a complex-terrain domain using WRF, a fast-running atmospheric model capable of resolving terrain-driven flows across the full project region (25+ km extent). This intermediate scale captures the interaction between regional winds and the steep topography of the Carquinez Strait, including the valley channels and ridgelines that govern pollutant transport between communities. Simulations will use representative snapshots — at least one day per season, with multiple intraday time steps — to characterize the most common wind conditions and their directional variability.
At the finest scale, micro-scale computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations will be performed for targeted areas of interest with a radius of approximately 2 km, with up to three domains per community. These simulations resolve building-scale and street-canyon flows that determine actual human exposure at the neighborhood level. For communities such as Rodeo, Crockett, Martinez, and potentially Richmond and Pittsburgh/Baypoint, the micro-scale domains will be driven by boundary conditions from the larger-scale models, ensuring physical consistency across scales. Community input will guide the selection of priority areas for micro-scale analysis.
This nested architecture is designed to be transferable: the same framework that serves one community can be extended to adjacent areas without duplicating the foundational mesoscale and complex-terrain work. As Brent emphasized, the modeling is a unique, transferable asset — something that a community like Martinez could ultimately use even without direct partnership with the project team.

## **2. Emissions Inventory and Source Characterization**
Rather than deriving new emissions inventories from scratch, the project will compile and synthesize existing emissions data from the air district, regulatory reporting requirements, and prior studies in the region. The air district has already conducted AERMOD and CALPUFF modeling in the area, and those datasets represent a starting point for understanding the baseline emissions landscape.
The scope of source characterization extends beyond refinery emissions alone. In communities like Richmond, freeway traffic has been identified as a dominant contributor to ambient pollution — a finding that reflects a broader regulatory asymmetry: refineries are required to invest millions in emissions controls, while freeway emissions remain largely unaddressed. The project aims to capture this full picture of chronic ambient exposure, including refinery operations, mobile sources, rail traffic, and fugitive emissions from pipes, leaks, and process equipment (including sources such as washwater ponds that emit aldehydes and other volatile compounds).
Brent noted that the complexity of refinery emissions characterization — fugitive sources, process vents, and area sources across multiple facilities — is itself a substantial undertaking. The team may consider limiting detailed micro-scale emissions modeling to one or two priority refineries, while relying on broader regulatory inventories for others. This tiered approach would allow the project to deliver deep analysis where it matters most while maintaining comprehensive regional coverage.
Advisory support from researchers with refinery and power-plant emissions modeling experience — such as Max Zhang, whose prior work focused on power-plant source characterization — will strengthen the emissions analysis. The team will budget for an advisor pool to provide technical guidance across emissions, modeling, and monitoring domains.

## **3. Monitoring Strategy and the Community Response Gap**
The monitoring component of this project addresses a well-documented gap in community air quality response. Research conducted during development of the Hyphae community air monitoring application found that across the refinery corridor, thousands of odor and air quality complaints have been filed with the air district, yet fewer than 20% receive a substantive field response. Communities and the air district's own Community Advisory Council have expressed frustration with the timing and completeness of regulatory response — particularly for nighttime events and episodic releases that are difficult to capture with fixed monitoring infrastructure.
This project positions mobile monitoring as a complement to — not a replacement for — the air district's regulatory role. The air district operates within constraints that limit its ability to respond to every community concern: thresholds for regulatory action may not align with the exposure levels that trigger health concerns, and the political dynamics of regulating major industrial employers create inherent tensions. The project team can fill these gaps by deploying high-quality instrumentation to respond to community-reported events, generating data that both addresses resident concerns and strengthens the air district's information base.
The monitoring approach begins with a thorough review of the air district's existing data and any prior monitoring conducted in the project area, identifying spatial and temporal gaps. Mobile monitoring campaigns will then be designed around community-identified priorities, with a primary focus on responding to odor complaints and episodic releases. This responsive monitoring model — grounded in community needs and supported by research-grade equipment — offers a more complete picture of chronic and acute exposure than fixed-site monitoring alone.
-----
## **4. Digital Twin and Community Interventions**
A central element of the modeling framework is the development of a digital twin — a computational representation of the built and atmospheric environment that allows communities to explore "what-if" scenarios. The digital twin will integrate weather data, terrain, land use, and emissions sources into a platform where community members can propose and visualize the potential impacts of interventions such as urban greening, vegetation buffer plantings, road diets, and modifications to urban layout.
Brent raised an important caveat: while the concept of community-driven interventions is compelling, the project team's modeling experience to date has focused primarily on roadway emissions, and the dominant sources in the refinery corridor are industrial. The team's approach will therefore frame the digital twin as building the capability for scenario testing, rather than committing to specific intervention outcomes. The modeling will demonstrate what is possible — for example, how a vegetation buffer might alter wind patterns and pollutant dispersion near a sensitive receptor — while acknowledging that the most effective interventions for refinery-sourced pollution may ultimately be technology-driven rather than design-driven.
The digital twin's value extends beyond any single intervention. By creating a pre-computed library of wind and dispersion scenarios matched to common meteorological conditions, the platform could eventually support near-real-time air quality mapping: local sensor data could be matched to the most representative pre-computed scenario to generate rapid exposure estimates without running full simulations in real time. This pathway from research tool to operational forecasting capability represents a long-term investment in community resilience.
-----
## **5. Pollution Transport Modeling**
Pollutant transport simulations will employ passive scalar methods, which model the advection and diffusion of contaminants by the wind field without coupling back to atmospheric chemistry. This approach is appropriate for the project's primary objectives — mapping pollution pathways and identifying source-receptor relationships — while keeping computational costs and model complexity manageable.
The passive scalar framework has known limitations. It does not account for gas-phase chemical transformations (e.g., ozone formation from NOx and VOC precursors) or changes in pollutant behavior during transport. For particulate matter, it also does not capture particle aggregation — the process by which ultrafine particles (UFPs) coagulate into larger particles as they travel from source to receptor. Since the project includes UFP monitoring, the team will evaluate the feasibility of incorporating population-balance methods to model particle size evolution. Juan's graduate research involved coupling population-balance models with CFD for sediment transport, and the same mathematical framework applies to atmospheric particle aggregation. However, validated parameter sets for UFP aggregation in atmospheric conditions are not yet available in the literature; this will be scoped as a feasibility study within the project's initial phase.
For volatile organic compounds and aldehydes — which are relevant to community odor complaints — passive scalar transport is a reasonable approximation, as these compounds tend to be already oxidized and do not undergo significant further transformation during short-range transport. Their deposition characteristics differ from particulates, and the modeling will account for this accordingly.
-----
## **6. Computational Requirements and Forecasting**
The multi-scale modeling program requires substantial computational resources. Mesoscale and complex-terrain simulations using WHARF run on CPU architectures; GPU acceleration is not currently supported, though upcoming model updates may change this. Preliminary GPU tests with OpenFOAM (the CFD solver used for micro-scale work) showed approximately 2× speedup for small test cases, but scaling behavior for production-sized domains remains to be evaluated.
At the mesoscale, WHARF simulations are designed for forecasting applications — a capability that could eventually support operational air quality guidance for communities. Brent emphasized that exploring this forecasting potential should be a priority: mesoscale models like WHARF could provide daily wind and dispersion forecasts faster and more efficiently than running full CFD, with CFD reserved for deeper analysis of specific events or scenarios. The project will lay the groundwork for this capability by developing the pre-computed scenario library and defining the requirements for operational forecasting.
The computational budget (\~$40–50K placeholder) will cover either cloud computing services or dedicated hardware. The team will label this generically as "compute costs" in the budget to maintain flexibility, as the specific rules governing cloud versus hardware purchases under this grant program are not yet clearly defined.
-----
## **7. Monitoring Instrumentation**
The monitoring instrumentation strategy prioritizes wind sensors as the primary deployment, supplemented by low-cost ultrafine particulate (UFP) sensors. Wind sensors are relatively inexpensive ($1,500–$2,000 each), require minimal infrastructure (no solar panels or complex power systems), and provide high-value data for model validation — the critical bridge between the modeling and monitoring workstreams.
The team's experience in Marin City demonstrated that co-located wind sensors can effectively link PurpleAir particulate data to wind conditions, even when the sensors are not in exactly the same locations. This validated approach will be applied in the project area: wind sensors placed strategically across the domain will enable correlation of existing PurpleAir data (already present in higher density in Richmond) with wind direction and speed, improving the spatial resolution of air quality insights without duplicating PurpleAir deployments that other grantees may already be planning.
The number of wind sensors required for adequate model validation across the expanded domain remains an open question. A baseline of 15–20 sensors has been proposed, with a potential scaling to 40 sensors if formal validation methods for complex terrain do not establish a clear minimum. The team will investigate published methods for determining sensor density in terrain-influenced environments.
For the Richmond grant specifically, the monitoring scope will be more modest: wind sensors and possibly low-cost UFP sensors, consistent with what is being deployed under the CARB/Rodeo grant. Larger mobile monitoring instrumentation (e.g., the mobile laboratory) will be allocated to the primary Bay REPAIR grant, where the full monitoring campaign is scoped.
-----
## **8. Drone Capability**
The project will include drone-based aerial monitoring capability to support thermal imaging, aerial photography, and potentially gas-phase measurements. The team will include Charlie, an existing certified drone pilot already engaged with the project, for immediate operational capability. A training budget line ($1,500–$2,500) will fund professional drone certification for at least one additional team member (Juan has volunteered), ensuring sustainable in-house capacity. The team will also flag community drone training as a potential engagement activity, with costs covered within the project budget where possible.
-----
## **9. Handoff from Existing Grants**
The proposal will clearly articulate the technical handoff between the existing CARB/Rodeo wind-modeling grant and this proposed dispersion and scoping project. The current grant established the wind modeling capability using OpenFOAM for terrain-influenced flows; it did not include pollutant dispersion modeling. This project builds directly on that foundation, extending the wind analysis into full dispersion modeling with emissions inputs — a natural and necessary next step that leverages the team's demonstrated capability.
Supplemental materials will include prior technical work (wind analysis visualizations, sensor deployment results from Marin City and Prescott) to demonstrate the team's track record without requiring new report-writing. As Brent noted, the visual outputs from prior projects — particularly the Marin City wind maps — have been effective in conveying the team's approach to reviewers and stakeholders.

**Joint Workplan Narrative: Bay Area Refinery Corridor Community Air & Wind Study**
**Richmond–Carquinez Strait–Delta Corridor (Richmond → Martinez → Pittsburg → Antioch → Crockett)**
**1. Overview and Purpose**
This joint workplan proposes a **three-year, community-driven air quality and wind study** across the Bay Area refinery corridor, spanning **Richmond through Martinez and the Carquinez Strait to Pittsburg and Antioch**, including major industrial sources such as refineries and power plants. The project is structured as a coordinated Opportunity Grant effort under both the **Richmond Chevron Local Community Benefits Fund** and the **Benicia-area fund**, and is explicitly designed to extend and amplify the ongoing **CARB Cycle 5 Community Air Grant (G23-CAGP-0537)** led by the Rodeo Citizens Association (RCA).
The project responds to a clear environmental justice reality: **low-income, industrially burdened communities along this corridor experience disproportionate exposure to air pollution**, including elevated rates of asthma, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and adverse birth outcomes. At the same time, these same communities suffer from a **systematic lack of air monitoring infrastructure**, particularly compared to wealthier areas such as Walnut Creek, Mill Valley, and the Berkeley Hills.
The purpose of this joint workplan is to:
  - **Reduce exposure disparities** by building a dense, community-centered PM2.5 monitoring network
  - **Understand pollutant transport** using advanced wind modeling and computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
  - **Empower communities** with real-time data and participatory science
  - **Inform regulatory action** by CARB and BAAQMD for future monitoring, enforcement, and planning
-----
**2. Alignment with CARB Cycle 5 and LCBF Goals**
This proposal is a direct extension of the CARB Cycle 5 workplan, which established a **citizen-science air monitoring coalition and technical modeling framework** for the Carquinez Strait region. 
The LCBF Opportunity Grants will expand this work geographically and technically by:
  - Extending the monitoring network westward to Richmond and eastward to Pittsburg and Antioch
  - Increasing sensor density and filling spatial gaps in disadvantaged communities
  - Scaling up the **wind and CFD modeling framework led by Hyphae Design Laboratory**
  - Integrating additional community engagement, health mitigation, and indoor air quality components
The project fulfills core LCBF goals by:
  - **Reducing air pollution exposure** through improved monitoring and targeted interventions
  - **Improving public health outcomes** via data-driven mitigation strategies
  - **Building economic and community resilience** through workforce development and citizen science
  - **Advancing integrated, community-defined solutions** across multiple jurisdictions

The study area includes:
  - **Richmond (Chevron refinery area)**
  - **Hercules, Rodeo, Crockett (Carquinez Strait corridor)**

  - **Pittsburg and Antioch (power plant corridor)**

  - One of the highest concentrations of industrial sources in Northern California
  - Multiple refineries (currently five, potentially four pending closure)
  - Power plants in Crockett, Pittsburg, and Antioch

  - **Significant monitoring gaps** in PM2.5 and co-pollutants

  - High cumulative pollution burdens in **CalEnviroScreen-designated disadvantaged communities**

  - \~200 **PurpleAir PM2.5 sensors**

  - Supplemental sensors for future phases (VOC, NOx, ultrafine particles)
This builds on the CARB Cycle 5 baseline network and addresses known limitations of sparse regulatory monitoring systems by increasing **neighborhood-scale spatial resolution** .
Key outputs:
  - Real-time public dashboards
  - Hotspot identification
  - Longitudinal seasonal datasets
-----
**4.2 Wind Monitoring and CFD Modeling**
Hyphae Design Laboratory will lead a **multi-scale wind and pollutant dispersion modeling effort**, including:
  - **Complex terrain modeling (10–25 km scale)** for regional transport
  - **Microscale urban CFD (1–3 km scale)** for neighborhood exposure
  - Integration of:
      - GIS and LiDAR-based digital twins
      - Meteorological datasets
      - Emissions inventories (refineries, traffic, power plants)
The modeling framework uses **OpenFOAM CFD simulations** to analyze airflow, turbulence, and pollutant transport pathways .
Key innovations:
  - Linking **community sensor data** to CFD validation
  - Mapping **plume pathways from sources to receptors**
  - Identifying **high-exposure zones** for intervention

**4.3 Iterative Monitoring–Modeling Feedback Loop**
A central innovation of this project is its **adaptive, iterative design**, in which:
1.  Community input identifies hotspots
2.  Sensors are deployed
3.  Data informs CFD models
4.  Models refine sensor placement
5.  Results are shared and re-evaluated
This continuous refinement improves both **data accuracy and community relevance** .

**Stationary and mobile monitoring:** Stationary air pollution monitoring offers high temporal resolution and large datasets at fixed locations. This allows time to be conveniently treated as a variable both for before-and-after longitudinal assessment of interventions, and cross-sectional assessment of source attribution (from traffic data for example) and sinks provided by landscape features (trees absorbing pollution for example). However, stationary monitoring has low spatial resolution, so cannot provide detailed spatial mapping of pollution concentrations, which may also be important for longitudinal and cross sectional assessment of the impacts of landscape features on exposure. Mobile monitoring fills this gap by providing high spatial resolution measurements (for example every few feet along roads or pedestrian paths). However, mobile monitoring lacks temporal resolution as mobile runs cannot economically be conducted at all times. Thus we propose a hybrid approach that combines stationary and mobile monitoring. 
**High cost and low cost sensors:** High-cost monitoring equipment provides high accuracy and precision with low bias, enabling it to be used for regulatory enforcement, but due to the high costs these monitors cannot be used for large numbers of stationary sites and mobile platforms. Low cost monitors can be deployed in many locations simultaneously, but may have questionable accuracy, precision and bias. Thus we propose a hybrid method, where high cost sensors are colocated with all low-cost stationary and mobile sensors periodically for calibration and validation. Colocation will first happen for 2 weeks prior to sensor deployment. Calibration equations will be developed that combined temperature, humidity and multiple pollution metrics to infer reference values from low-cost sensor outputs. Sensors will output raw (uncorrected) values to cloud database for adaptable correction in the cloud. Colocation will be repeated at the end of the study. 
 
**Analytes:** The pollutants of interest are informed both by available public information on likely pollutants in the area, community knowledge of local sources, as well as what is feasible to monitor within the project budget. We propose the following analytes, their low cost sensors used for stationary and mobile monitoring, and the high-cost sensor to use for calibration and validation:
 **PM2.5 and PM1: **These small particulates are produced by combustion from local point sources, traffic from engine exhaust and brake & tire wear, and from regional influx including wildfires, salt spray and other sources. Low cost sensors available for these include both Sensiron SPS30 and Purpleair FLEX II sensors. Both of these sensors have been evaluated by the South Coast Air Districts AQ-SPEC program and have been found to have good correlations with reference instruments in southern California. Other literature suggests that the SPS30 may be more reliable for size differentiation (REF) than purpleair, although purpleair has the advantage being directly comparable to the existing purpleair network of public data, although this has a noticeable “hole” in coverage directly in our East Oakland neighborhood. We propose to colocate these with the Bay Air Center Sensor Verification System (SVS) which uses a Met-One 212 2 particle counter as reference. 
 **Ozone and NO2: **Ozone in urban air is often formed through chemical reactions between sunlight, nitrogen oxides (NOx), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). NOx and VOCs are produced by traffic and industrial sources. Typical urban concentrations of and NO2 are in the low ppb range. Low cost sensors available for these must be sensitive enough to discern these low levels, so sensors marketed to occupational health and industrial control are not usable. Furthermore, most low cost ozone sensors see significant interference from NO2 and so to get accurate ozone readings a simultaneous high-selectivity NO2 sensor must also be used. The SGX PS1 series of solid polymer electrolyte sensors offers low ppb sensitivity as well as low power consumption (which can help with solar-battery powered platforms), while Alphasense offers a selective NO2 and a combo O3/NO2 sensor in their B+ series that are also suitable. We propose to colocate these with the Bay Air Center Sensor Verification System (SVS) which uses a 2b POM FEM listed ozone sensor and a 2b model 405 FEM listed NO2 sensor. 
 **Carbon Monoxide:** Carbon Monoxide (CO) is an acute poison produced by combustion sources. Low cost sensors available for CO include the SGX PSI-CO-10 and the alphasense B+ series. We propose to colocate these with a Teledyne ML9830B FEM listed photometric CO analyzer as a reference.
 **VOCs:** VOCs are a large and diverse group of airborne chemicals of varying degrees of toxicity. While there are low-cost total-VOC (tVOC) sensors available, tVOC is not necessarily useful metric for health exposure, since some low toxicity VOCs (like acetone) may be present in high concentrations without posing a threat, while high toxicity VOCs (like benzene) may be present in small amounts while still posing a threat. Thus it is necessary to “speciate” VOCs, to identify and quantify each chemical species. There are no low-cost devices for direct VOC speciation. However, we propose using passive samplers to adsorb VOCs, and sending these to a lab which can run GC-MS analysis for VOC speciation. This can be done in a series of campaigns, where samplers are put in locations where suspected VOC sources are (auto paint shops, gas stations, industrial sites) for a couple weeks than then collected and sent to the lab. For each set of collection sites around a source, a wind sensor can also be included so that the wind direction can be included in the analysis.  
 **Wind: **Pollution measurements are made more interesting by the inclusion of simultaneous wind speed and direction measurements. This allows speculation about the source of pollution, for example a sensor next to a freeway that measures high pollution half of the time and low pollution the other half of the time might be more insightful if for example a colocated wind sensor showed that the high measurements only happen when the wind is coming from the freeway towards the sensor, while the low ones happen when the wind is blowing from the sensor towards the freeway. For stationary nodes we can use the Davis 6140 anemometer for this, and colocating with the SVS which has AIRMAR 220WX for validation. 
**Low Cost Sensor Platform integration:** We propose deploying* XX *stationary low cost sensor nodes and training community members with *XX *low-cost mobile platforms. Both the stationary and the mobile platforms leverage open-protocols for sensor fusion, telemetry and cloud data ingestion. Hyphae has created custom PCBs that integrate pollution sensors, GPS location, wind speed and direction sensors with sensor fusion microcontrollers and adaptive telemetry (LoRaWAN falling back to WIFI if available, which falls back to cellular LTE). Each stationary node measures PM from 2 sensiron SP30 (with optional purpleair socket), ozone, CO and NO2, wind speed and direction and averages the readings over 2 minutes, with telemetry sent every 2 minutes a cloud based time-series database. Mobile monitors use the same stack but with GPS and telemetry every 1 second, plus local SD card backup if telemetry fails. The mobile nodes are powered with rechargeable batteries and are small enough that trained community members can attach them to the roof of a car with magnets or suction cups, or put them on a bicycle for cycle routes or a backpack for pedestrian accessible areas.  
 
**Data Analysis and Interpretation:** Analysis will focus on 1) are there statistically significant differences between locations and times (do pollution vary across space and at different times), which will help us correlate pollution with sources, sinks, and transient sources like traffic. 2) source apportionment; when the wind is blowing from suspected sources, do pollution concentrations rise downwind? This will help establish which sources are most needing interventions. 3) cross sectional impact of landscape features; do existing trees serve as a sink for pollution? This will help inform design of green infrastructure interventions for air pollution mitigations. 4) with the sensors and mobile campaigns in place and ongoing, using pre-post analysis we can assess the impact of interventions after they are implemented.

# **New Sensors Allow Communities to Analyze Hazardous Air Pollutants**
Tiffany Dobbyn, College of Agricultural and Environmental Sciences
April 02, 2025
In communities across California, residents are grappling with poor air quality, often without a clear understanding of the pollutants in their environment. In Vallejo, a neighborhood near a cement mixing plant struggles with constant dust on the inside of homes, including kitchen cabinets, counters and tables. Other communities contend with traffic and aircraft emissions, industrial pollution and seasonal wildfire smoke.
A new project, led by researchers from the University of California, Davis, is trying to help these communities take action by bringing advanced air monitoring tools directly into their backyards. The [Community Air Project](https://communityair.ucdavis.edu/) is a collaborative effort with researchers from UC Davis, Irvine and San Diego, along with several community-based organizations in Northern and Southern California. 
The project aligns with California’s [AB 617](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=201720180AB617), a 2017 law aimed at identifying and reducing air pollution hotspots in disadvantaged communities. The team is focusing on five regions – San Bruno, Vallejo, Sacramento, Los Angeles and San Diego — facing high levels of hazardous air pollutants, including toxic metal compounds that have been linked to serious health effects.
### **Advancing technology for accessible air quality data**
To detect and measure toxic metals in the air, the team has deployed novel, low-cost air monitoring instruments developed at UC Davis in the lab of [Anthony Wexler](https://engineering.ucdavis.edu/people/anthony-wexler), director of the UC Davis Air Quality Research Center, and a distinguished professor in the Departments of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, Civil and Environmental Engineering and Land, Air and Water Resources. 
Wexler said the traditional, commercially available instruments used to measure hazardous air pollutants such as toxic metals can cost upwards of $200,000. In an effort to make air quality measuring more affordable, the California Air Resources Board commissioned Wexler to develop a more cost-effective device. 
Wexler and his team of post-doctoral fellows, students and researchers created the Toxic-metal Aerosol Real Time Analysis, or TARTA. Wexler said the parts to build the TARTA instrument costs only about $3,000, so not only is it more affordable, but it’s also portable and about the size of a small briefcase.
“It’s light and it’s small, you can just stick it in a car or carry it in a backpack, which we’ve done, and it only requires a small amount of power, so you can run it on a battery,” said Wexler.
The TARTA device works by pulling outside air through a nozzle, where airborne particles accumulate on a surface over time. A high-voltage spark then heats the collected particles, causing them to emit light at specific wavelengths. 
“That energy basically makes a big spark that rapidly blasts and heats the deposited particles,” Wexler explained. “When they cool, they emit light of a certain wavelength that says we’ve got lead, or arsenic, or chromium or something else.”

Brenda Lopez Reyna, a postdoctoral researcher in Wexler’s lab, said the TARTA has undergone several improvements to make it more efficient. One key upgrade is a 3D-printed part that stabilizes the electrodes and improves detection, allowing the device to measure lower concentrations more accurately.
So far, there are seven TARTA devices being used across the state. The TARTA can be used in real-time to potentially track the sources of polluted air, based on wind direction and other factors. This allows community groups to target pollution hotspots and gather data at times when certain pollutants, like those from manufacturing plants, are emitted at higher levels.

## **Q17 — Barriers and Risk Mitigation (draft)**
**Technical Modeling Challenge: Multi-Scale Coupling**
The most significant technical challenge in Bay REPAIR's modeling work is the downscaling problem: transitioning from mesoscale atmospheric model outputs (typically 500m–3km grid resolution) to the sub-100m resolution needed for neighborhood-scale CFD around refinery facilities. This boundary-coupling challenge — producing unphysical behavior where coarse and fine-resolution models must be reconciled — is a known limitation in the field and not unique to this project. Bay REPAIR addresses it through two strategies: first, by pursuing partnership with academic researchers who have developed advanced WRF model variants incorporating vegetation and building effects at 500m resolution, whose outputs are better matched to CFD boundary conditions than standard public WRF libraries; and second, by allocating dedicated budget (approximately $100,000) for a structured technical development phase in collaboration with expert partners, rather than treating the coupling problem as solved. The project's monitoring network is designed to provide the validation data needed to evaluate model performance honestly as development proceeds. # Monitoring Plan

**<comment_start id=kix.xjfpedchsd8c>Bay REPAIR Monitoring Plan<comment_end id=kix.xjfpedchsd8c>**
This monitoring plan includes 2 broad approaches: 1) a set of stationary monitoring nodes will be strategically located, ideally with simultaneous measurement of prevailing upwind and downwind sites for each major source of concern, and 2) a mobile response platform prototype that can be activated on short notice to respond to community reports of odors or visible plumes. This mobile unit can also be used for ongoing spot checks and extended mobile monitoring campaigns. The mobile response prototype will be used to further develop the concept with the future goal of creating a more comprehensive community based emergency response system. 
**Stationary Nodes:**
Each stationary node will include the following components (in some cases multiple options are described which can be selected during the peer review of the methodology:
1.  **Adaptive telemetry and sensor fusion stack: **A microcontroller with associated radio modules will ingest data from various sensors, and transmit the data to the cloud, first checking for LORAWAN network, and if LORAWAN fails then, if WIFI credentials are available, checking for available WIFI network, and if that fails using a cellular 4g LTE backup. The nodes also log all data to a local SD card backup. The microcontroller platform includes accessory chips that allow analog to digital conversion of sensor readings from custom transimpedance amplifiers for 3 and 4 pin gas sensors, as well as multiple UART channels, RS232 converters, and i2c bus for reading other sensors. 
2.  **GPS:** a MTK3333 or similar GPS unit will be used to track sensor location. While the nodes are meant to be stationary, this enables the sensor to be moved as necessary without revising location logs in the cloud database.
3.  **Ultrafine particle (UFP) sensor:** A Particles Plus 15000-OEM Condensation Particle Counter (CPC) will count particles as small as 5 nanometers and as large as 3 micrometers. This device has a 1 liter water reservoir and water consumption of \<3mL/day, and so can run for almost a year without refilling the reservoir. 
4.  **Optical particle counter:** To facilitate relating the UFP data to more commonly available PM2.5 data (for example derived from regulatory monitors or community based purpleair and clarity networks) the nodes will also include an OPC like the Plantower 5300, Sensiron SPS30 or Piera 7100. This will help in communicating the important gaps in ongoing monitoring initiatives as they relate to UFPs.
5.  **Ozone and NO2: **Ozone (O3) in urban air is often formed through chemical reactions between sunlight, nitrogen oxides (NOx), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). NOx and VOCs are produced by traffic and industrial sources. Typical urban concentrations of O3 and NO2 are in the low ppb range. Low cost sensors available for these must be sensitive enough to discern these low levels, so sensors marketed to occupational health and industrial control are not usable. Furthermore, most low cost ozone sensors often see significant interference from NO2 and so to get accurate ozone readings a simultaneous high-selectivity NO2 sensor must also be used. The SGX PS1 series of solid polymer electrolyte sensors offers low ppb sensitivity as well as low power consumption (which can help with solar-battery powered platforms) and industry leading cross sensitivity, while Alphasense offers a selective NO2 and a combo O3/NO2 sensor in their B+ series that are also suitable.
6.  **Carbon Monoxide:** Carbon Monoxide (CO) is an acute poison produced by combustion sources. Low cost sensors available for CO include the SGX PSI-CO-10 and the alphasense B+ series.
7.  **Formaldehyde: **the SGX PSI-HCHO-1 offers linear error of ±15ppb in a low-power, low cost package that may be suitable for detecting major deviations from typical urban concentrations.
8.  **tVOC: **The IonScience MINIPID-2-HS tVOC PID sensor has a resolution of 0.5ppb and a range of 0 to 3ppm. 
9.  **H2S: **The SGX PS1-H2S-10 has resolution of 10ppb and range of 0-10ppm in a low-power, low cost package that may be suitable for detecting major deviations from typical urban concentrations.
10. **Wind:** Wind speed and wind direction can be measured using the Davis 6410 anemometer, or the Calypso Ultra-Low-Power ultrasonic wind meter. 
11. **Temperature and humidity:** Temperature and humidity can be measured using the Sensiron SHT85, which has accuracy of ±0.1°C and ±1.5% relative humidity. This information is critical for applying corrections to particulate and gas sensor measurements, as they have temperature and humidity dependent biases. 
12. **Power management: **The nodes are setup to to use 12V DC power, and draw up to 1.5A with the CPC pump, OPC fans/heaters, SD card writing, and GPS running simultaneously, with brief bursts of higher power during telemetry events, which can configured from every 6 seconds to any averaging period. 2 minute averages for telemetry are often used to align with purpleair networks. Depending on availability, power may be run using low voltage wire from a nearby source, or if necessary, a 12V 80W solar panel and 100Ah LiFePO4 battery can keep the device running continuously.  
13. **Enclosure:** The microcontroller and power management are stored in waterproof enclosure. The CPC is also in the waterproof enclosure but has an inlet pump connected to the outside through a PM2.5 cyclone and PM10 hood. The OPCs are placed on the bottom of a radiation shield facing down into the open air, while the gas sensors a inside the radiation shield with airflow coming through rain proof vents. 
14. **Cloud infrastructure: **Sensor telemetry from LoRAWAN is routed through a nearby LORAWAN gateway to Hyphae’s cloud based lorawan network server (LNS) where it is then provided to Amazon Web Service (AWS) MQTT broker. When WIFI is used for telemetry, the data is sent directly to the MQTT broker. When cellular backup is used, the data first goes to the Blues Notehub platform where it is immediately routed to the MQTT broker. The MQTT broker broadcasts each sensor packet (indexed by unique device ID number) to any subscribers that have subscribed to the topic. Hyphae maintains multiple cloud data ingestion servers that subscribe to the MQTT messages, decode them and store the data in Influxdb time-series databases. Access to the MQTT broker can be provided to any interested community groups or agencies that would also like realtime data, and access to the database for historical data is also available. 
Stationary sensor locations will be selected based on community input about problem areas, analysis of existing data from other monitoring programs, and results of CFD modeling. In general it is preferred to have simultaneous measurements from prevailing upwind and downwind locations relative to a suspected source, so that wind data can be used to estimate pollution point of origin. LORAWAN gateways will be placed at a high up location (like on top of a hill or tall building) where possible to get line of sight for radio transmission from all potential sensor locations. Deploying LORAWAN gateways may turn out to be unnecessary if all of the chosen sensor locations have local WIFI available, and cellular can be used in lieu of either, although the Blues cellular radios come with 500MB prepaid data, and beyond that they must be paid on an ongoing basis. With well compressed payloads and 2 minute sampling averages the 500MB should last several years, but the LORAWAN network is also helpful for expanding the sensor network in the future, as it can be used to deploy lower cost LORAWAN nodes for a variety of purposes. 
**Mobile response platform prototype:** In the service of developing a more comprehensive community based volunteer emergency response program, we are prototyping a mobile response platform to learn how the community can employ air quality analytics to address localized real-time air quality issues as they arise. The mobile platform will include an electric vehicle equipped with the following features:
1.  **Sensor Fusion and data management: **A small Intel NUC or equivalent computer running Ubuntu with Robot Operating System 2 (ROS2) will serve as a sensor fusion server for integrating all sensor data onboard the vehicle. This computer will be fitted with a user friendly interface to allow community members to monitor processes and quickly troubleshoot monitoring equipment in the field. Fused sensor data will be stored on large NVME drives in ROS bagfile format, which can be exported to other database formats and uploaded to the cloud between mobile runs. 
2.  **GPS and IMU:** A u-blox ZED-F9R based RTK GPS with 30Hz data output and \~1cm accuracy will enable mobile measurements to be precisely geolocated, enabling detailed accounting of landscape features, and also mobile measurements at speed. This will receive NTRIP corrections over a cellular modem from nearby NTRIP casters. The GPS module will be integrated with a high speed gyroscope, accelerometer and magnetometer to enable dead reckoning for short periods (for example when driving through a tunnel, under an overpass or in other GPS-denied environments).
3.   **Ultrafine particle (UFP) sensor:** A Brechtel mSEMS-aMCPC miniaturized scanning electrical mobility spectrometer with advanced miniaturized condensation particle counter. The aMCPC can count particles down to 5nm with a data output rate of 180ms, enabling mobile monitoring at speed. The mSEMS can size particles between 5nm and 280nm with scan rates down to 1.25 seconds per scan, enabling size sorting of UFPs at 25mph drive speed. The size sorting can help with source apportionment, as the smallest nanoparticles tend to be fresh from combustion, while larger aggregation mode nanoparticles form as distance from source grows. Smaller nanoparticles are also more likely to experience deposition on leaf surfaces, and so the size sorting can help discern the mitigating impact of landscape features.  
4.  **Optical particle counter:** The mobile unit will also include an OPC like the Brechtel Model 9405 miniaturized Optical Particle Counter (mOPC). This extends the UFP sensors scanning range from 270nm up to 3μm with 72 bins at 1Hz data output.
5.  **ARA Instruments N-FRM: **An filter sampler is included so that the mobile platform can park in a hotspot and sample the air for between 1 and 24 hours to collect dust samples on pre-weighed filters. These can be assayed for black carbon optically, and then sent to a lab for EPA IO-3.5 ICP-MS analysis of airborne heavy metals. 
6.  **SKC pumps with VOC and aldehyde tubes:**  SKC pumps are included in the mobile platform so that operators can collect VOC and aldehyde samples on carbon and DNPH tubes to be sent to a lab for EPA TO-17 and EPA TO-11A respectively. This is especially important for responding to reports of odors. 
7.  **12VDC refrigerator:** A 25L mobile cold storage device is required to keep the VOC adsorbent and DNPH tubes below 4°C both before and after the sampling periods. 
8.  **MultiRAE pro: ** The multirae pro multi gas monitor can be used for 2 primary objectives: 1) using the ppb level tVOC PID sensor, plumes can be identified for further investigation with the SKC cartridges. 2) The multirae pro can have 6 sensors, so in addition to the tVOC, it can be outfitted with NH3, H2S, Formaldehyde, Mercaptan, and CO sensors (or any others from a selection of around 25 options). If an operator is responding to a smell complaint, and checking the area with SKC samplers and UFP sensors, these sensors can be configured to alarm in the event of a sudden, larger toxic release. This is a reasonable precaution in that a community odor complaint could be indicative of, or even presage a dangerous leak or release from refinery infrastructure. 
9.  **NO2 sensor: **a Teledyne T500 cavity attenuate phase shift (CAPS) device will be used for rapid and continuous monitoring of nitrogen dioxide with parts per trillion level sensitivity. Paired with the UFP sensor this can help distinguish between combustion and other dust producing sources. 
10. **Weather station:** a Calypso ultra low power ultrasonic wind sensor and SHT85 based temperature and humidity sensor will also be included to apply temperature and humidity corrections and to use wind data for source apportionment. 
11. **Drone:** The GPS, UFP sensor, OPC, and SKC pumps and weather station are light and low power enough to be deployed on a small UAV for assessing vertical profiles of pollution plumes. This can be done in specific campaigns where a certified drone pilot is available, which can be used to validate CFD models and to prototype and develop programs for future phases when the community mobile response team has designated drone pilots.
12. **Wind Lidar:** a ZX300E continuous wave wind lidar will allow measurement of vertical wind profiles from the ground in 10 steps from 10 to 300 meters above ground, with 10 second data rates (1 second average or each of 10 steps). This can tell us how the atmospheric boundary layer is performing with respect to refinery stack emissions heights and be used to assist validation of the CFD models.
**Colocation Experiments: **Each sensor will be colocated with a reference monitor for up to 4 weeks prior to deployment. If possible the colocation will be done at existing facilities run by the Bay Area Air District, though pending availability reference instruments may also be provided by the Bay Air Center, or rented and set up at project facilities. For criteria pollutants (PM2.5, O3, CO, NO2) FRM or FEM monitors will be preferred for colocation, for example Teledyne t640 for PM, 2B POM for O3, Thermo 48i-TLE for CO, and 2B 405, Teledyne T500 or T200U for NO2. For UFP sensors the colocation will be done with a recently calibrated higher quality instrument, for example Hyphae’s Brechtel aMCPC, the air district’s TSI CPC or UC Berkeley’s Spider Magic 810. Outdoor colocation will not be performed with the tVOC, formaldehyde, H2S, ammonia, or mercaptan sensors, but they will still be calibrated as described in the calibration section. Colocation data will be collected and used to develop calibration equations that leverage multiple linear regression, support vector machines, SGboost or other algorithms to support data accuracy using all available data.  
**Sensor calibration: **Community members will be trained to do periodic zero and span calibration on O3, CO, and NO2, and tVOC sensors. O3 calibration will be performed using a 2b technologies model 306 ozone calibrator. NO2 will be done with an ACD electrochemical NO2 calibration generator. CO will use 10ppm standard span gas cylinders. tVOC sensors will be calibrated with isobutylene standard cylinders. Multirae Pro sensors will be calibrated according to the manufacturer instructions. 
**Mobile Platform Training: **Selected community members will be trained to operate the SKC samplers and ARA N-FRM samplers, chain of custody for filter and tube handling. They will also be trained to operate the mobile monitoring equipment (UFP sensor, T500, Multirae, and data acquisition platform). During training, mobile UFP and NO2 data will be collected for building a data corpus for further analysis. Trials for mobile response will be conducted with odor reporting being conveyed to the mobile response team for field trials. 
**Quality Assurance and Community Data Protocol Plan:** In the first stage of the project, a comprehensive Quality Assurance Project Plan (QAPP) will be developed and submitted for peer review by air monitoring experts, including consultation with BAAQMD technical staff. Because this initiative bridges research-grade monitoring with community-led emergency response, our QA framework will be explicitly designed to ensure the data is both scientifically defensible and accessible to our community partners.
Rather than a standard, static document, the QA framework will serve as a living operational guide for our multi-stakeholder partnership. The final plan will formalize the following key components:
  - **Data Quality Objectives (DQOs):** We will define strict quantitative goals for precision, bias, completeness, and sensitivity. This ensures that the data collected, whether from the stationary LoRaWAN nodes or the community-operated mobile platform, is reliable enough to accurately identify local sources, perform source apportionment, and support community health advocacy.
  - **Community-Accessible Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs):** The highly technical workflows outlined above will be translated into user-friendly SOPs. This includes visual, step-by-step checklists for community members executing field calibrations (e.g., zero/span checks on the O3, CO, NO2, and tVOC sensors), safely handling SKC pumps, and responding to localized odor events.
  - **Chain of Custody and Lab Integration:** The plan will document rigorous chain-of-custody protocols for handling physical filters (for EPA IO-3.5) and chemical cartridges (for EPA TO-17 and TO-11A) as they move from the community mobile unit to accredited laboratories.
  - **Data Verification and Algorithm Validation:** We will detail the automated and manual protocols used to flag, clean, and verify the continuous time-series data hosted on our AWS/InfluxDB infrastructure. This will specifically document how the calibration equations derived from our pre-deployment colocation experiments will be applied and validated over time.
By formalizing these protocols during Phase 1, we will ensure that the community is equipped with high-quality, actionable data that meets rigorous scientific standards while directly serving the community resilience goals of the Bay REPAIR program.


# Modeling Plan

### CFD Modeling Overview 
Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations have proven invaluable for analyzing the wind flow and dispersion of pollutants in urban environments (Pantusheca et al., 2022; Blocken et al., 2013; 2015; Tominaga, 2024; Tominaga & Stathopoulos, 2016; Di Sabatino et al., 2013; San Jose et al., 2021; Buccolieri et al., 2011) and wind flow over complex terrain (Blocken et al. 2015, Rodrigues et al, 2015, Zhao et al., 2021(a, b); Temel & Beeck, 2016; Alavi et al., 2024). 
#### **Multi-scale Modeling **
At the mesoscale, meteorological models capture large-scale atmospheric phenomena such as the urban heat island effect, regional wind systems, and scalar transport over broad areas (e.g., California). These models, including the Weather Research Forecast (WRF) model and the Model for Prediction Across Scales (MPAS), can provide boundary conditions for smaller-scale CFD simulations ( Rodrigues et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2021(a,b); Lucas et al., 2016). WRF is commonly run at horizontal resolutions of 1 km or coarser, with vertically varying resolution that can reach as fine as 25 meters near the surface, allowing it to capture the structure of the atmospheric boundary layer while maintaining computational efficiency at mesoscale.
Microscale CFD focuses on detailed flow dynamics around buildings, streets, and vegetation. This scale is critical for resolving local variations in wind speed, turbulence, and scalar concentrations driven by urban design, including street canyons and vegetation barriers. The spatial extent of microscale simulations is typically constrained by the desired resolution, often sub-meter, to accurately capture conditions at the pedestrian level, and by the significant computational resources required. As a result, if the goal is to cover a large urban area (e.g., West Oakland) at sub-meter resolution, the computational domain may need to be divided into separate, overlapping, uncoupled simulation tiles; and/or require more compute resources (e.g., CPUs or GPUs). While more computationally intensive than mesoscale or terrain-resolving models, microscale CFD is critical for applications requiring high spatial fidelity in complex urban environments. Mesoscale models or idealized atmospheric conditions can be used to define the surrounding (boundary) conditions driving the large-scale wind.  
Complex-terrain CFD modeling operates at an intermediate scale between mesoscale and microscale approaches. This scale is particularly relevant in regions with significant topographic variation, such as hillsides, valleys, or coastal gaps, where terrain-induced acceleration, flow separation, and channeling can strongly influence wind and scalar transport. These terrain-driven effects are typically unresolved in coarser mesoscale models. However, they can be captured by terrain-resolving CFD simulations conducted over broader extents (on the order of tens of kilometers) at resolutions ranging from tens to hundreds of meters. Near ground vertical resolution can range from meters to tens of meters.  
#### **Pollution Modeling and Sources **
Urban air quality is shaped by a combination of ground-level and elevated emission sources, especially in areas adjacent to major industrial facilities. Ground-level emissions, primarily from vehicle traffic, contribute substantially to near-surface pollutant concentrations. Elevated emissions, such as rooftop vents or refinery stacks, disperse differently and may lead to localized impacts downwind, depending on atmospheric stability and local wind conditions (Pantusheva et al., 2022; Chávez et al., 2012; Bijad et al., 2016; Guo et al., 2019; Toja-Silva et al., 2018; Bahadori, 2022). The inclusion, resolution, and representation of these sources depend on the modeling scale. At the mesoscale, models (e.g., WRF/MPAS) typically incorporate broad emission inventories and parameterized plume behavior to represent scalar transport. At this scale, however, the effects of complex terrain and urban morphology on near-surface pollutant concentrations remain unresolved. Complex-terrain CFD models operate at tens- to hundreds-of-meters resolution, resolving terrain-induced channeling, sheltering, and recirculation patterns that significantly influence plume pathways in hilly or coastal environments. At the finest scale, microscale CFD models resolve individual sources and near-field dispersion behavior at meter to sub-meter resolution, capturing pollutant concentration gradients at the pedestrian scale.
Emission inventories are used to characterize and calibrate pollution inputs across source types. In the Carquinez corridor, relevant sources include the Phillips 66 and Chevron refineries, marine terminals and vessel traffic along the strait, freight rail lines, and high-volume roadways including I-80 and I-680. For stack and elevated emissions, it is essential to define the exit velocity and temperature of the plume, as these parameters govern initial buoyancy and determine whether the plume rises, disperses laterally, or is entrained into building wakes or terrain recirculation zones. Pollutant dispersion is modeled as a passive scalar transport problem (Pantusheva et al., 2022; Steffens et al, 2012; Tong et al, 2015), meaning the pollutant is carried and dispersed by the airflow without affecting the flow itself, allowing concentration fields to be computed across complex terrain and urban environments without the computational cost of fully coupled reactive or thermally active transport models. Emission sources are defined as point, line, area, or volume sources depending on source type and data availability. Where detailed emission inventories are unavailable, normalized unit emission rates can be applied, allowing source contributions to be scaled post-simulation. Combined with corridor sensor network observations, this approach enables source-to-receptor attribution and, where sufficient monitoring data exists, inversion-based estimation of source emission strengths (Chen et al., 2024). 
It is important to distinguish between regional and local sources of air pollution when interpreting measured concentrations. Regional sources, such as wildfire smoke or emissions from distant highways and industrial zones, contribute substantially to background pollution levels, while local sources drive sharp exposure gradients at the neighborhood scale. 
### Study Area 
The region of interest (Richmond, Rodeo, Crockett, Vallejo, Benicia, Martinez) is located on the southern shoreline of the Carquinez Strait, where San Pablo Bay narrows into a channel connecting to the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta. The strait forms a natural gap between the rolling hills of Contra Costa County to the south and the ridges of Solano County to the north. To the west, San Pablo Bay opens toward the larger San Francisco Bay, while to the east, the strait transitions into the interior delta waterways. The surrounding terrain is varied: coastal hills rise immediately southeast of Rodeo, creating an elevated backdrop that separates the community from inland valleys. Across the strait to the north, lower ridgelines frame the opposite shoreline. The waterfront features a mix of industrial facilities, an open shoreline, and marina infrastructure, all situated directly adjacent to the narrowing water channel. San Pablo Bay’s broad waters lie just west of the community, with tidal flats and marshlands extending into the shoreline areas. This combination of open water, narrow channel geometry, and enclosing terrain establishes a complex physical setting. It defines how the bay, strait, and surrounding hills interact to shape the local environment, forming the geographic context for both regional-scale and microscale modeling efforts. Urban features, such as buildings, narrow streets, and tree canopies introduce additional small-scale disturbances to the wind field. These elements cause localized changes in speed and direction that are not captured in mesoscale models.

#### **Modeling Approach**
The modeling begins with a foundational phase focused on simulating wind flow to identify optimal sensor placements for monitoring wind and pollution. Modeling the wind pattern is essential, as it provides insight into local airflow dynamics, which are also critical for understanding pollution transport before advancing pollution modeling and monitoring. 
The wind modeling process relies on two primary inputs: (1) a domain model (or digital twin, a 3d representation of the study area including complex terrain, buildings, and other urban structures); and (2) meteorological (weather) data that define the surrounding large-scale wind conditions or numerical weather prediction data from models like WRF/MPAS. With these inputs, we define the computational domain and mesh that captures the key physical features at a desired resolution. Meteorological inputs are used to set boundary conditions, i.e. the atmospheric forcing over the simulation domain. 
The modeling phase begins by defining the appropriate scale of CFD simulation based on the community-led objectives and geographic context. Two scales of simulations are considered: (1) complex terrain simulations, which span broader areas (e.g., \~10 km radius), are used to resolve topographic influences such as hills, valleys, and coastal effects. (2) Urban-scale (microscale) simulations, which focus on smaller domains (e.g., \~1 km radius) at meter to sub-meter resolution, enable detailed analysis of flow interactions with buildings, vegetation, and streets. Each approach offers trade-offs between spatial resolution and computational cost. Complex terrain simulations provide a regional wind context but operate at coarser resolution. In contrast, microscale simulations capture localized flow behavior around the built environment but are limited in spatial extent due to their computational demands.
CFD can implement passive scalar transport modeling to study pollutant dispersion. This method employs high-resolution wind fields obtained from simulations that resolve terrain and urban structures. It tracks the spread of non-reactive scalars, like PM2.5, throughout the area. In complex terrains, this modeling is often used to analyze the dispersion of industrial plumes, roadway emissions in valleys, and releases in harbor or coastal areas, where topography significantly influences transport and accumulation. At the microscale, the approach examines how pollutants move around buildings, streets, vegetation, and other urban features. It captures phenomena such as recirculation in street canyons, localized stagnation points, and concentration gradients near the source of emissions. Overall, these applications provide a computationally efficient way to quantify concentration patterns while considering both the effects of terrain and urban environments on flow behavior.
The modeling scales (complex terrain and microscale) can be nested to leverage the strengths of each approach within a unified simulation framework. Mesoscale models (such as WRF/MPAS) provide time-varying boundary conditions that drive terrain-resolving CFD simulations. In turn, terrain-resolving CFD can supply refined inflow and boundary conditions to microscale CFD domains, capturing topographic acceleration and channeling effects that would otherwise be absent. This hierarchical nesting approach allows large-scale atmospheric dynamics to propagate down to the pedestrian level, enabling physically consistent predictions of wind, turbulence, and scalar transport across scales spanning hundreds of kilometers to sub-meter resolution.
#### **Simulation Domains and Modeling Cascade**
The modeling framework is organized as a three-tier nested simulation cascade, mesoscale to complex terrain to microscale, in which each scale provides boundary conditions to the next finer scale, ensuring that large-scale atmospheric dynamics propagate physically consistently down to the pedestrian level in each community area of interest. Rather than running continuous time-stepping CFD simulations, which would be computationally prohibitive at corridor scale, the framework uses a set of representative steady-state conditions selected to capture dominant flow regimes across seasons. Boundary condition approaches are described in the Proposed Modeling Framework section below. At least one representative day per season will be selected for simulation, with additional days added based on modeling findings and community priorities such as historical flaring events or periods of elevated health concern identified through the CSRC process.
The WRF innermost domain provides boundary conditions for the corridor-wide complex terrain CFD simulation, which spans approximately 25 km across the Carquinez Strait region covering Richmond, Rodeo, Crockett, Port Costa, Vallejo, Benicia, and Martinez. This domain resolves the topographic influences that are the defining features of this corridor: the Crockett Hills sheltering effect on Rodeo, the strait's channel geometry accelerating and directing flow, the coastal gap between San Pablo Bay and the inland delta, and the elevated industrial sources along the southern shoreline. The complex terrain domain operates at tens-of-meters resolution with a mesh refined near the ground surface, using LCZ-based roughness classification to represent the land-sea contrast and varied terrain cover across the region. At the finest tier, microscale CFD domains are nested within the complex terrain simulation. Each microscale domain covers approximately a 2 km radius and explicitly resolves urban-scale features: buildings, vegetation, traffic corridors, and industrial structures at sub-meter resolution. The number and location of microscale domains are determined through community engagement process: corridor communities identify priority areas based on health burden data, proximity to industrial sources, and community-defined concerns. The complex terrain CFD solution provides the boundary conditions for each microscale domain. Each microscale domain runs both existing conditions and community-selected intervention scenarios including urban greening, road diets, and emission reductions enabling direct comparison of projected exposure benefits against the validated baseline.
#### **Preliminary Wind Data Analysis **
To guide the selection of representative simulation periods, wind observations from available corridor weather stations and public mesoscale model outputs are analyzed to characterize seasonal and diurnal wind behavior. For each community, high-frequency wind records are sourced from nearby stations via the Synoptic network and compared against mesoscale model outputs to evaluate model performance and identify representative days in each season that closely match observed patterns. The analysis produces seasonal wind roses, diurnal wind-speed and direction time series, and station-to-model comparisons that, together, characterize the dominant flow regimes, directional persistence, and terrain-driven variability across the corridor. These outputs inform the selection of CFD simulation periods and boundary condition inputs, with at least one representative day per season identified per community, and additional days selected based on wind regime variability or community-identified priority events such as historical flaring episodes.
As an illustrative example, a wind data analysis was conducted for the Rodeo area using records from three nearby stations spanning 2021–2025. Despite sharing the same regional S–WSW corridor, local wind conditions varied substantially between stations, with mean speeds ranging from \~2.0 m/s at a sheltered inland location to \~4.8 m/s at an exposed hilltop site only a few kilometers away. Winter introduced intermittent easterly reversals during morning hours, representing conditions under which pollutant transport pathways shift toward communities that are otherwise upwind under prevailing flow. This station-to-station contrast is direct observational evidence that mesoscale models cannot resolve the terrain-driven flow structures governing near-surface pollutant transport in this corridor, and directly motivates the CFD framework proposed here. A comparable analysis will be conducted for each community in the study area, Crockett, Vallejo, Benicia, Port Costa, and Martinez, forming the meteorological foundation for the full corridor modeling cascade.
#### **Prior Modeling Efforts**
The BAAQMD Path to Clean Air technical assessment (2024) estimated a regional PM2.5 background of 6.0 µg/m³ in the PTCA area, with local sources contributing an additional 1.1 µg/m³, of which Chevron alone accounts for approximately 61% of local PM2.5 emissions. The assessment relied on three complementary models: CMAQ for regional background, CALPUFF at 100-m resolution for Chevron-specific plume transport, and AERMOD at a 50-m receptor grid for all remaining local sources, with all models driven by WRF-derived meteorological fields. Critically, the Air District noted that the corridor's complex topography, a ridge line running from Point San Pablo to Point Richmond, flat central lands, hills to the east, and a three-sided shoreline, precluded the use of a single meteorological observation set in AERMOD, requiring WRF-modeled fields as a substitute. This limitation is not unique to Richmond: in Martinez, BAAQMD similarly acknowledged that sea-breeze forcing and orographic effects render standard dispersion modeling inadequate, while in Benicia and Rodeo-Crockett, community-scale dispersion assessments equivalent to the PTCA plan remain absent altogether. Across the corridor, AERMOD's Gaussian framework which assumes steady-state plume behavior, a single representative meteorological location, and a 50-meter receptor resolution cannot resolve street-level concentration gradients, building wake effects, or terrain-channeled flows that drive neighborhood-scale exposure disparities. As a first step, this project will conduct a structured review of existing AERMOD-based assessments across the corridor to identify modeled outputs, emissions inventories, and meteorological inputs that can be carried forward. This review will directly inform the development of a CFD-based modeling framework designed to address the resolution and meteorological fidelity gaps that Gaussian approaches cannot bridge, building toward community-scale exposure characterization that reflects the actual complexity of the Carquinez environment.
Emission sources for the CFD simulations can include traffic corridors, refinery stacks, marine terminals, and other sources identified through the scoping review and community input process. To ensure consistency with prior regulatory analyses, we will align source characterization and emission rates with existing AERMOD-based assessments including refinery permitting studies and CEQA documentation such as the Phillips 66 Biofuel Conversion Project environmental review carrying forward established source locations and emission inventories rather than reconstructing them from scratch. This grounds the CFD framework in regulatory precedent while extending the analysis to the finer spatial resolutions and meteorological complexity that Gaussian approaches cannot resolve.
### Proposed Modeling Framework
The simulations are performed using OpenFOAM (Weller et al., 1998), an open-source computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solver based on the finite volume method. OpenFOAM’s flexibility enables users to create tailored models for complex fluid dynamics, heat transfer, and multiphase flow, while allowing custom integration of environmental sub-models for atmospheric, traffic, and pollution conditions. The wind flow equations consist of the unsteady Reynolds Averaged Navier-Stokes (URANS) with the k-epsilon turbulence model (Launder & Spalding, 1974; Versteeg & Malalasekera, 2007). In RANS turbulence is modeled rather than resolved in full detail, making RANS generally much cheaper computationally than LES (Large Eddy Simulation). Additional turbulence closures may also be considered for RANS, including the realizable k–ε model and the k–ω SST model. These equations consist of the conservation of fluid mass, conservation of momentum, and conservation of mass of each pollutant (e.g., PM2.5 concentration). Although neutral atmospheric conditions are commonly assumed for urban CFD studies (Pantusheca et al., 2022; Franke et al., 2011), buoyancy effects may be considered through surface heat flux boundary conditions. The flow is considered incompressible, and the Boussinesq approximation is applied for buoyancy effects.
Where computational resources permit, OpenFOAM's LES capability — using subgrid-scale models such as the dynamic Smagorinsky (Germano et al., 1991; Lilly, 1992) or the WALE model (Nicoud & Ducros, 1999) — can be employed to explicitly resolve energy-containing turbulent motions, providing higher fidelity representations of unsteady wake dynamics, concentration fluctuations, and stable boundary layer intermittency that RANS closures cannot capture (Tominaga & Stathopoulos, 2016; Blocken, 2018). LES requires physically realistic turbulent inflow boundary conditions to avoid unrealistic laminar fetch; common approaches include the recycling-rescaling method (Lund et al., 1998), synthetic eddy methods such as the Divergence-Free Synthetic Eddy Method (DFSEM) implemented natively in OpenFOAM (Poletto et al., 2013), and precursor ABL simulations — the latter used operationally in NREL's SOWFA framework for wind energy LES over complex terrain (Churchfield et al., 2012). However, LES typically requires computational meshes one to two orders of magnitude finer than RANS, time steps on the order of fractions of a second, and extended simulation periods to accumulate statistically converged flow fields, making it most appropriate for targeted high-priority receptor locations or sensitivity studies rather than domain-wide screening.
The wind field is solved using OpenFOAM's simpleFoam solver for steady-state cases, employing the SIMPLE algorithm (Van Doormaal & Raithby, 1984), or pimpleFoam for transient cases, employing the PIMPLE algorithm (Issa, 1986; Jasak, 1996), both handling incompressible turbulent flows. Second-order spatial discretization schemes are applied throughout, consistent with best practices for urban-scale simulations (Blocken et al., 2015). 
Complex terrain-resolving CFD simulations are conducted over broad extents (on the order of tens of kilometers, \~20 x 20 km) at resolutions ranging from hundreds to tens of meters. Near ground vertical resolution can range from meters to tens of meters.  The computational mesh is refined near ground to resolve topographic effects following practices outlined by Blocken et al. (2015) and Rodrigues et al. (2016). The complex terrain is split by roughness categories (based on land climate zones LCZ), defining land and sea surfaces by roughness lengths to improve the realism of the atmospheric boundary layer over the complex terrain. 
​​At the microscale, the model explicitly resolves urban-scale features, including buildings, trees, and traffic corridors, with sub-meter resolution. These features introduce localized flow disturbances such as downdrafts, deflection, and reduced downwind speeds that are difficult to predict without CFD modeling. Vegetation influences airflow by enhancing vertical mixing (i.e., lofting), generating windbreak effects, and inducing drag. Vegetation is modeled as a porous media (e.g., Darcy–Forchheimer) that is parameterized with vegetation-specific resistance data (Janhall, 2015; Bowker et al., 2007; Holzmann, 2021; Kastner & Dogan, 2022) Alternative vegetation models consist of momentum and turbulent source terms (Liu et al., 1996; Sogachev et al., 2006). The sources are defined in terms of leaf area density and drag coefficient. In addition, vehicle-induced turbulence (VIT) is included to represent near-road mixing caused by moving traffic (Hashad et al., 2022). The computational domain and mesh are established according to standard guidelines by Franke and Baklanov (2007), Blocken et al. (2013, 2015), and Kastner and Dogan (2020, 2022). OpenFOAM simulations will use customizable grids that adapt to complex geometries. 
Two strategies are considered for setting boundary conditions (surrounding large-scale wind conditions): ABL-based boundary conditions use idealized logarithmic wind profiles, such as the formulation proposed by Richards and Hoxey (1993), and are suitable when computational efficiency is prioritized. Mesoscale-derived boundary conditions incorporate spatially varying wind fields from mesoscale models like WRF to more accurately represent meteorological forcing (Rodrigues et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2021(a,b); Leng et al., 2022). These boundary condition approaches are not mutually exclusive nor tied to a specific simulation type. Depending on community guidance, technical feasibility, and data availability, either method may be applied to either the complex terrain or microscale simulations. This flexibility ensures that the final modeling approach aligns with local needs while maintaining robust scientific validity. 
For the ABL approach, wind speed and direction at 10 meters above ground are sourced from either monitoring observations or WRF outputs. WRF data is also used to evaluate seasonal and daily wind patterns and to identify representative conditions for simulation. The High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR) model, operated by NOAA, is a specialized configuration of the WRF model. It provides publicly available, hourly forecasts at a 3 km resolution and is widely used for short-term weather prediction and mesoscale modeling applications. It is particularly useful for urban wind modeling because it: updates hourly with recent observations; Captures wind variation through multiple layers of the atmosphere; Offers consistent data coverage across CA since 2014. 
While publicly available meteorological mesoscale datasets such as the High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR, 3 km), the North American Mesoscale model (NAM, 12 km), and ERA5 reanalysis (31 km) can provide initial and boundary conditions for a separate WRF, their spatial resolution is insufficient to capture the mesoscale flow features that drive wind dynamics in coastal, complex terrain environments. Running a dedicated nested WRF simulation, with domains progressively refined from tens of kilometers down to hundreds of meters allows these mesoscale flow features to develop at resolutions commensurate with the complex terrain CFD domain (\~20 × 20 km at tens-of-meters resolution), ensuring physically consistent time-varying boundary conditions at the CFD inflow interface (Haupt et al., 2019). This approach follows the methodology demonstrated by Rodrigues et al. (2016), Zhao et al. (2021a,b), and Chen et al. (2024), where WRF nested domains provided time-varying fields that drive CFD simulations over complex urban terrain. The innermost WRF domain should be run at horizontal resolution comparable to or finer than the complex terrain CFD domain, with vertical levels concentrated in the lowest 100 m of the boundary layer to resolve the surface layer structure that drives near-ground dynamics. At least one representative day per season will be selected for simulation, with additional days added based on modeling findings and community priorities such as historical flaring events or periods of elevated health concern identified through the CSRC process.
#### **Quantitative Assessment **
The project's novelty lies in its iterative approach to air monitoring and modeling, involving cycles of community engagement, data analysis, CFD simulation, validation, and stakeholder feedback. This process enables continuous refinement, improving data accuracy and model reliability with each iteration. By integrating feedback and new data, the project adapts to real-world conditions and community needs, creating a responsive framework for air quality management.
To meet quality objectives, all CFD models will undergo verification and validation (V\&V) in accordance with the NASA/GRC framework. Verification ensures the model is correctly built, confirmed through grid independence studies and iterative residual checks, while validation confirms it produces accurate, realistic results. Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) will also be applied to assess and propagate input uncertainties such as wind speed, emission rates, and boundary conditions, enhancing confidence in model predictions. CFD model outputs will be directly compared with data from both the fixed stationary sensor network and the mobile monitoring unit deployed across the corridor, providing distributed validation datasets for Rodeo, Crockett, Vallejo, Benicia, Port Costa, and Martinez spatially. A ZX300 continuous-wave wind lidar will provide vertical wind profile measurements to validate CFD boundary layer inputs. Model and data accuracy will be assessed using standard statistical metrics, including Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Bias Error (MBE), and correlation coefficients. Spatial and temporal data completeness will be tracked across the sensor network, with a focus on maintaining consistent coverage in key areas. Where available, CFD results will be benchmarked against regulatory-grade dispersion studies (e.g., AERMOD).
### Preliminary Modeling
CFD boundary conditions will be set primarily through one-way coupling with WRF, in which time-varying fields (velocity, temperature, and turbulence) extracted from the WRF innermost domain are prescribed at the CFD domain boundary. Where full WRF coupling is not yet available, an Atmospheric Boundary Layer (ABL) approach using idealized wind profiles derived from station observations provides an alternative, though WRF coupling remains the preferred method for physically consistent representation of terrain-driven flow in this corridor.

To illustrate the microscale CFD modeling capability proposed for this project, Figure below presents results from an ongoing EPA-funded air monitoring project in Marin City, California, a community with comparable complex terrain and a highway-adjacent setting. A high-resolution digital twin was constructed, incorporating terrain, buildings, vegetation, and traffic corridors, and microscale CFD simulations were run at sub-meter resolution for representative wind conditions. A key finding from this work is that, regardless of the prevailing regional wind direction, whether WSW or NE, near-surface winds at the community level are consistently experienced as arriving from the northeast. This counterintuitive result is driven by a recirculation zone that forms when WSW winds interact with the surrounding hills, generating a reversed near-ground flow that directs air back toward the community from the northeast. Under stronger WSW conditions, the recirculation zone intensifies and can extend outward to the adjacent highway corridor, effectively entraining roadway emissions and pushing them inward toward residential areas, the opposite direction one would predict from regional wind data alone. This finding precisely illustrates the terrain-driven, community-scale exposure dynamics that standard Gaussian dispersion models cannot capture and directly motivates the CFD-based framework proposed for the Carquinez corridor.

**Budget Guidelines for Partners — BAAQMD LCBF Round 1**
Bay REPAIR Local Community Benefits Fund (LCBF)  ·  Internal partner briefing  
**How the budget is structured**
Each co applicant gets their own tab in the Excel workbook. Every tab has the same parts:
  - Direct costs — everything tied to the project: staff time, equipment, travel, consultants
  - Indirect costs — your organization’s overhead: rent, utilities, shared admin. Capped at 15% of your direct costs
  - Contingency — reserve for unexpected costs within the approved project scope. Capped at 10% of your direct costs
**The three caps to know**
Indirect costs: 15% of direct costs, applied at the project level across all partners combined.
Contingency: 10% of direct costs, applied at the project level across all partners combined.
Professional Services & Consultants: 25% of total direct costs, applied at the application level. This is the most important cap for our team — technical advisors and academic consultants sit under this ceiling. Combined they cannot exceed 25% of total direct costs across the whole application.
**Co-Applicant vs. Consultant **
Co-Applicant: co-applicants must receive at least 25% of the total application budget combined.
Consultant (technical advisors, academic partners): provides expertise and guidance rather than direct implementation. Subject to the 25% cap total. Each consultant must be listed as a separate line item with a description of their scope and estimated cost.
**Practical limits per partner tab**
  - Indirect costs = one line item only
  - Contingency = one line item only
  - Equipment purchases over $5,000 must each be itemized on their own line
**Opportunity Grant requirements**
  - At least 25% of the total application budget must be allocated to co-applicants combined — Hyphae’s technical scope is expected to satisfy this requirement
  - Consultant fees must stay under 25% of total direct costs across the application — this is a hard limit if we have only one project
  - Project Summary tab auto-populates — do not edit it. Red cells mean a cap is exceeded and must be fixed before submitting. Yellow cells are advisory warnings to check at the application level
**How Community Partners Fit into Budget**
Community partners can participate in one of three ways depending on their role in the project:
**Co-Applicant** — If a community partner has a real implementation role (hosting monitoring equipment, leading outreach, coordinating community response, collecting data), they can be named as a co-applicant. Their budget share must be justified by their actual scope of work. Funding flows through OCE via a subaward agreement. There is no cap on their share, but all co-applicants combined must meet the 25% floor.
**Consultant** — If a community partner's role is more advisory (attending meetings, reviewing materials, providing community input or expertise), they fall under the consultant category. Remember: all consultants combined cannot exceed 25% of total direct costs across the application.
**Letter of Support (no budget)** — Some community partners don't need funding. If they are contributing in-kind — space, access, relationships, local knowledge — they can participate through a formal letter of support without appearing in the budget at all. This is common for schools, community groups, and local organizations.
**The key question for each community partner:** Are they doing scoped, billable work — or are they lending their name, network, and community trust? That answer determines where they land.
**Roles**
  - OCE — lead applicant, community/organizational lead
  - Hyphae — co-applicant, technical lead (monitoring, modeling, equipment, consultants)
  - RCA — consultant or LOS?

UV radiation is divided into 3 main groups:
UVA rays have the least energy of UV rays. These rays can cause skin cells to age and can cause some indirect damage to cells’ DNA. UVA rays are mainly linked to long-term skin damage such as wrinkles, but they are also thought to play a role in some skin cancers.
UVB rays have slightly more energy than UVA rays. They can damage the DNA in skin cells directly and are the main rays that cause sunburns. They are also thought to cause most skin cancers.
UVC rays have more energy than the other types of UV rays. Fortunately, they don’t reach the ground because they react with ozone high in our atmosphere. So, they are not normally a risk factor for skin cancer. But UVC rays can also come from some man-made sources, such as arc welding torches, mercury lamps, and UV sanitizing bulbs used to kill bacteria and other germs (such as in water, air, food, or on surfaces).

# CARB5 Rodeo 
[Rodeo\_Project\_Narrative (working).docx](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1XVtiFbXi9FrUR4Io-AlGCJlKl95ek9Fr/edit?usp=share_link&ouid=116157413706206850098&rtpof=true&sd=true)

## **Project Title: Carquinez Strait Air Coalition  **
## **Applicant & Partners: Rodeo Citizens Association, Contra Costa Resource Conservation District, Hyphae Design Laboratory**
## **Project Summary:**
The Rodeo Citizens Association (RCA) is spearheading a project to address long-standing air quality issues in the refinery corridor of the Bay Area by establishing the Carquinez Strait Air Coalition (RCAC). We propose to advance these community-driven environmental monitoring and regulatory goals through a phased approach, beginning in this CARB Cycle 5 grant, where we intend to convene a Research Council of local citizen scientists and EJ groups to consolidate existing community research and conduct a targeted wind study. Given Rodeo’s complex topography and variable wind patterns, the wind study is critical to guide the precise siting of future instruments, ensuring pollutant flow and exposure risks are accurately captured. Additionally, this grant funds the establishment of a PurpleAir Sensor Array, providing residents with accessible, real-time air quality data as a community engagement tool and a safeguard against potential regulatory inaction. These efforts lay the groundwork for implementing a more sophisticated sensor array in CARB Cycle 6 and subsequent funding rounds.
The RCA is supported by the Contra Costa Resource Conservation District (CCRCD), which offers essential administrative and project management capacity, and the Vallejo Community Air Monitoring Network (CAMN), which provides technical expertise. Hyphae Design Lab also serves as a technical partner, leading the wind study to ensure precise sensor deployment for a future Community Air Monitoring Plan (CAMP) while building RCA’s advocacy capacity with regulatory agencies, CARB, and other stakeholders. This approach replicates Hyphae’s success in helping other local EJ coalitions achieve AB 617 recognition, first in West Oakland, with ongoing efforts in Marin City.
Most importantly, our project has the backing of the Air District, which has entered a new stage of commitment to refinery emissions regulation. Our goal is to align our extensive citizen science research and advocacy portfolio with regulatory power, contributing to a new era of proactive environmental governance. By laying this technical and organizational groundwork, the RCAC aims to position Rodeo for future CAMP implementation and, ultimately, for Community Emissions Reduction Program (CERP) development under AB 617. This project moves the RCA from passive data collection to coalition-based advocacy, demonstrating that citizen-led efforts are both technically robust and essential for impactful environmental health interventions.

We write with genuine excitement and a spirit of shared purpose to invite your organization into one of the most consequential community-led environmental health and air quality efforts ever undertaken in the Bay Area refinery corridor.
Building on a recently awarded **$499,529 CARB Community Air Grants Cycle 5 award** for **wind modeling and air quality monitoring** in Rodeo, Crockett, and Vallejo, we are now proposing **Bay RepAir** — an expansion of that work across the entire Bay Area refinery and energy corridor, from Richmond to Antioch, with stronger tools, wider partnerships, and a community-science model that places residents firmly in charge of the data that shapes their lives and health.
|  |
| :- |
| ***The communities along this corridor — predominantly low-income and multiethnic — face much higher rates of asthma, cancer, cardiovascular disease, and adverse birth outcomes than California averages. They live in the shadow of heavy industry, yet remain among the least monitored communities in the region. Bay RepAir is designed to change that.*** |

 
|  |
| :- |
| **What Makes Bay RepAir Genuinely New** |

 
**Existing monitoring — refinery fenceline sensors, Air District stations, even existing PurpleAir PM2.5 networks — leaves critical and well-documented gaps.** Fenceline monitors sit below stack height, so plumes that rise above them and settle into neighborhoods go undetected. Regional networks are too sparse to reveal block-by-block differences in exposure. **And when flaring events or accidental releases occur, no independent community-controlled capacity currently exists to measure what was released and where it went.** Less is currently understood about emissions from newer renewable diesel refineries — another gap Bay Repair will help close.
**Bay Repair addresses all of these gaps at once,** through four integrated capabilities that no prior community-led project in this corridor has combined:
 
•     **Advanced wind and pollution dispersion modeling — **Hyphae Design Laboratory will use computational fluid dynamics (CFD) — the same engineering approach applied in aerospace and industrial design — to build three-dimensional digital models of how wind carries pollutants through the streets, terrain, and built environment of each corridor community, producing a detailed, neighborhood-by-neighborhood picture of who is breathing what, and why.
•     **Dense, community-sited air quality sensor network — **Numerous low-cost PM2.5 and multiple wind sensors, placed by residents through community engagement, will generate continuous, publicly accessible air quality data in neighborhoods that currently have none.
•     **Community Rapid Response Team with legal-grade sampling — **Trained community members will deploy portable multi-pollutant monitors, collect chain-of-custody air, soil, and particulate deposit samples and drones during flaring events and emergency releases — producing state-of-the-art technical data rigorous enough that regulatory agencies cannot dismiss it.
•     **Affordable toxic air contaminant (TAC) analysis —** Chemical “speciation” or identification will be available for reactive volatile organic compound (VOC) concentrations — making benzene, hydrogen sulfide, formaldehyde, ozone, and other harmful compounds measurable by and for the community, not only by industry and regulators. 
 
|  |
| :- |
| **Unmet Community Research Needs This Initiative Will Address** |

 
The pollutants most closely linked to health burdens in these communities — PM2.5, reactive VOCs, ultrafine particles, hydrogen sulfide, and black carbon — are either not monitored at all, not monitored at the neighborhood scale, or not monitored in real time. Communities cannot advocate for what they cannot measure. Bay Repair will deliver:
•     Baseline air quality data for cities such as Pittsburg, Antioch, Bay Point, and San Pablo, which  have effectively no independent monitoring today
•     The first corridor-wide account of how refinery and industrial emissions travel across the full length of the Carquinez Strait and San Pablo Bay under real wind conditions
•     Community-owned, open-access data usable in regulatory proceedings, health research, and legal contexts
•     A lasting foundation of trained local **citizen scientists** in every partner city — capacity that will endure well beyond the life of any single grant

•     **Letter of Commitment — **Active participation in the **Community Science Research Counci**l, local meetings, sensor siting, and shared advocacy.

Organizations that have **already committed** to the coalition include the Benicia Community Air Monitoring Project (BCAMP), Healthy Martinez, the Rodeo Citizens Association (RCA), the Contra Costa Resource Conservation District (CCRCD), the Vallejo Community Air Monitoring Network (CAMN), the Our Children’s Earth Foundation (OCE). Our core team — RCA, Vallejo CAMN, Hyphae, and OCE — is already delivering on CARB Cycle 5. Bay RepAir scales that trust and expertise corridor-wide.
We are actively and warmly seeking new partners in **Richmond, San Pablo, Bay Point, Pittsburg, Antioch,** and throughout Solano County.
For too long, refinery corridor communities have been studied and regulated from the outside, without the tools to speak for themselves in the language of data. Bay RepAir is an effort to change that relationship — humbly, collaboratively, and with the full weight of good science behind it.
