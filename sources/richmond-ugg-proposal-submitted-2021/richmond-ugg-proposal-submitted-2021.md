Answer: Project Background

Richmond is an environmental justice community, where residents are exposed to air pollutants from the state's largest oil refinery, two major highways, metals processing, and a commercial port among others. Richmond is also an under-treed community. Neighborhoods with the least tree coverage are those with the highest pollution exposure and are also the poorest, communities of color, and burdened with health and economic disparities. This means that Richmond residents are burdened with the impacts of industries and transportation routes that serve non-Richmond residents without the protective presence of an urban forest canopy.

Groundwork Richmond (GWR) is a community based organization in Richmond that focuses on addressing key environmental justice issues and breaking the cycle of poverty with effective employment training and youth engagement programs. GWR has a depth of experience in tree planting and maintenance (including the sole maintenance contract for all trees planted in the City), strong relationships with impacted communities, community organizations, and the City of Richmond, and a positive reputation for the work we do.

Project Summary This project expands Groundwork Richmond's tree planting and environmental justice work to the Richmond Parkway, a primary corridor that runs through the western part of the city, drawing a border between industries and neighborhoods including Atchison Village, Iron Triangle, and North Richmond, some of the most polluted and impacted neighborhoods of the City.

This project aims to: buffer residential areas from pollution; increase tree canopy in under planted neighborhoods; improve pedestrian and bike paths to facilitate non-vehicular traffic; reduce traffic speeds and create safer planted median buffers to prevent or reduce damage from potential traffic collisions; and engage youth in skill building and job training. Our design interventions focus on this corridor because buffering pollution is most effective as close to the source as possible and neighboring sensitive receptors: residential areas, outdoor transit zones (sidewalks and bike paths), and schools.

This project will focus on three different conditions along the Parkway and design site specific greening approaches. It therefore aims to be a preliminary showcase that can inform future expansion along the full

7 Discuss any demographic, social, and/or cultural issues that are important to the local community and that will

influence design, implementation, and maintenance of the project.

Answer: As historically 'black neighborhoods" the West side of town has long been characterized by the chronic

disinvestment that was designed into the landscape by racist housing policies dating back to the rebirth/reorganization of the city during World War Two. The recruitment of black war workers from the South helped to build the arsenal of democracy but challenged white's expectation for whites only communities leading to a series of discriminatory housing policies that hemmed in African American households into the census tracts adjacent to the project area. Accordingly these census tracts have suffered generations of disproportionate pollution burdens and have received a dearth of investment. That coupled with workplace discrinmination has resulted in diminished household incomes and political influence. The combination of misbegotten histories has resulted in a trickle down effect in a cash strapped city that often addresses the squeakiest or best connected wheel. Accordingly, the West Side neighborhoods have watched their city streets and sidewalks crumble and watched home owned be replaced by rentals units.
