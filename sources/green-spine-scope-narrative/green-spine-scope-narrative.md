**Green Spine**
**Health-Impact Driven Ecosystem Modeling **
**Introduction**
Texas Trees Foundation is spearheading the Green Spine corridor intervention in the South West Medical District (SWMD) of Dallas, Texas. The Harry Hines corridor between Market Center and Mockingbird is to be re-landscaped, potentially with modifications to traffic flow. The cloverleaf interchange between Inwood and Harry Hines is to be replaced with a park. The goal is to use evidence-based design to facilitate a health-improving intervention, and set up the area as a living laboratory for sustainable design. Hyphae has been engaged to model and monitor the district to support the design basis and facilitate before and after studies of the health impacts of the intervention.
 Thermal comfort was chosen as a primary endpoint for two reasons: 1) extreme heat is a pervasive problem in the district and 2) there is a plausible chance that we can measurably change the thermal comfort in the district using landscape interventions, in particular the planting of trees. Air pollution is another endpoint which appeals because there is significant traffic in the corridor, with pedestrian focussed infrastructure unbuffered from traffic-sourced pollution. Tree planting is also a potentially effective intervention for reducing air pollution exposure for users of the district, although effective monitoring for air pollution impacts of vegetation is more expensive than for thermal comfort. 
 We have initiated a three-phase plan for modeling and monitoring the microclimates in the district in concert with the intervention design process to enable the design to be evidence based, and to enact a before-and-after assessment of the intervention efficacy.
**Background**
 Throughout 2021 TTF organized a visioning collaborative that produced preliminary concepts for traffic changes and landscape improvements. During this phase we produced some small in-silico models of a few prototypical locations in the district to perform thermal comfort and air pollution modeling. A summary of the results of the preliminary thermal comfort modeling, and the air pollution modeling of selected sections from the visioning design plans can be found here: [Green Spine Simulation Results](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/14pa6ST4NVTzvjlFdc9XA70qCvX56Pv8PQl_aQWTMGUM/edit?usp=sharing).   While these models can be used to guide design decisions, modeling of specific design scenarios is necessary to optimize the best design. Furthermore, to ground-truth the models and establish a baseline for before-and-after efficacy analysis, it is necessary to start monitoring conditions in the district prior to the intervention taking place. Thus a 3 phase modeling and monitoring project was initiated.
 Phase 1 monitoring was envisioned as a pilot deployment of a central monitoring hub on the rooftop of Parkland Hospital, which could route LORA radio signals from 30 battery or solar powered sensor nodes to the cloud for analysis. The 30 sensors would be placed throughout the district, but confined to a pilot area between Butler and Medical District Drive. Phase 1 modeling was envisioned to develop the models started in the visioning phase for integration into further design processes. Phase 2 will expand the sensor network to 150 sensors across the whole corridor, with additional modeling of specific design scenarios. Phase 3 will involve continued monitoring, modeling and evaluation into the post-intervention and longitudinal domains. Phase 1 is nearing completion, and in preparation for phase 2 we are proposing the following design coordination scope.
**Design coordination**
In the first part of the design coordination period Hyphae is building upon baseline simulations started last year, taking advantage of new inputs as they become available, such as traffic counts and improved surveys. We will move forward on these while the collaboration is initialized with the new design team. Hyphae plans to run models for air quality based on traffic inputs, as well as potential changes to speed profiles or stop frequency. We will also study median variations for AQ impact more in detail following our preliminary results. 
We are looking forward to receiving materials from the engineers as well as any new documentation of existing conditions, including tree survey. The preferred traffic alignment is especially important for simulating corridor scenarios. As these studies are completed Hyphae will provide a report, as well as maps, presentations, with annotated insights. 
While we complete baseline existing conditions models and proposed lane and traffic updates, we are also developing a benchmarking simulation, for example the impact of a maximal planting scenario for evergreens vs deciduous and small vs large trees. These benchmarks become useful because the impact of detailed design scenarios can be compared to reference impact ranges.

We propose a choreographed collaboration schedule to avoid one team waiting for the other, exact sequence can be refined together.  But for example, Field Operations might start by prioritizing the likely first phase corridor design for which Hyphae has already generated models, while Hyphae completes baseline models of the extended corridor and park and incorporation of traffic data.
When certain design options and sections of the corridor are drafted we can begin modeling them while JCFO tackles other segments of the corridor or park for example. When their designs are ready for modeling we will have results for the first group of designs to pass back to them for iteration. 
We will repeat this hand-off procedure again and then recapitulate our goal-setting exercise for any useful changes made clear during the work to date. 
We will also provide interim recommendations based on the first course of simulations in each condition. These will be sketches and design recommendations as well as analytical exhibits. Additionally, we can jump in and run quick models when needed for example if JCFO is considering a few different solutions and want feedback.
We will revise our models based on iterations provided to us by the design team and refine our queries to target the particular dynamics interesting to the team. We will run models for second iterations on both corridor and intersection urban conditions, including the park area as needed. We will provide results in preliminary/raw form along with revised sketch recommendations for the designers to incorporate in their 30% design documents. These sketches might be scaled hand drawings, 3D massing models, illustrated sections, or any format which is most effective in communicating an intervention typology. 
Subsequent to the submittal of 30% design, in our experience, there are still many decisions to be made that can significantly affect the performance of the design. First value engineering from he 30% set requires redesign and strategic decision making. Additionally as projects get bid and built, many changes come for example in plant species and size availability which also affects the design and the performance.
Hyphae will make ourselves available for responding to Requests for Information with clarification, drawings, models, or additional simulation as requested. We will also review designs before each submittal for fidelity to the agreed performance objectives. 
Beyond this ongoing design assistance, Hyphae will be prepared to work on authorship of paper(s) for submission to academic fora on whatever topics we with the client deem promising.
We are eager to begin working with you and propose setting a time in the coming days to discuss some of the basic details of how we will share information and keep open communication.

The Hyphae Team

***“****Harry Hines Blvd. currently does little to contribute positively to public health or  promote healing within a major medical district. TTF is currently discussing and negotiating a scope of services to administer a research program to assess the preconstruction and post-construction conditions, and their effect upon environmental and public health. This will be achieved through a detailed assessment of changes in environmental exposures and biological markers in a study population that is directly impacted by the proposed Harry Hines Blvd. improvements.”*
*Quoted from the RFP*
We find Texas Tree Foundation's commitment to measurable, impact-driven health goals commendable and forward-thinking. This is why PWP asked us to be involved. We are a mission-driven firm committed to and at the frontier of researching, designing, modeling, advocating for and delivering health-driven urban greening projects. We have experience working with large, complex teams of academic health researchers in partnership with community groups, agencies, urban designers, ecologists, and engineers. We encourage you to review our project specific qualifications for further detail and references. 
Because we are experienced and passionate about this work, we have learned that achieving demonstrable environmental health impacts requires rigorous collaboration between all stakeholders ( designers, community groups and researchers) early and throughout. It also requires the environmental engineers to do significant modelling, optimization and validation early in the design process, to evaluate possible outcomes, predict potential dose responses and establish achievable research goals. 
We believe your impact driven goals warrant a new way of thinking about the urban landscape design process, one which is rooted in iterative high resolution environmental science-based modelling and ecosystem engineering. Therefore,* we propose health-based environmental modelling throughout the project as an integral part of the design process. This process contributes to refining landscape interventions for optimal outcomes while also validating design approaches.*
We do understand that much of this work was not explicitly included in the RFP, nor likely in predicted design budgets, but believe it is critical to the mission of the project and we seek to blur the line between the design team and the research team. We are excited to help the Texas Trees Foundation build out the Impact Research track of the project and we are committed to helping build ambitious local and national health research partnerships and raise additional grants to execute high impact environmental health studies.  
We have outlined an approach below to integrate health impact drivers into the fabric of the design and engineering process  and explain some of the elements we have included in the scope of work and why. We acknowledge however, that this is merely a sketch, and the process will require significant more collaboration with scientists and community members, and Texas Trees Foundation. While this explains some of our thinking, further presentations, discussions and time are included in the scope to help facilitate this process.
The first step of an impact driven project from the design team's perspective would be to work intensively with the Impact Team, the Equitable Engagement team, in house experts and previous consultants as well as the relevant advisory board specialists to define the potential impact and our goals. The elucidation of the desired health impacts must inform the design process, as some green infrastructure interventions will have a different set of potential benefits and design considerations compared to others. For example, reducing traffic-sourced air pollution for nearby at-risk populations requires dense evergreen plantings alongside major roadways, while leveraging the health benefits of visible greenery for hospital patients might require planting tall trees near hospital windows. The determination of which of these intervention strategies would be most effective will also depend on site-specific conditions. Thus a baseline site analysis will also inform both the determination of impact goals and the intervention design. While the project goal-setting process can begin with existing data and existing reports, it is generally best framed with additional environmental data and modelling to establish realistic benchmarks that are established based on predicted outcomes.
There is a spirited debate within the environmental exposure community as to the value of environmental modelling; The famous statistician George Box penned the even more famous quote *“Essentially, models are wrong, but some are useful.”  *We have developed numerous scientifically defensible and impact driven urban landscape performance projects and our answer has become resoundingly that both modelling and monitoring are needed. This is especially the case when stakeholders desire to advance the field of knowledge in addition to creating local impacts. Particularly in the context of  urban infrastructure, which is both slow and costly to implement, the ability to vet scenarios through models allows one to validate and choose effective design solutions more rapidly and reliably. 
While it is possible to monitor before and after conditions without modeling the design solutions first, we would argue that it is more effective and risk-mitigating to actively engineer the landscape to perform the ecological functions that stakeholders set as their target goals. For example, existing modeling efforts have shown that while certain configurations of roadside tree plantings can dramatically reduce pollution downwind, other configurations of trees can make air pollution downwind worse, indicating a need for site-specific intervention modeling during the design process. Especially with relatively high vehicle mile per day predictions, vegetated buffer conditions could be critical to exposure mitigation especially near sensitive receptors and adjacent pedestrians
We propose a two stage environmental modelling process as part of the design, where we build lower resolution models of the entire corridor as well as higher resolution models of proposed greening interventions. Environmental modeling can include fluid dynamic simulations of the distribution of temperature, humidity, human comfort, wind flows, particulate matter, NOx, Ozone, BVOCs and both building facade and ecosystem impacts.
 
We would first build 5-meter resolution models at the full two mile corridor scale. This will provide broad insights that allow us to break the corridor into similar conditions and also isolate unique hotspots. This will also provide a baseline from which to do benchmarking. Do we want to meet the average comfort index along the corridor, or do we want to reduce the extremes that are felt? These benchmarking goals will be developed in partnership with the stakeholders and, hopefully, through collaboration with the Equitable Engagement team. The modeling will provide a broad-strokes reference-point to frame, at an order of magnitude, what is possible to shift—environmentally—along the corridor. 
During the second design phase, we would produce higher resolution models of specific conditions and more detailed intersection and road profiles, then evaluate various green infrastructure design conditions and configurations for environmental performance.  This requires building sub-meter resolution models to study and test different streetscape and landscape configurations at typical conditions along the corridor as well as extreme or unique conditions, such as street canyons with taller buildings, high traffic intersections and off-ramps, pedestrian heavy intersections. *We propose to evaluate variables including plant species, spacing, growth rate, and 3D structure to directly inform final designs of street sections and landscape plans, as well as to predict ecosystem services against which to measure and model change.*
**Impact Research Ideas**
We are very interested in collaborating on long term research, whether minimal or extensive.
We have been involved in numerous rigorous studies and would propose the goal of developing a randomized clinical trial that could serve as the backbone for a number of research inquiries and partnerships. We have a number of long term research partners across the country who focus on health and urban greening and air quality. In the first phase of the project, we propose a summit to bring five experts to Dallas for a health summit with other local researchers. We expect Texas Trees to lead the stakeholder development but we have also begun to investigate potential local research and have done some initial research into the local hospital community research structure. For example, the Office of Clinical Research Facilitation (OCRF) at UT Southwestern is responsible for all clinical trials linked to UT Southwestern, Children's Hospital of Dallas and Parkland Memorial Hospital and would seem to be a great early collaborator.  Below we start to outline some interesting environmental and human subject studies that could emerge:
  - Changes in MOT (Mode of Transit)
  - Change in life expectancies, hospital recovery times
  - Change in VOC exposures and health impacts
  - Greenview Study from hospital rooms
  - Greenview Study from various MOTs (walking, driving, bus, etc.) 
  - Traffic & Pedestrian interactions, road speeds, street crossings
  - Psychosocial Studies
  - Human Comfort/Heat Island
  - Nature Prescriptions and Walking Studies
**Project Specific Firm Background**
Hyphae is an ecological innovation firm with a team of biologists, environmental engineers, geospatial data analysts, landscape architects and urban planners that apply multidisciplinary systems-thinking to challenging problems. Our work is split between project consulting and government and philanthropic funded research and development dedicated to identifying environmental determinants of health within the built environment and inventing solutions. Being born in a community in Oakland plagued with environmental injustice, our work is fueled by a very personal, mission-driven passion to help reverse those impacts. We have developed and managed teams with some of the nation's leading medical and environmental health exposure researchers and have received funding from the National Institute of Health (NIH), the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) and National Science Foundation (NSF) programs to advance the design, engineering, modeling and monitoring of near road green infrastructure. We are happy to provide our network of collaborators both as a reference to our work and as potential advisors where it may fit.  
See our associated projects for more detail. We also included an article published last week about our health driven research in Louisville Kentucky, as well as example environmental modelling outputs.
