**Willow Pass Road Green Infrastructure Bus Stop Improvements **
**Project Description**
This project proposes integrated green infrastructure (GI) and active transportation improvements at bus stops along Willow Pass Road in Bay Point, California, a community designated as a disadvantaged community under SB 535. Willow Pass Road serves as one of Bay Point's primary thoroughfares, connecting residents to essential services, employment, and commercial centers. The project seeks to address compounding environmental and public health burdens by transforming bus stops into resilient, multi-benefit green infrastructure hubs that improve air quality, mitigate heat, manage stormwater, and enhance safety for transit riders, pedestrians, and cyclists.
**Community Need and Climate Vulnerability**
Bay Point faces severe and well-documented climate vulnerabilities. The Contra Costa Health Services report *Climate Change Vulnerability in Contra Costa County: A Focus on Heat* (2015) identifies Bay Point as a community with high vulnerability to extreme heat, compounded by elevated indicators for poverty, linguistic isolation, childhood populations (under age 5), asthma rates, and a lack of urban tree canopy (page 32). Using Scripps Institute of Oceanography projections from Cal-Adapt.org and the GFDL climate model, Bay Point is projected to experience 21–35 days over 100°F by the 2090s (page 13)
Current climate vulnerability data and the Climate Vulnerability Map reinforces this urgency: the project area carries a **HIGH future vulnerability rating for extreme heat**, with 15.6% of days projected to exceed 100°F by 2050. The area also exhibits high vulnerability to PM 2.5 pollution and is classified as an **urban heat island**. The California Heat Assessment Tool rates the project area with a Heat Health Events score of 3.55 exceeds the 75th percentile, placing it in the **HIGH** category, while its Heat Health Action Index score of 45.41 reflects a **MODERATE** level of existing adaptive capacity, underscoring the need for targeted interventions.
Bus stops, as fixed points where community members wait in direct sun and traffic, represent some of the most acute exposure sites for these compounding hazards. Research confirms that air pollution at bus stops can be up to 3.5 times higher than in surrounding areas, with ultrafine particles, far smaller than PM 2.5, peaking within 100 feet of intersection centers due to vehicle stops and accelerations. Surface temperatures at conventional bus shelters routinely exceed 119–127°F on hot pavement, while adjacent vegetation and shade can reduce experienced temperatures to the mid-70s°F.
**Bus Stop Approach**
*Active Transportation Integration* Bus stop improvements will be designed to support the full active transportation journey, not just the wait. Bulb-out designs will calm traffic, shorten pedestrian crossing distances, and provide protected space for cyclists transitioning between bike lanes and transit. Where feasible, protected bike lanes and pedestrian lanes will be evaluated to reduce conflicts between modes. Bus stop placement will be reviewed for opportunities to relocate stops past intersections, demonstrating that this simple repositioning can substantially reduce transit riders' exposure to ultrafine particles generated by vehicle idling and acceleration.
*Green Infrastructure for Air Quality and Stormwater* Each bus stop will incorporate bioretention cells and planted buffers sized to capture and filter stormwater runoff from the adjacent roadway. Willow Pass Road carries significant vehicle traffic, and roadway runoff is known to carry brake wear particulates, heavy metals (including copper, zinc, and cadmium), and tire rubber compounds, pollutants that bioretention facilities are effective at capturing before they enter the storm drain system. The bus stop near Mountain View Ave. presents a particular opportunity to manage stormwater at a sensitive location, providing both water quality and flood risk co-benefits.
Planted buffers between the back of bus stops and adjacent residential neighborhoods will provide a green screen that reduces direct particulate and noise exposure for residents living immediately behind the roadway corridor, a meaningful co-benefit for a community with elevated asthma rates.
*Green Roofs and Tree Canopy for Heat Mitigation* Bus shelter designs will explore the integration of green roofs, vegetated shelter canopies that reduce radiant heat, extend the cooling effect of the shelter microclimate, and contribute to stormwater capture. Trees will be planted at or near each stop to provide canopy shade, reduce the urban heat island effect, and create the psychological benefit of a more comfortable waiting environment. Research from the Twin Cities found that tree canopy at bus stops made a 10-minute wait feel like only 7 minutes, a meaningful rider experience improvement that can support transit ridership. Tree and plant species selection will prioritize native and drought-tolerant species appropriate to Bay Point's climate, with consideration for long-term maintenance.
*Environmental Monitoring* Air quality, temperature, and radiant heat <comment_start id=kix.w39oxhetps01>sensors<comment_end id=kix.w39oxhetps01> will be installed at bus stops to create a real-time environmental monitoring network along the corridor. Sensor data will provide baseline and ongoing documentation of conditions at each stop, support evaluation of GI effectiveness over time, and contribute to community-level understanding of localized heat and air quality exposure. This data can inform future planning, support grant reporting, and provide an early warning resource for heat health events.
**Site Opportunities**
Six bus stop locations along Willow Pass Road have been identified as priority opportunities, at Goble Drive, Enes Avenue, Ambrose Avenue, Henkel Corporation, Mountain View Avenue, and Chevron, each with varying right-of-way conditions, setbacks, and adjacent land uses that present distinct design possibilities. The Enes Avenue location includes a private parcel opportunity for a bioretention facility covering over 86,000 square feet of drainage area, with co-located bus stop improvements and potential for a community-oriented parklet space. The Clearland Drive location offers potential connection to the Delta de Anza Regional Trail, linking transit access to regional active transportation infrastructure.
By integrating green infrastructure, active transportation safety, urban forestry, stormwater management, and environmental monitoring into bus stop improvements along Willow Pass Road, this project addresses Bay Point's most pressing climate vulnerabilities at the very places where residents are most directly and daily exposed to them. It transforms what are currently inadequate, hazardous waiting environments into resilient community assets — providing cleaner air, cooler temperatures, safer streets, and measurable environmental benefits for one of Contra Costa County's most climate-vulnerable communities.

Project Description
Willow Pass Road is the primary thoroughfare in Bay Point, California, an SB 535-designated disadvantaged community, connecting residents to essential services, employment, and commercial centers. This project transforms underserved bus stops along the corridor into resilient, multi-benefit green infrastructure hubs addressing Bay Point's most urgent environmental health challenges.
Bay Point is among Contra Costa County's most climate-vulnerable communities. The county's own *Climate Change Vulnerability in Contra Costa County: A Focus on Heat* (2015) report identifies Bay Point as highly vulnerable to extreme heat, with elevated rates of poverty, linguistic isolation, asthma, and scarce tree canopy. The community is projected to experience 21–35 days over 100°F by the 2090s (Scripps Institute of Oceanography/Cal-Adapt), carries a HIGH future heat vulnerability rating with 15.6% of days exceeding 100°F by 2050, and has a Heat Health Events score of 3.55, above the 75th percentile. Bus stops are among the most acute exposure points: air pollution at stops can be 3.5 times higher than surrounding areas, and exposed pavement surface temperatures regularly exceed 119°F.
Proposed improvements at <comment_start id=kix.klq9s19xrobq>six priority locations<comment_end id=kix.klq9s19xrobq>, Goble Drive, Enes Avenue, Ambrose Avenue, Henkel Corporation, Mountain View Avenue, and Chevron, include bioretention cells to capture roadway runoff laden with brake wear particulates and heavy metals, protecting Lawlor Creek and the broader storm drain system. Green roofs and tree canopy at shelters will reduce radiant heat and urban heat island conditions. Planted buffers between stops and adjacent neighborhoods will shield residents from traffic-related particulate exposure. Bus stop placement will be evaluated for relocation past intersections to reduce rider exposure to ultrafine particles. Active transportation improvements including bulb-outs and protected bike and pedestrian lanes will support safe access. Air quality, temperature, and radiant heat sensors at each stop will provide real-time environmental monitoring, document project effectiveness, and support community heat health preparedness.
By integrating stormwater management, air quality improvement, heat mitigation, and active transportation safety into Bay Point's bus stop infrastructure, this project delivers measurable, co-located benefits where residents need them most.
Describe any other benefits the proposed project is anticipated to provide that are not specifically included in question 12 above (e.g., increased recreational opportunities, improved health and/or safety, etc.) 
The bioretention facilities and green infrastructure improvements will reduce stormwater runoff and flood risk during major rain events, improving flood management for the Willow Creek watershed. Where opportunities exist along the corridor, the project will support enhancement native vegetation. Tree planting funded through this grant will provide critical shade and cooling infrastructure for a community projected to face severe extreme heat events, while also creating vegetated buffers that filter air pollution from Willow Pass Road, protecting not only adjacent residents but also pedestrians and cyclists traveling the corridor daily.
Importantly, project implementation will be carried out in partnership with Contra Costa County and through the Ecostewards program, ensuring that green infrastructure installation, tree planting, and ongoing maintenance are performed by local community members. The Ecostewards program will provide local jobs, workforce training, and educational opportunities tied directly to the project, building long-term community capacity to steward and maintain the improvements. This approach ensures that Bay Point residents are not just beneficiaries of the project, but active participants in its implementation and long-term success.
Explain the need for the proposed project. If applicable, explain how your project will provide “meaningful and direct” benefits to a disadvantaged community, a severely disadvantaged community, or a vulnerable population (as defined in Appendix D and described on pages 3-4 of the Grant Guidelines). 
Bay Point is a designated disadvantaged community under SB 535 and one of Contra Costa County's most environmentally burdened and climate-vulnerable neighborhoods. Willow Pass Road, the community's primary thoroughfare, exposes residents, transit riders, pedestrians, and cyclists daily to elevated air pollution, extreme heat, and flood risk, with bus stops serving as some of the most acute unprotected exposure points along the corridor. Air pollution at bus stops can be 3.5 times higher than surrounding areas, pavement surface temperatures exceed 119°F, and the community faces a projected 21–35 days over 100°F annually by the 2090s. Bay Point's Heat Health Events score of 3.55 exceeds the 75th percentile, and the area carries HIGH future vulnerability ratings for both extreme heat and PM 2.5 exposure. These burdens fall disproportionately on a population already facing elevated rates of poverty, linguistic isolation, childhood asthma, and limited tree canopy. This project directly addresses these compounding vulnerabilities by delivering green infrastructure, tree canopy, stormwater management, and active transportation safety improvements at the precise locations where disadvantaged community members wait for buses, walk, and bike every day. Benefits are meaningful and direct: cooler, cleaner, safer bus stops reduce heat and pollution exposure for transit-dependent residents; bioretention facilities protect homes and Willow Creek from flooding; and local implementation through the Ecostewards workforce program ensures that jobs, training, and long-term stewardship remain within the community.
