**Project: Ambrose Center Park Green Infrastructure Enhancements Phase 2**
**Proposal Narrative (subject to the 15-page limit) includes the following items:** 
  - **Abstract**- Summarize (approximately 200 words) your project’s environmental significance, major activities, <comment_start id=kix.1tb5r22jt9ql>timeframes<comment_end id=kix.1tb5r22jt9ql>, and <comment_start id=kix.r588abmsqt62><comment_start id=kix.tfvvefp9xhuk>measurable outcomes<comment_end id=kix.r588abmsqt62><comment_end id=kix.tfvvefp9xhuk>.
Ambrose Center Park and the surrounding neighborhoods within Bay Point, Contra Costa County, are economically disadvantaged communities with several hazard waste and cleanup sites, major highways, and a nearby power plant that exposes the community to high levels of pollution. Through community based, environmental health and justice driven green infrastructure (GI) solutions, the Ambrose Center Park Phase 2 Project (July 2024 - June 2028) will improve water quality through the establishment of a stormwater quality monitoring program and mitigate flooding/climate change issues while also providing stewardship/educational opportunities. The project will build off the improvements from Phase 1 of the IRWM grant by accounting for increased construction costs to ensure the successful completion of the project. The project comes out of public meetings about GI opportunities held with stakeholders to better understand community needs and potential partnerships, and to identify Ambrose Center Park as the site location. 
The project’s primary outcomes are the development of success criteria and metrics related to water quality issues to measure a decrease in pollution to the nearby community while also mitigating flood risks. Then, we expect to measure significant increases, via a pre- and post- survey, in the environmental skills and pathways towards stewardship in the workforce team. 
  - **Water quality improvement and/or wetlands restoration **
The Contra Costa Resource Conservation District (CCRCD) will develop and monitor GI interventions before, during and after implementation. In addition CCRCD will work with County officials and the Clean Watershed Program on best management practices for monitoring and submit a Project Monitoring Plan. Nutrient and pollutant loads including N, P, TSS, and E. Coli will be tested before and after the interventions to meet county and state standards. 
The San Francisco Bay Shoreline Adaptation Atlas identifies more than two dozen adaptation measures that are potentially appropriate to the Bay Point Operational Landscape Unit (SFEI and SPUR 2019). Although the majority of Bay Point Operational Landscape Units (OLU) shoreline bayward of the railroad is open space, very little is publicly owned and protected (SFEI 2022). While Bay Point's shoreline is dominated by industrial, commercial, and military uses, there are some small areas suitable for tidal marsh restoration and ecotone levees along the back edge of existing tidal marshes. An analysis found 3.8 miles of additional shoreline suitable for ecotone levees to protect rail lines, Superfund sites, and other shoreline developments from future flooding. In the upper watershed, green stormwater infrastructure could be used to reduce fluvial flooding in developed areas (SFEI 2022). The proposed bioretention facilities at Ambrose Center Park will function as auxiliary flood mitigation to the adjacent muted tidal wetlands and will thus be connected to the tides. However, to prevent backflows during periods of amplified tides, a water control structure such as a flap gate will likely be required.
Moreover, from the Bay Point Stormwater Infrastructure Assessment (<https://thewatershedproject.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Stormwater-Infrastructure-Assessment-Bay-Point.pdf>), the inundation maps produced from the hydrologic and hydraulic modeling indicated that during a 2-year design storm, flood depths greater than 0.1 feet is common throughout Bay Point. We can expect that similar flooding conditions exist at Ambrose Center Park, Willow Pass Road, and the adjacent DAC neighborhood. According to the Assessment, the lack of stormwater infrastructure in Bay Point leads to runoff issues for the community, with long flow paths and high flow rates exacerbated by discontinuous curb and gutter and low lot and first floor elevations. The proximity to Delta wetlands and the area's topography presents drainage challenges such as low slopes in channels and pipes that inhibit gravity drainage and the potential for high tide levels. The sporadic growth of the area without modern engineering and development standards has resulted in little to no drainage infrastructure, with key components often not publicly owned or controlled. These circumstances have resulted in localized drainage and flooding issues at a number of locations within the community. Reports of such issues are regularly cataloged and responded to by Public Works maintenance staff as part of ongoing efforts to upgrade and maintain public facilities. The Assessment recommends implementing LID/green infrastructure approaches including measures such as rain gardens, bioretention swales, pervious pavement applications, and trash capture infrastructure, particularly in areas such as Ambrose Center Park and the DAC community, to mitigate drainage issues and address the following objectives of the EPA Strategic Plan:
Objective 1.2: Accelerate Resilience and Adaptation to Climate Change Impacts
Objective 2.1: Promote Environmental Justice and Civil Rights at the Federal, Tribal, State, and Local Levels 
Objective 5.2: Protect and Restore Waterbodies and Watersheds
By developing the next generation of environmental workers, our project will satisfy the following objective of the EPA Strategic Plan: 
Objective 7.2: Promote Pollution Prevention
By developing a data driven Stormwater Quality Monitoring Program, our project will satisfy the following objective of the EPA Strategic Plan: 
Cross-Agency Strategy 1: Ensure Scientific Integrity and Science-Based Decision Making
  - **Project activities**
The main activity for this phase of the larger implementation project is to complement, expand, and monitor the constructed green infrastructure elements with the human capital and skills that will be needed to sustain this project, and through the development of a green skilled workforce, the potential for newly trained environmental workers to obtain new climate-related jobs and career pathways. By partnering with other local community-based organizations, the team will work together to pilot a stewardship program to maintain, monitor, and evaluate implemented green infrastructure, including local resident job training and hiring. We will develop a curriculum, outreach to the local community, and hire folks interested in learning how to maintain and monitor green infrastructure projects. With the increasing number of green infrastructure projects, this will allow the new hires experience in a growing field and potentially a competitive advantage in other similar jobs. We intend to create that next generation of environmental field workers.
The Stormwater Quality Monitoring Program will establish and implement a comprehensive plan over a four-year period, led by a team of two half-time employees and two full-time employees to ensure effective monitoring, data collection, analysis, and reporting. During the first year, monitoring protocols and QA/QC procedures will be developed in compliance with EPA regulations, monitoring sites will be established, and necessary equipment will be installed in addition to training team members on data collection and safety protocols. The training will include how to anticipate a measurable storm event, how to collect and document the collection of stormwater samples, how to perform and document visual assessments, how to handle and send the samples to the lab, how to interpret the results, and how to keep accurate and complete records and report appropriate information to the permitting authority.
We will be monitoring for TSS, NO3-N, NH3-N, total Kjeldahl nitrogen (TKN), and TP, metals (Copper, Lead, Zinc, Magnesium), selected organics (PAHs, Phthalates, Pesticides), pH, and fecal coliform while meeting the requirements in the NPDES and MS4 permitting for the County standards. During the second year we will start conducting regular monitoring and data collection as per established protocols, implement data quality checks and validation processes, and begin basic data analysis to identify trends and anomalies. During the third year we will enhance the data analysis techniques; explore correlations and potential sources of pollution, prepare quarterly reports summarizing findings and trends, and evaluate program effectiveness and suggest modifications. During the fourth year we will perform in-depth analysis, including predictive modeling for future scenarios, compile a comprehensive final report covering the entire monitoring period, and conduct a program review and identify lessons learned and best practices.
The CCRCD, using their field crew to help train and work alongside the new workforce, will develop a stewardship job training model that outreaches and works with other local community organizations such as the Bay Point Garden Club and Bay Point All N One. Along with these and other community partnerships, CCRCD will develop a long-term, community-based stewardship program for Ambrose Center Park that include watering, plant and tree maintenance, trash clean up, and water quality/environmental monitoring and evaluation.
To further encourage community buy-in and instill a sense of stewardship over the newly installed green infrastructure elements, the CCRCD team will plan and implement a series of volunteer community workdays throughout the project period. We will outreach to the local community to participate in service-learning style workdays so community members can better understand the need and value of these types of projects. By encouraging community members to reconnect with their local watershed, and perhaps get their hands dirty planting a native start, we envision that community members will be more inclined to take care of their watersheds and make positive environmental choices everyday. This will also be a way to introduce new individuals to the Ambrose Community Center and the public services it provides. We intend to hold a workday at least every quarter. 
To ensure we are inclusive of the community’s plans and visions for its own town, we will incorporate into the planning process several public input meetings. At these meetings, the CCRCD will provide context about the project and how it fits, and benefits, their everyday life today and in building resilience for the impending effects of climate change. These local meetings will ask community members on the individual and family level about their aspirations and how they would like the space to look and thus best serve the community functionally. The design team will then intertwine this community data within the possibilities of green infrastructure techniques, workforce development, and ongoing monitoring and maintenance of the site. To close out this activity, the design team will also make sure to follow up with community members to share how the public’s input was incorporated into the project and ensure the design team “heard” the community correctly.  
This current proposal to the EPA for funding will enhance and expand on the funding already confirmed (and will be used as this project’s match) from the Bay Area IRWM program with funding through the Department of Water Resources. The main green infrastructure components were developed from the Prop 1 DACTIP needs assessment, also planned and executed by the CCRCD. During the needs assessment process, CCRCD worked primarily with Hyphae Design Lab, an environmental engineering firm, to plan and implement community meetings to share potential green infrastructure projects and gather the opinions and vision from citizens. Hyphae also assisted in researching and mapping potential GI sites throughout Bay Point and neighboring communities (which we intend to implement with future funding opportunities). 
The needs assessment identified Ambrose Center Park in Bay Point as a recommended next step and location for constructing GI projects. The top water issue identified by Bay Point was water’s interconnected trash and litter problem. Our green infrastructure elements will be designed to help capture litter, which will then be cleaned out and maintained regularly by CCRCD field staff and workforce development participants. Our Project will also increase access to the site by the extension of an existing trail using permeable pavers. This trail interconnectivity will enhance the site’s use as a thoroughfare by connecting the main street in Bay Point, WIllow Pass Road, with the nearby Delta DeAnza Trail. 
Our Project will also benefit Bay Point citizens through tree plantings to provide more shade during extreme heat events. Then, as highlighted by the Bay Point community in the DACTIP needs assessment, our Project will increase access to parks and trails as well as increasing connectivity of thoroughfares for pedestrians and bikers.  
Our Project will create lasting impact for Bay Point in both the short and long terms. Several GI techniques will help alleviate concerns around flooding, increase water quality, and provide native habitat for wildlife while conserving water. 
  - **Climate Change Resiliency**
While Ambrose Center Park does have existing storm drains on site, during average yearly rainfall events there are multiple locations shown in the below map (in aqua) that experience flooding and ponding issues that limit access and use of the facilities and open space by the surrounding communities. An existing ditch on the west side of the site also provides some additional drainage capacity but also has flooding problems as it is undersized and receives flows from the majority of the site.

A recent study conducted by Point Blue Conservation Science and the USGS Pacific Coastal and Marine Science Center has mapped the projected impacts of sea level rise (SLR) on water table levels along the California coast. Results can be viewed in the Our Coast Our Future Webmap (https://ourcoastourfuture.org/hazard-map/). Water table levels near the shoreline can be heavily affected by SLR, and new adaptation projects should consider this when designing for rising water table levels. While the railroad alignment may offer protection from Bay-Delta flooding, it can also act as a dam to stormwater flows, causing floodwaters to back up during high flows. The study area landward of the railroad is less vulnerable to SLR, except for low-lying areas along creek channels and areas likely to flood as water table levels increase with rising sea level.

The Bay Point OLU habitat preservation and restoration priorities are outlined in the SFEI Baylands Ecosystem Habitat Goals Science Update, which includes the acquisition and protection of open space bayward of flood-protection levees. The goal is to create areas for existing marshes to migrate upslope in response to SLR, and to implement ecotone levees, living shorelines, or other green infrastructure (SFEI 2022).  
     
There are various sources of flooding vulnerability in the site area, including storm surge, king tides, inadequate stormwater infrastructure, and SLR. The railway shown in the photo below currently runs through the middle of marshland just north of the site, constructed at the upland edge of the historic marsh boundary. “The image exemplifies drainage issues at the intersection between the tidal wetland to the north of the railroad tracks and the populated lowland to the south of the railroad tracks and two embankments which act as levees <comment_start id=kix.svm4b1s7pd9j>(Balance 2017, Figure 2-10)<comment_end id=kix.svm4b1s7pd9j>. These modifications to the natural environment in the form of embankments, built decades ago to support railway infrastructure, act as a significant barrier to the movement of runoff from the lowlands into the wetlands and out to the Delta” (Balance 2017). Railway embankments have been obstructing the natural flow of Willow Creek tributaries for decades. While embankment fill placement was necessary to prevent flooding and land subsidence at the Delta margins, inadequate conveyance capacity and lack of maintenance have caused issues. These issues include insufficient conveyance capacity, maintenance issues, backwater flooding, prolonged ponding, and trash and coarse debris accumulation. The damaged culverts have caused much of the trash load to be deposited before reaching the wetlands and Delta to the north, which is better than conveyance of trash into the wetlands, but still far from optimal (Balance 2017).
Although the railway may act as a defense line against Bay-Delta flooding, it causes floodwaters to back up during high flows along low-lying channels, acting as a dam to stormwater flows. Similar conditions are seen for the Willow Creek tributary that runs through the site that feeds into the tidal wetland to the north. As such Ambrose Center Park and the surrounding neighborhood is likely to be flooded as water table levels increase with rising sea level. As mentioned previously, multiple site visits and reports from Ambrose Recreation and Park staff have indicated multiple locations of flooding on the site even during regular rainfall events. Flooding is noticed ponding in the parking lot and often spills onto the sidewalks and the southwest entrance to the community building. This poses safety concerns for seniors and persons with disabilities parking at the community center and entering the building (see attached letter from Ambrose Recreation and Park District). This also results in the loss of parking spaces and decreased access to the building for local community members wanting to use the district’s services. 
Accordingly, the primary benefit of the project is stormwater/flood management and during the preliminary prioritization analysis conducted for the Contra Costa County SWRP (<https://www.cccleanwater.org/development-infrastructure/stormwater-resource-plan>), the project site area was ranked as one of the highest priority sites in Bay Point to benefit from the implementation of stormwater management receiving scores for removing pollutant loads, providing flood control, reestablishing natural drainage, developing open space, and enhancing the local community.

Over a 24-hour period the project will reduce stormwater flows into Willow Pass Road and the Susian Bay by 191,808 cf (9.31%). Using the Contra Costa IMP Sizing calculator the footprint of the project site is expected to provide 42,714 CF of storage volume for stormwater treatment and flow control and reduce onsite flooding and flooding adjacent on Willow Pass Road.

The proposed GI strategies will improve the capacity of the drainage ditch on the west side of the property, alleviate flooding in the parking lot, ballfields, and Willow Pass Road, while also improving the water quality and pollutant load that is ultimately discharged into Suisun Bay. All of these flood reduction benefits will occur within the DAC that depend on Ambrose Park for green space, recreational, education, and community gathering opportunities.

 (Underserved Community)
Ambrose Center Park (located at 3105 Willow Pass Road, Bay Point, CA 94565, 38.02959483938509, -121.93270514732879) is in an area considered part of an underserved community by the EPA in the San Francisco Bay Water Quality Improvement Fund map (denoted in the map to the left by the yellow star). According to the Department of Water Resources, this site also qualifies as a disadvantaged area in their DAC mapping tool (<https://gis.water.ca.gov/app/dacs/>). The MHI of this census tract is $47,778 with a population of 9,047. 
Bay Point experiences significant social and economic disparities compared to the rest of the State. There are higher rates of cost-burdened households and households in poverty located in low-lying areas that are at a high risk of flooding ([DCD 2020](https://envisioncontracosta2040.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/BayPoint_Draft_01-30-2020_corrected-1.pdf)). The presence of heavy industry and manufacturing contributes to pollution, leading to negative health outcomes such as higher instances of low birth weights, asthma-induced and heart-attack induced emergency room visits (DCD 2020). Bay Point is a diverse community, with 15% of the population being Black or African American and 12% being Asian. Additionally, over 56% of people identify as Hispanic or Latino (of any race) (DCD 2020, [BCDC 2017](https://www.adaptingtorisingtides.org/project/contra-costa-county-adapting-to-rising-tides-project/)). A significant proportion of households, approximately 15.2%, have a non-English speaking head of household (BCDC 2017).
The community center in Ambrose Center Park not only acts as the admin offices for the Ambrose Recreation and Park District, it serves the surrounding disadvantaged community as a primary hub for classes, events, and meetings but also as a critical space for cooling during extreme heat events as well as a short term shelter for natural disasters. Each year, the community center serves approximately 4,000 local residents on site (see attached letter from Ambrose Recreation and Park District). The center houses programs that help to educate and uplift young students, including the Ambrose Teen Center, Bay Point Youth Academy, and Little Sprouts Academy. In addition, the adjacent Ambrose Community Garden provides reserved plots for families and individuals to grow food and increase local food supply, food security, and build community through a shared, stewarded space. The community center also provides space for volunteer group meetings, such as the Bay Point Garden Club and there is also a pre-school on the site. This project will reduce the threat of flooding not only to the community center and the surrounding disadvantaged community who use the space but also the adjacent park and Willow Pass Road, which is a main transportation thoroughfare. 
The restoration economy has a long history of being an extractive process, one that plans, implements, and monitors projects in communities with minimal engagement from the individuals and families of these communities. The CCRCD seeks to design projects that are more circular, as compared to extractive, which leaves the project’s benefits in the community. Throughout this process, the CCRCD will engage the community to ensure the project will reflect the local vision that families and individuals have for their own watershed. For planning, we will outreach to the community and hold public input meetings to hear and collect data. This will then be incorporated into design possibilities and shared out to make sure we are hearing the community’s wants correctly. We will also prioritize hiring local contractors during construction. Then, to help train the next generation of environmental workers, we will design a service learning style of monitoring program that hires local community members and prepares folks for environmental maintenance jobs that are needed today and in the future.
