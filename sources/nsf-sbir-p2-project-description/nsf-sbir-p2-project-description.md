**Part 1. Results of the Phase I Project.**
The objective of Phase 1 R\&D was to create a prototype software tool that takes as input low-cost and widely available information about a geographic location, and outputs site analytics and design guidance for green infrastructure investments. The output analytics are of such a quality that could have previously only been obtained through expensive surveys, and the design guidance through costly hourly work of drafters, landscape architects, and ecosystem service valuation professionals. The objective of Phase I R\&D was principally focused on a method for quickly estimating the leaf area density (LAD) of roadside vegetation from widely available street-view photographs, but at a level of detail equivalent to that of data gathered from expensive LIDAR instruments, and processed at great computational costs. The method required gathering LIDAR point-clouds of trees, and using the Beer-Lambert law to generate the point relative density index (RDI, a factor that can be related to LAD by using voxel size, estimated leaf angel and a species specific coefficient) from the LIDAR data. The resulting LIDAR-LAD estimate was then paired with street-view photography and used to train neural networks to estimate the LAD from photographs. The results of such inference can be used to determine the air-pollution mitigating potential of roadside vegetation before and after interventions intended to improve air quality via ecosystem building. Our phase I work tested several neural network architectures, and discovered that a conditional generative adversarial network (CGAN) based pixel-wise regression model could estimate LAD (unadjusted for species) from street-view photographs with an average R2 of 0.82 on a subset of images used for validation. The CGAN estimated RDI with an average R2 of 0.93. However, we expect the model is overfit on the neighborhoods that we scanned, as the characteristic plant species and urban geometries will inform the model, hence in phase 2 we will scan a diverse selection of neighborhoods to make the model generalizable.
In phase 1 we developed a ground-truthing method for conifer leaf area density estimation based on a surface area to weight ratio of an adhered wax layer. In phase 2 we will gather such samples with each new LIDAR acquisition to ultimately develop a reliable and generalizable estimation of LAD from RDI.
While the bulk of this research was on roadside vegetation and using LIDAR and photography data gathered from automobile mounted sensors, during phase 1 we also began research and development of similar CGAN algorithms to estimate wider area leaf area index (LAI) from aerial photographs trained on LAI calculated from aerial LIDAR acquisitions. This enables consideration of the impacts of backyard vegetation, vegetation on the interior of large properties, and connectivity at larger scales of the ecosystem. During phase 1 when estimating 1 meter resolution LAI from non-contemporaneous 30cm resolution aerial 3-band imagery, our average pixel to pixel R2 was 0.3. However, subsequently we obtained contemporaneous 18cm 4band imagery, and found that changing the resolution of the output LAI resulted in different levels of accuracy. When estimating 1 meter resolution LAI from 18cm resolution aerial 4-band imagery, our average pixel to pixel R2 was 0.75 (with some examples of the inference shown in figure 1), while estimating 3m LAI from 18cm imagery yielded an average R2 of 0.89. Estimating 46m LAI from 18cm imagery gave an average R2 of 0.98.  
Going into phase I there was great uncertainty that the proposed method of using paired LIDAR derived leaf area estimates and photography to train neural networks would be a feasible process for  estimating leaf area from photographs. However, the success of phase I demonstrates the feasibility of that approach, which can be leveraged to its full commercial potential through phase II funding.

During phase 1 we also initiated research and development of the automated intervention design synthesis component of the software tool, which estimates plantable areas from satellite or aerial imagery (using neural networks trained on aerial LIDAR) and creates tree planting plans that are optimized for air quality improvement. The initial estimation of plantable area was somewhat crude, in that it combined aerial LIDAR derived height data with multispectral imaging to provide a raster that denotes ground areas that are not paved, and are also devoid of trees. This represents a maximum available space (without costly concrete removal) but does not include setbacks from overhead utility lines, intersection sight-lines, edge of road surfaces and other planting setback requirements that may vary between municipalities. While we found that the cGAN pipeline can also be used to estimate this directly from low-cost aerial imagery, we decided to put more effort into this in phase 2, where we can train the algorithm on data that has all of the setbacks specified, which will make the plantable areas much more realistic. 
 In phase 1 we also began developing the automated tree placement algorithms that take the plantable area as an input and programmatically builds vegetated air barrier planting plans in roadside parcels. In phase 2 we will integrate this into the platform and user interface. We will also expand it to be responsive to various design rubrics and stakeholder constraints.
During phase I we assessed the feasibility of using CFD simulations to vet design iterations and validate the utility of leaf area density as a design parameter. These simulations modeled particulate matter transport from traffic sources through and around roadside vegetation. The LAD was varied to see how deposition and dispersion of particulates was impacted by variations in LAD. The results were consistent with the hypothesis that more LAD leads to more deposition and dispersion, and is hence beneficial for receptors downwind of a roadside vegetated buffer. However, additional modeling indicates that such conclusions can only be casually leveraged in cases of well designed, dense, uninterrupted roadside vegetated air barriers. While seeking such interventions is a core focus of our approach, it is important to note that in cases where trees are  lollipop-shaped or buffers have gaps, air pollution can be trapped near ground level, or accumulate due to air velocity reductions, making conditions potentially more hazardous than with no trees at all. Such conditions, in particular lollipop-shaped trees, are common, especially when designing for heat island reduction through shade production. Thus the common tree planting approaches used for air pollution mitigation and heat island mitigation can be at cross-purposes in near-road environments. This complexity creates a need for cost-benefit equations to include the relative benefits of air pollution and heat mitigation in the evaluation of design iterations. These relative benefits can be estimated with the environmental simulation pipeline that we began to develop in phase 1. The CFD platform that we used for this, ENVI-MET, while practical for manual testing cannot be easily automated or incorporated into our automated design tool pipeline, hence in phase II we will utilize openFoam, an open source CFD library that we can customize for our purposes and include in our platform as an automated fluid solver for design optimization.
During phase 1 we developed a backend database architecture to facilitate the processing and utilization of large amounts of custom geospatial data types, and a front-end user interface for providing analysis and design products to customers. In phase 2 we will update this to include new functionality warranted by the new analytical products, new design products, and distilled insights. 
Concomitant to the phase 1 research, we have worked with our network of partnerships to implement evidence-based roadside greening interventions geared towards air-quality and community health improvement. As of July 2021 our partners in the Green Heart Project with funding from the National Institutes of Health have gathered baseline biomarkers and other health data from 700 community members in the south Louisville residential neighborhood that we scanned during phase I. Since then with funding from The Nature Conservancy our partners have used our designs to plant \>2000 of 8000 planned mature trees in the neighborhood, including dense vegetated air-barriers along the major freeway that cuts through the neighborhood. We have also returned to scan the new trees and to enable consideration of partially planted states in our future analysis of the air quality monitoring that has gone on continuously throughout the project. In phase 2 our partners will finish planting the rest of the trees and continue monitoring the air quality and health study participant biomarkers in the planted and adjacent control neighborhoods. We will then use this data to inform our product’s cost-benefit analysis, taking advantage of measured efficacy of different types of interventions on air quality and human health outcomes. 
Since completing phase I, we have engaged with a number of customers seeking the outputs of our design tool, and have used the prototype developed in phase I to provide the outputs to them. In discussions with customers and potential customers, we have learned that there are key features of the product that are of more or less value depending on the situation. For example, customers in the early pre-funding stage of projects are looking for cost-benefit analysis derived from the combination of site analysis, schematic design, and pre-intervention environmental monitoring. However, customers who have moved into the preconstruction and construction phases are more interested in detailed design drawings and post-intervention monitoring and evaluation.  Our phase 1 efforts mainly addressed the urban scanning locations will also include Austin, TX, Baltimore, MD, Birmingham, AL, Brownsville, TX, Chicago, IL, Cleveland, OH, Des Moines, IA, Durham, NC, Fresno, CA, Green Bay, WI, Los Angeles, CA , Memphis, TN, Milwaukee, WI, Minneapolis / St. Paul, MN , New Bedford, MZ, New Haven, CT, New York, NY, Paterson, NJ, Philadelphia, PA, Phoenix, AZ, Pittsburgh, PA, Portland, ME, Portland, OR, Salt Lake City, UT, Sonoma County, CA, St. Louis, MO, Tampa, FL, VA Beach/Williamsburg, VA, Washington, DC, and Woodbine, IA.former analysis and schematic design, we will use phase 2 funding to continue developing these. 
**Part 2. Phase II Technical Objectives, Approach and Work Plan.**
**Increase Street-view algorithm accuracy through additional training:**
Currently the working cGAN models we developed in phase I were trained on LIDAR data that we gathered in West Oakland, CA and Louisville, KY. While the models have low error in those neighborhoods, we expect them to have higher error in neighborhoods that have substantially different vegetation species, architecture, road layouts and general character. Consequently in phase II we will expand our LIDAR data gathering to many other cities, to obtain a more generalized cross section of the types of neighborhoods that will be queried as inputs to the product. We will also incorporate publicly available mobile LIDAR-RGB datasets to leverage the large amount of data intended to support the development of self-driving vehicles for our vegetation models.
For street-view analysis training, we must gather paired LIDAR scans and 360o RGB imagery from many locations that have a broad variety of ecotypes, architecture and land-use typologies. These locations should thus represent a cross-section of US environments. We will gather urban data in cities where we are currently engaged with local communities and governments to use the design tool to develop interventions, including Richmond, CA, Stockton CA, Pittsburgh, CA, Dallas, TX,  as well as additional parts of Oakland, CA and Louisville, KY. To overlap with EPA’s Enviroatlas communities, these phase 2  We will also scan non-urban areas to ensure generalizability of the algorithm. Non-urban areas that we will scan in phase 2 include both agricultural areas and undeveloped areas. For example, a cross section of agricultural regions in each of the USDA’s 10 farm production areas will be included in the training datasets, as will undeveloped forest, mountain, desert, prairie and other areas within each region. This comprehensive spatial spread of data gathering will allow us to train a highly generalizable algorithm that can be used for accurate inference in a wide variety of places. 
Each location will be scanned in the summer and in the late fall, winter or early spring so that a distribution of leaf phenology is represented in the training data. In addition to gathering paired LIDAR and RGB data from each location, we will also gather samples for physical surface area measurement using the wax-dip method. This whole scanning effort will take place throughout the first year of the project, with the second year allocated to processing the data. 
Once the scanning is conducted, the data must be processed, with the LIDAR-LAD estimation via Beer’s law performed on each frame, which is then aligned with the corresponding RGB imagery. Subsequently the image-LIDAR pairs must be used to train the CGAN to develop the inference model. 
**Groundtruth LAD:**
In phase 1 we developed promising proof of concept for estimating the LAD as measured with LIDAR from photographs. However, for maximum utility we must ground truth the LIDAR derived metrics with direct measures of plant surface area. In phase 1 we took hundreds of samples of foliage from LIDAR scanned trees and measured their surface area using our wax-dip method. We found that the surface area per unit of crown volume spanned multiple orders of magnitude across species, and this led us to the conclusion that different leaf morphologies may produce different relations between wax-dip surface area and LIDAR measured LAD. For example one can intuitively imagine that the attenuation of LIDAR pulses by a very densely packed group of thin pine needles might be less than that of a single broad-leaved leaf of much lower surface area. Thus to maximize the accuracy of estimates of the LAD from the LIDAR a species specific coefficient is desired. While deriving such a coefficient for every species that we might encounter would be a monumental task, it may nonetheless be helpful to derive coefficients for a smaller number of broad classes of leaf morphology. Thus in phase 2 we will train our instance segmentation algorithms to discern between needleaves and broadleaves, and within those categories we will further classify each tree as (in the case of needleaves) complex (like Junipers and Cypress) vs simple (like Pines) and (in the case of broadleaves) as large-leaved (like Sycamore, Sugar Maple, Northern Red Oak), or small-leaved (like Holly, Willow, Coast Live Oak). This categorization can be used to set the coefficients used for calculating the LAD from the LIDAR derived RDI which is then used to train the CGAN. 
**Increase aerial LAI algorithm generalizability through more training:**
For aerial LAI from 4-band imagery, we anticipate that phase 2 funds will not be sufficient to mobilize aerial LIDAR infrastructure such as the piloted aircraft that we leveraged in phase I. While we can gather some aerial data using UAVs; as these have a limited ceiling by FAA regulations and cannot operate at all near airports (a common condition in urban areas) the bulk of the aerial LIDAR data will come from public datasets gathered by state, county and federal initiatives, as well as USGS terrain mapping projects and NOAA’s digital coast program. These LIDAR datasets cover many areas and in some areas cover multiple years. Figure 2 shows a map of areas covered by aerial LIDAR available in the USGS database; additional data is available from other sources, for example Kentucky and Texas have programs to fly LIDAR for their whole states and make that data available publicly, while Montana and Oregon provide substantially more data for their states than USGS. .

**CGAN optimization:**
In phase 1 we used the pix2pix algorithm as presented in the original paper (Isola et al. 2017). However, as we have scaled up our training dataset sizes we have noted that the CPU ram requirements can become awkwardly high, in our last training set we used just under 2 terabytes of memory. While we got around this by deploying swap space on an NVME drive (a slow workaround), and are proposing to outfit workstations in phase 2 with 2 TB of RAM (see budget justification), we may be able to reduce the memory requirement of the training by altering the algorithm. van der Ouderaa  & Worrall (2019) propose an invertible CGAN architecture which uses a fixed memory budget per layer of depth, as opposed to a cumulatively increasing one as in pix2pix. While this may only partially reduce our memory requirements, in phase 2 we will test the inverted CGAN approach to see if it performs better. 
Using higher resolution images in our CGAN training will also be necessary to employ the types of information used in our plant placement and setback analysis, (as shown in figure 4). This is because it will be difficult to measure setbacks of 20 feet for example from features that might be outside a given 256X256 pixel image chunk. Hence the larger the image size the better for training CGAN on setbacks. However, using larger images in pix2pix further exacerbates the memory utilization issues. However, Laxman et al. (2021) have developed a multiscale gradient implementation of pix2pix type paired image to image translation that halves the memory requirements for higher resolution images without sacrificing accuracy. In phase 2 we will test this approach for increasing the resolution of our CGAN data. 
**Plantable area from aerial imagery algorithm:**
Plantable area in this context consists of the locations in the landscape where it is feasible to plant trees or shrubs without removing concrete or structures. While it is in many cases possible to remove concrete, this substantially increases the costs of implementing greening and must be addressed on a more case-by-case basis. A crude method of estimating plantable area thus involves finding the grassy or bare earth regions that are not already covered with tree canopy or shrubs. Our existing LIDAR processing algorithm can do this already. However, it is important to consider what we call “setbacks” when delineating plantable area. Setbacks refers to the distances that must be maintained between planting installations and various features of the built environment, for example many municipalities require that trees be planted more than 15 feet from overhead power lines, more than 20 feet from intersections, more than 10 feet from utility poles, more than 10 feet from fire hydrants, more than 8 feet from roads that are both salted and snow-plowed etc. Once all of these factors are accounted for the actual plantable area is significantly less than the crude measure using only grassy non-canopy areas. Thus for phase 2 we will train our algorithm to identify these setbacks when estimating the plantable area prior to providing the plantable area to the plant placement design engine. For training a model to estimate plantable area, we will use the same LIDAR data as is used for estimating LAI and LAD to estimate plantable area, but using a different point cloud processing algorithm. The phase 2 algorithm will identify the pertinent features of landscape from the LIDAR, such as overhead power lines, edges of pavement, utility poles, and signage. Once all of the features have been identified, the setbacks are drawn from a customizable set of rules that can be altered by the user (since different municipalities have different regulations concerning setbacks). These sets of rules will be included in the input dataset for training the CGAN to estimate the plantable areas from aerial imagery, so that they can be altered prior to inference to generate the correct setbacks.

 The parametric approach requires writing specific equations and code to identify planting locations and choose plant types according to a set of logical rules. The list of rules and rubric elements will be accessible to the software user to modify the placement algorithm as needed to adapt to different stakeholder’s needs. For example one of our customers in a residential neighborhood wants to maintain sightlines for crime prevention strategies so prefers there be no shrubs taller than 4 ft or trees with canopies below 6ft, while another one of our customers managing a freeway right of way isn’t concerned with crime prevention but is very interested in eliminating mowable areas (to reducing mowing budgets). **Plant Placement Design goals:**
The algorithms developed in phase 1 for plant placement emphasized design of vegetated air-barriers along highly trafficked roadways. Because of the continued importance of air pollution as a mediator of health benefits of green infrastructure, we will continue this focus on air pollution mitigation for community health moving into phase 2. However, since we initiated phase 1, more research has been published potentially linking other benefits of green infrastructure to high-value health benefits, in ways potentially unrelated to air pollution mitigation. For example, greenness measured by NDVI was found to correlate with lower cancer mortality, with a minimum of confounding due to PM2.5 exposure (Coleman et al. 2021), suggesting the possibility of non air-pollution mechanisms for protective effects of vegetation. Some such mechanisms have been putatively identified, for example plant biodiversity has been linked to a decreased risk of asthma, where overall greenness measured by NDVI increased risk, when controlling for air pollution as a covariate (Donovan, Landry, & Gatziolis, 2021). Plant biodiversity was strongly correlated with decreased childhood leukemia (Donovan et al. 2021), with a hypothesized mechanism involving immune system regulation via the biodiversity hypothesis of inflammatory disease, which posits that decreased microbiome diversity contributes to chronic inflammatory and autoimmune disease through undereducation of the immune system (Haahtela et al. 2013). The leaf surface area metrics that our tool emphasizes intuitively relate to such hypotheses by providing the substrate for the foliar microbiome (Stone et al. 2018), and indeed leaf area has been correlated with biodiversity in general (Peng et al. 2017). Other structural properties of vegetation (vertical stratification and complexity), have been shown to strongly correlate with nearby airborne microbial biodiversity (Robinson et al. 2021). Indeed, diversity of street-tree species has recently been shown to correlate with reduced risk of cardiovascular and stroke mortality (Giacinto et al. 2021).  Thus in addition to optimizing designs around air pollution mitigation, our plant placement algorithms will be expanded to seek maximization of biodiversity, which beyond maximizing surface area includes facilitating structural diversity, species and genera diversity, and, to further ensure both structural diversity and resilience, diverse ages of plants to encourage continuous succession.
While schematic level designs such as the one shown in figure 4d specify only the size and shape of plants proposed for an intervention, and can be used to generate rough cost and benefit estimates, implementation of planting plans requires more detailed designs that specify plant genus, species, and variety or cultivar. The choice of species requires not just knowing which species will thrive in a given environment under given site conditions, but also what plants are available in the desired size categories in the local horticulture trade. We have assembled databases of tree, shrub, forb and grass  species/varieties with pertinent  attributes, such as maximum height and width, moisture requirements, sunlight/shade requirements, disease resistance/concerns, soil pH and drainage requirements, growth rates, leaf area density ranges, hardiness zones / temperature requirements, salt tolerance (very important for roadside plantings in areas with snow), etc. for various locations where we have done designs. For the phase 2 software development, we will deploy these databases into a unified structure that can be accessed by the plant placement algorithm to maximize biodiversity in designs while using the most appropriate species for a specific area. While maximizing diversity of an intervention in isolation may be somewhat trivial given a list of appropriate and available species, maximizing the diversity of the area in which that intervention is located requires also considering the existing species distribution, as when considering diversity in the information entropy context (for example the Shannon Diversity Index), adding additional members of already well represented species will decrease diversity. Hence the algorithm will also be programmed to consider local existing population data, if available (ie in neighborhoods where we already have tree survey data). While such functionality will eventually be added to our street-view analysis pipeline to identify tree species from google photographs through instance segmentation, we are not proposing to complete a generalizable version of such an algorithm in phase 2. 
Connectivity is another important parameter to maximize when seeking healthy, resilient biomes, and itself encourages biodiversity (Kong et al. 2010), hence in phase 2 we will also include connectivity measures as parameters in plant placement optimization algorithms. Connectivity metrics are many and varied, but several have gained traction as ecologically important in the context of urban forestry. The methods of Saura & Torne (2009) use graph theory to derive several metrics of connectivity between habitat patches, and that paper has been cited at least 450 times in the ecological literature. We are also developing our own metrics using the surface area data that we generate with our phase 1 software. For example we take the leaf area index raster, and each pixel is considered a node, and edges connect nodes with weights corresponding to the node’s leaf area index value, resulting in a graph of the leaf area network. In this case pixels with 0 LAI (representing areas with no leaves) have no node, so only components that are within a pixels distance from each other are connected, and within connected components the more leaf area a pixel has, the more capacity (edge weight) it has in its connection to its nearest neighbors. The capacity refers physically to the ability to support and exchange microorganisms primarily, but is expected to also correlate closely with the ability to support insects and other larger animals. By doing network analysis on the graph, for example by computing the eigenvector centrality (discussed in the landscape ecological context by Estrada & Bodin, 2008) we can assign relative importance to each node. Figure 5 shows the results of this analysis, with LAI on the left and eigenvector centrality of the LAI graph on the right. We will include computation of this and other connectivity metrics (such as those described in Saura & Torne, 2009) in the phase 2 tool analytical engine so that these measures can be used for ongoing clinical health studies and correlated with existing health and biodiversity geospatial datasets, as well as for guiding the design process of implementations towards maximizing connectivity and biodiversity.

The user interface will allow a connectivity strategy to be selected, and then the plant placement algorithm will use that to prioritize planting within the site constraints. The outputs of design iterations can be run through the same connectivity analyses as the analytical engine provides for existing conditions. This will enable the user to test different strategies for balancing connectivity with other objectives. 
Leaf surface area is also related to evapotranspiration (Yan et al. 2012), which along with shade drives urban heat island effect mitigation by trees (Paschalis et al. 2021). The urban heat island effect is anticipated to more than double the costs associated with climate change over traditionally studied effects such as sea-level rise and water resource loss, with heat island costs alone to take up to 10% of GDP for badly hit cities by the end of the century (Estrada et al. 2017). One cost estimate found that the positive return on investment for green infrastructure as a mitigation strategy for heat island exceeded that of a reflective surfaces strategy (Johnson et al. 2020). Thus in addition to optimizing plantings to maximize air pollution mitigation and biodiversity, in phase 2 we will expand the algorithms to consider the benefits of shade and evapotranspiration for heat island mitigation. Since completion of phase 1, one of our customers has requested a specific emphasis on this, and so we have prototyped a pipeline for our software that includes both analysis of land surface temperature from satellite data sources and modeling of the potential impact of interventions on air temperature and thermal comfort indices.  Figure 6 shows the outputs of such an analysis in the customer’s medical district, in which the effect of 3d building models and 3d vegetation models (derived from LIDAR) on wind velocity in a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation domain are used to estimate the universal thermal climate index (UTCI). The UTCI is a thermophysiological comfort index; an equivalent temperature adjusted for biophysical responses to meteorological inputs (Jendritzky et al. 2012). We use the UTCI derived from CFD simulations of wind, vegetation and structures to guide the prioritization of tree planting sites for heat island mitigation. When pollution deposition and dispersion are included in the simulations, the relative contributions of heat and pollution mitigation to the cost benefit analysis of green infrastructure interventions can be estimated.

Impact assessment functions of the phase 2 tool will calculate the air pollution and temperature mitigation effects of the proposed design. This will require creating a “digital twin” (digital 3d model of the terrain, buildings and vegetation) of the site and planting plan, and simulating traffic emissions deposition and dispersion as well as  heat island mitigation by existing and installed vegetation. We will employ a fluid dynamic simulation in the digital twin combined with a radiative transfer model to calculate these benefits. 
The OpenFoam fluid solver takes as inputs meshes of buildings and terrain interpreted as solids, and the mesh of vegetation interpreted as a porous solid, which acts as a momentum sink according to the Darcy-Forchheimer Equation, with the LAD serving as the appropriate specific surface area coefficient. Our aerial LIDAR processing pipeline already segments buildings, terrain and vegetation into separate point clouds, so each can be meshed separately and recombined. The fluid solver software requires fastidiously watertight and non-self-intersecting meshes, so our phase 2 automation development will focus on converting the aerial LIDAR buildings into meshes using a geometric method inspired by Bauchet & Lafarge (2019). We will also investigate using our CGAN pipeline; by training the CGAN on the LIDAR digital surface model paired with aerial imagery, it may be possible to generate 2.5d models directly from aerial imagery where no LIDAR is available. The vegetation will be voxelized into LAD voxels, and then meshed with a convex hull algorithm. The resulting set of meshes will be provided to the openFoam solver, with the LAD values used to provide the surface area coefficient to the Darcy-Forchheimer equation used for the treatment of the vegetation mesh as a porous volume. We will also provide meteorological data and a series of simulations will be performed. The simulations will solve Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes equations on the domain given wind vectors representing the prevailing winds at the peak of summer and the dead of winter. For air pollution modeling, we will use traffic count information gathered from state transportation departments or purchased from Streetlight to assign a relative source value. We will use the scalar transport equations proposed in Tong et al. (2016), and have vegetation zones serve as a sink using the deposition model of Zhang et al. (2001). For these estimates we will focus on ultra-fine particulates (\<100nm) as they are the most health impacting, but will also include calculations for \<2.5𝜇m particles, as they are a popular metric for comparison. For thermal comfort calculations we will use the outputs of the openFoam simulation to provide wind vectors to the UTCI equation, along with temperature, radiation and humidity data (from EPW files obtained from DOE’s EnergyPlus Weather program), and sky exposure + direct corporeal irradiation calculated from the 3d model.  The results of the UTCI simulations will be fed back into the design process as shown in figure 6. The results of the pollution simulations will be fed back into the design process to seek 2 goals: Firstly, “do no harm”; intervention designs that make air pollution worse in populated areas will be noted as counterproductive and avoided. Secondly, “maximize reductions”; the design process will be iterated towards greater reductions in pollution in populated areas. These are the rubrics that will be deployed in the phase 2 product. During phase 2 we will explore the integration of EPA’s Benmap tool (Sacks et al. 2018) for providing financial impacts from health-care costs and mortality costs. These require formulating inputs as tons of pollutant removed, which will require further validation of the simulations using air monitoring which will be funded beyond phase 2 SBIR.

**Part 3. Broader Impacts: Societal, educational or scientific benefits beyond commercial considerations.**
The health and prosperity of the American People are imperilled by climate change, pollution, and biodiversity loss. These problems have not already been solved because they are complex, multidisciplinary, and expensive to deal with. Our software platform addresses this by reducing the costs of the complex multidisciplinary processes involved in analysis, modeling, design, implementation and evaluation of precision greening projects. This will make climate adaptation, pollution mitigation and ecosystem defragmentation more readily available to the communities that need them most. This increased availability of adaptive infrastructure to vulnerable populations will improve health outcomes, reduce health-care costs and increase economic opportunities.
