## **Welcome to the Integrated Climate Adaptation and Resilience Program (ICARP) [Extreme Heat and Community Resilience Program ](https://opr.ca.gov/climate/icarp/grants/extreme-heat-community-resilience.html)(EHCRP) Full Application Form.**

Completed Full Application Forms will be accepted from January 29, 2024 – May 7th 11:59 P.M. PST.
### Program Background
The [Extreme Heat and Community Resilience Program](https://opr.ca.gov/climate/icarp/grants/extreme-heat-community-resilience.html) (EHCRP) funds planning and implementation projects to reduce the impacts of extreme heat, build community resilience, and create frameworks for change. 
**Grant Types:** Planning and Implementation 
**Award Amounts:  **
  - A total of $3 million will be available for Small Planning Grants, with each award ranging from $100,000 to $250,000. 
  - A total of $5 million will be available for Large Planning Grants with each award ranging from $300,000 and $750,000. 
  - A total of $3 million will be available for Small Implementation Grants with each award ranging from $100,000 and $450,000. 
  - A total of $9 million will be available for Large Implementation Grants with each award ranging from $500,000 and $4 million. 
**Eligibility:  **
*Eligible entities include* Local and Regional Public Entities, Academic Institutions, California Native American Tribes, Community-based Organizations, and Coalitions.  
*Ineligible entities include* for-profit entities, state agencies, and individual community members are *not* eligible to apply as Applicants or fiscal sponsors but can be subcontracted to provide research or other services. 
**EHCRP Timeline:**
  - **29 January 2024:** EHCRP Round 1 Final Guidelines, Notice of Funding Availability (NOFA), Pre-Application Interest Form released 
  - **29 January 2024 – 23 April 2024:** Application Period and Application Workshops  
  - **5 March 2024, 5p.m. PST:** Request for comprehensive technical assistance due. To be prioritized for technical assistance, submit the Pre-Application Interest Form by March 5 at 5 p.m. PST**. **After this date, the technical assistance provider may not be able to review or respond to your request.
  - **23 April 2024, 11:59 p.m. PST:** Due Date for Submitting Pre-Application Interest Form. 
  - **7 May 2024, 11:59 p.m. PST: **Due Date for Submitting Full Application Form.
**Additional Resources:**
  - Refer to the [EHCRP R1 Final Grant Guidelines](https://opr.ca.gov/climate/icarp/grants/docs/20240129-EHCRP-R1-Guidelines.pdf) for comprehensive eligible activities, program requirements, and additional project guidance. 
  - Register for an application workshop or office hours on the [EHCRP website](https://opr.ca.gov/climate/icarp/grants/extreme-heat-community-resilience.html).

## **Project Vision and Description**
*Up to 20 points *
**Project Vision: Provide a vision statement. What is the vision for this project? Where could this project take your community? Who are your local champions? Explain how this project builds community resilience to extreme heat and aligns with the vision and values of the EHCRP.\***
The vision of the California Environmental Justice Coalition is to form a community-led research observatory and task force capable of monitoring and mitigating the deadly effects of extreme heat in the Central and Imperial valleys. Our project, the Community-led Microclimate Observation Network (COMMON), aims to transform these regions into models of climate resilience, and brings a unique focus on farmworker-populated agricultural areas that are often left out of extreme heat remediation efforts directed towards cities.
In this endeavor, CEJC has partnered with environmental justice member organizations with proven records of championing their community’s health and environment. These organizations, supported by technical partners, stand to make a historic intervention in the field by correlating hyperlocal extreme heat data with population and hospital data that will provide updated heat-health intervention measures, provide better outreach and early warning systems, and an empirical basis for updating codes, standards, and regulations that presently fail to protect vulnerable populations from extreme heat.  
As COMMON is designed not only to monitor but also to actively respond to extreme heat, both with emergency outreach tools and climate adaptation solutions, its goals of equity, community leadership, and scientific rigor are in clear alignment with those of the EHCRP.
Limit: 200 words
*Points Available: 8*
**Project Description: Provide a comprehensive overview of the project by clearly describing the key steps and deliverables of the project.\***
COMMON aims to enhance the climate resilience of the San Joaquin and Imperial Valleys with community-driven solutions. The following 30-month implementation timeline combines community-led outreach, sensor installation, data collection, software development, and mitigation solutions.
*Year 1: Coalition-building and Citizen Science*
\- CEJC and Partners conduct rigorous community outreach campaigns across the San Joaquin and Imperial valleys to identify heat-vulnerable neighborhoods.
\- Simultaneously, technical Partners from NASA’s Jet Propulsion Lab (JPL), Hyphae Design Labs (HDL), and UC Merced (UCM) model the area’s potential high-risk environments. High resolution modeling, usable within first year of project, provides communities with data to choose where to deploy sensors, conduct simulations, and plot mitigation strategies. HDL then trains monitoring technicians of respective Partners on assembly and installation of open source thermal stress sensors, as well as in use of Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform to collect and interpret the data.
*Year 2 and 3: Immediate Use of Data & Sensor Deployment *
\- HDL develops an extreme heat early warning and outreach mobile app capable of alerting residents about heat events and guiding users to cooling centers and sensitive receptor sites, avoiding hotspots along the way.
\- By Month 24, Partners and HDL have installed sensor network in selected neighborhoods, 
\- Partners assist HDL, JPL and UCM in providing community-driven data overlay of community’s primary walking and transit routes, gathering places, and sensitive receptors.
\- Hyphae creates high resolution digital twins of geographical areas prioritized by communities. 
\- Partners and community finalize targeted greening sites with Tree Fresno.
*Greening Projects and Adaptation Reporting*
\- In year three, installed pilot greening projects are outfitted with sensors to monitor impact on local microclimates and evaluate their effectiveness as natural cooling measures.
\- Project culminates in report detailing outcomes and efficacy of interventions, community feedback, and models for expanding COMMON to other regions.

Though each of the subcontracted CEJC partners fights its own unique pollution challenges, we are all united in the fight against extreme heat. In order to adequately assess the vulnerability of our respective communities, however, we require data that has enough granularity to a) pinpoint high-risk areas and b) correlate that vulnerability with health outcomes. With this hyperlocal data provided by our technical partners, we can then direct decision-makers to implement projects, codes, standards, regulations, and policies that protect our populations from extreme heat.
<comment_start id=kix.2xt15me0rv52>Based on a combination of remote sensing data, environmental simulations, and community-placed heat stress sensors, the community will use Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform to generate meter-scale thermal stress metrics. These calculations first integrate 70 meter regional ECOSTRESS satellite surface temperature data, weather data, and Google-Streetview derived skyview/shade-factor imagery for each region. Based on this heat data, as well as on an assessment of sensitive receptors and mitigation potential, CEJC’s collaborative process produces a first-level prioritization of target neighborhoods. HDL will then generate a digital twin from LIDAR data at the 1 meter scale, which will enable hyperlocal environmental simulations of heat stress in the target neighborhoods. These metrics are generated iteratively as the community discusses prioritization and mitigation strategies to adaptively optimize the priority locations based on heat stress potential (temperature, population, sensitive receptors), mitigation potential (suitability for addition of cooling infrastructure or route planning), and logistics (mitigation costs). Community members will place thermal stress sensors in the target neighborhoods to validate the simulations. A cohort of volunteers will also be outfitted with mobile sensors to track thermal stress throughout the activity spaces of the community. Trusted community leaders then circulate through the chosen neighborhoods to confirm that the gathering places of its most vulnerable inhabitants are carefully mapped out and included in the models.<comment_end id=kix.2xt15me0rv52>

# California Environmental Justice Coalition (CEJC) Extreme Heat Grant Proposal
April 24, 2024
The following is a draft summary of CEJC’s proposed grant application for the upcoming Extreme Heat and Community Resilience Program (EHCRP), a grant offered by the State of California’s Office of Planning and Research (OPR) [Integrated Climate Adaptation and Resiliency Program (ICARP)](https://opr.ca.gov/climate/icarp/).
About Us
The California Environmental Justice Coalition (CEJC) represents over 90 grassroots environmental justice communities across the State. Each organization fights its unique pollution challenges ranging from landfill regulation to unsafe school environments, water quality, and even decommissioned nuclear weapons facilities. These disparate issues arise out of a shared legacy of racist policies redlining and its unfair distribution of pollution burdens on our disadvantaged communities. In 2021, the National Weather Service classified extreme heat events as the most lethal weather-related cause of death in the United States. The revelation was not news to us here in the Central Valley, the region targeted by the following large implementation project. As our coalition has been dealing with extreme heat long before it entered the national conversation, the Office of Planning and Research can be confident that our coalition is informed, organized, and prepared to respond on Day 1 of our proposed 30-month timeline. 
The Plan
CEJC proposes a Community-led Microclimate Observatory Network (ComMON) in two counties of the Central Valley, with ambitions to expand to other counties with other partners. Our endeavor combines data collection, emergency response software modeling, and nature-based solutions in heat-stressed areas of urban Stockton and Fresno, as well as in outlying agricultural areas, all delivered, operated, and maintained by trusted community-based organizations. We first propose a community-led installation and deployment of a sensor grid to monitor extreme heat. Next, we subcontract our partners, Hyphae Design Labs, in developing an extreme heat notification and outreach app to support communities during extreme heat events. Finally, we propose a series of unique pilot greening programs, the impact of which will be included in the sensor network’s ongoing data collection efforts as a measure of the Observatory’s practical use. 
Our proposed sensor network adds the data set of extreme heat into existing efforts to monitor and mitigate air pollution in the San Joaquin Valley’s SJVAir Collaborative, the largest community-managed remote sensor network in the country. We then reinforce these data-sets with satellite and aerial imagery provided by NASA’s Jet Propulsion Lab and then sync them with UC Merced’s ongoing efforts to correlate heat stress with rates of hospital admittance. Finally, these varying data streams are fed into Hyphae’s cutting edge modeling instruments to create an early warning outreach tool that a) predicts extreme heat events and b) generates shade maps that guide users through the coolest routes to schools, job sites, and community centers, and away from the hottest parts of the city. In the final phase of the implementation process, after the installation of the sensor network and the development of the outreach app, we will implement a series of pilot greening programs to be planted in 6 designated areas of San Joaquin and Fresno County. The efficacy of these greening interventions will then be monitored by the installed sensor apparatus, thus demonstrating the utility of ComMON as an extreme heat observatory and task force.

**Current heat warning systems are woefully inadequate in an age of climate change**
By Ashley Ward April 11, 2023
https://www.statnews.com/2023/04/11/heat-warning-systems-health-risks-climate-change/
"We need effective heat warning systems to properly inform [people at risk](https://www.statnews.com/2022/07/19/heat-waves-risk-to-people-with-chronic-illness/) when they should take caution. The first step is to revisit and redesign our current systems, which are deeply inadequate. Health problems from heat occur at temperatures significantly lower than when warnings are issued. This is because, for the most part, weather warnings for heat are issued based on the probability and magnitude of the heat event itself, rather than health outcomes. For example, local National Weather Service offices may issue a heat warning for a heat event that falls in the 98th percentile of heat events for that area, when health impacts are [seen](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7126132/) at the 95th percentile or even lower. This means that health impacts are experienced long before any warning is issued.
Additionally, current heat warning systems often rely upon simple indicators of dangerous conditions, such as daytime maximum temperatures. But more robust measures exist. For instance, health impacts are closely linked to persistently high overnight temperatures. This is particularly the case among our most vulnerable, such as pregnant women, who experience [increased risk of preterm birth](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31367892/) when overnight temperatures remain high.
[Wet bulb globe temperature](https://convergence.unc.edu/tools/wbgt/) is also an effective measure for exertional heat stress that can affect student athletes or outdoor workers, more so than simple daytime maximum temperatures. The wet bulb globe temperature accounts for wind speed and solar radiation, which play a significant role in heat-health outcomes, in addition to air temperature and humidity. Even heat index, which combines both temperature and humidity and is commonly used by decision-makers, would allow for a more robust metric for a formal warning system.
Different groups of people are vulnerable to heat at different thresholds, which further complicates the warning system challenges. Aging populations and pregnant women are susceptible to heat at different thresholds than working-age people, who are susceptible at different thresholds than student athletes. We need to establish a commission of scientists who can evaluate these factors, establish exposure thresholds, and make recommendations for a warning system that reaches the populations at risk, when they are most at risk.
Most importantly, any successful warning system would have to come with ways to mitigate heat exposure. Warning without action is not helpful. However, the recommended cooling strategies can’t be reliant upon air-conditioned environments and must recognize non-AC interventions, such as [electric fans](https://www.statnews.com/2019/08/05/fans-safe-during-heat-waves-study-suggests/), evaporative coolers, cool showers, or foot immersion. While it would be wonderful if every home was equipped with an air conditioner and people could afford to run it, this isn’t the reality for a great number of households — the very same households who are highest at risk for heat injury, illness, or death."

California Environmental Justice Coalition EHCRP grant proposal

The following is a summary of CEJC’s proposed grant application for the upcoming Extreme Heat and Community Resilience Program (EHCRP), a grant offered by the State of California’s Office of Planning and Research (OPR).
**FYI CEJC and Hyphae Design Lab (HDL) requires all materials from partners to be submitted to us by no later than May 1, 2024 in order to meet the May 7 deadline. ** 
About us
The California Environmental Justice Coalition (CEJC) represents over 90 grassroots environmental justice communities across the State. Each organization fights its community’s unique pollution challenges ranging from landfill regulation to unsafe school environments, water quality and even decommissioned nuclear weapons facilities. These disparate issues arise out of a shared legacy of redlining and its unfair distribution of pollution burdens on our disadvantaged communities. Our coalition is further united by the current climate crisis and its attendant effects on water, soil, and weather. In 2021, the National Weather Service classified  extreme heat events as the most lethal weather-related cause of death in the United States. Though recent attention on extreme heat is most welcome, the revelation was not news to us here in the Central Valley, the region targeted by the following large implementation project. As our coalition has been dealing with extreme heat long before it entered the national conversation, the Office of Planning and Research can be confident that our coalition is informed, organized, and prepared to respond on Day 1 of our proposed timeline. 
The Plan
In the following large implementation project, CEJC proposes a Community-led Microclimate Observatory Network (CoMON) in two counties of the Central Valley, with ambitions to expand to other counties in subsequent rounds of funding. Our endeavor combines data collection, emergency response software modeling, and nature-based design solutions in heat-stressed areas of urban Stockton and Fresno, as well as in outlying agricultural areas, all delivered, operated, and maintained by trusted community-based organizations. We first propose the installation and deployment of a sensor grid to monitor extreme heat. Next, we develop an extreme heat notification and outreach app to support communities during extreme heat events. Finally, we propose a series of pilot greening programs, the impact of which will be included in the sensor network’s ongoing data collection efforts as a measure of the Observatory’s practical use.

In Phase 3, after consulting community needs, CEJC and HDL create an extreme heat outreach tool in the form of a mobile app. The app is designed to alert the community in the event of an extreme heat event, proposing optimal routes to sensitive receptor sites such as schools, community centers, churches, and designated cooling centers. This will include metrics of the cooling potential of existing and proposed greenspace, shade, wind, and facilities, and will use network analytics to direct people to utilize paths and locations with the minimal possible heat stress.  The simulations behind the tool will use local weather forecasts to provide forcing inputs for simulations of heat stress that take advantage of 3 dimensional digital twins of the area. The digital twins are put through simulation process based on the Urban Multi-scale Environmental Predictor (UMEP) tool, which calculates meter-scale mean radiant temperature and wind-velocity, while taking into consideration the effects of buildings, vegetation, terrain and material properties on the wind flow,  sunlight and radiation. The results of the simulation are meter-scale maps of hot and cool zones, which are then fed into a network analysis algorithm that calculates the shortest path weighted by heat-stress between an origin and destination. These simulations are computationally intensive, and so will only be available in selected pilot neighborhoods at first. During this pilot period, other neighborhoods will still be able to use the tool, but with a less computationally intensive model, which uses heuristics based on Google-Streetview derived view indices, which calculate skyview index and shade cover by analysis of low-cost Google-Streetview imagery. In both cases, the routing recommendations are based on simulations or heuristics, which present an ethical concern if not backed-up by validation and ground truthing. We will use community deployed sensor networks, and remote sensing thermal imaging to validate and ground truth the simulations and heuristics. These methods of the modeling and validation cycle will then be subject to peer review in the scientific community to ensure validity and rigor. After a period of usage of the app, it will then be possible to discern its impact on health outcomes using hospital admissions data analyzed by the University of Merced affiliated team members.   <comment_start id=kix.iz3tnpbjuvhn>\[DF expands about possibilities for app\]<comment_end id=kix.iz3tnpbjuvhn>

3.  *Farmworker oases* in heat-affected areas of Central Valley agricultural sites. These are designed as either *stationary groves of trees and trellises*, or *mobile shade kits*.<comment_end id=kix.csrpvl92ih0q>
In Phase 6, CEJC, supported by HDL, and fellow co-applicants Tree Fresno and Little Manila Rising, implements the agreed-upon NBS in the designated urban and rural locations. 
In Phase 7, new data from the sensor network deployed in Phase 1 are collected to assess the effectiveness of CEJC’s Phase 5 greening interventions. This includes before-and after evaluation of the efficacy of the deployed interventions on both environmental conditions (such as radiant temperature, surface temperature, wind speed, humidity etc), and also human behavior interaction with the environment ie which paths people use, and where people gather, to assist in understanding the total impact of the interventions. Atr this stage the community health data on hospital admissions and health outcomes will be analyzed to determine the possibility of linking the interventions with changes in community health.
In Phase 8, the community-led Observatory, with the assistance of JPI, HSRI, and HDL, continues to monitor and evaluate extreme heat in the designated pilot areas, gathering new data that are to be directed towards future greening interventions and financed by future rounds of funding. The before and after evaluations of intervention efficacy can then be leveraged to raise additional funding for scaling the most effective interventions across the state. 
We imagine the Stockton-Fresno axis as the initial nodes in an evolving grid of cities and rural areas of the Central Valley. With time, as CoMON extends to the rest of California, it will be positioned to act as an extreme heat bellwether and task force for the whole State, equipped to identify hot spots and respond with the necessary outreach response infrastructure, data-driven NBS, and road diet interventions.

Subject: Report on online listening session hosted by State of California’s Extreme Heat & Community Resilience Program (EHCRP) \[attended 12/6/2023\]
 
The following is a brief report on a “listening session” hosted by Braden Kay, manager of the EHCRP program in the State of California’s Governor’s Office of Planning and Research.
<https://opr.ca.gov/climate/icarp/grants/extreme-heat-community-resilience.html>
<https://theclimatecenter.org/about/people/braden-kay/>
 
The EHCRP program is a sub-program of the broader Integrated Climate and Resiliency Program (ICARP), whose funding issues from the 2021 State Climate Budget
<https://opr.ca.gov/climate/icarp/grants/extreme-heat-community-resilience.html>
      <https://www.gov.ca.gov/2021/09/23/governor-newsom-signs-climate-action-bills-outlines-historic-15-billion-package-to-tackle-the-climate-crisis-and-protect-vulnerable-communities/>
 
Most of these listening sessions have been designed to get maximum input from the affected communities. The session that I attended, however, was directed to other people vying for the grant. The attendees mainly represented municipalities and counties, with some academics and only a few other consultants.    
 
In addition to laying out the underlying values of the grant (public health, climate resilience, social equity), Braden and the other facilitator shared some important practical details about the guidelines (link for guidelines:[ https://opr.ca.gov/climate/icarp/grants/docs/20231012-Public\_Review\_EHCRP\_Draft\_Guidelines.pdf](https://opr.ca.gov/climate/icarp/grants/docs/20231012-Public_Review_EHCRP_Draft_Guidelines.pdf))
^^ In this report, I cite page numbers from this document for reference.
 
The big take-aways for me were the following:
\-       The funds are allotted according to either “planning” or “implementation” proposals, but applications can blend both approaches.
\-       Note that the funds allotted to the implementation grants were much higher:
o   Small planning grants: $100k - $250k (pp. 21 ff.)
o   Large planning grants: $300k - $750k
o   Small implementation grants: $100k - $450k (pp. 25 ff.)
o   Large implementation grants: $500k - $5 million
^^ see in particular tracks C and D (pp. 28 ff.)
\-       They are allocating 35% of total budget to Planning and 65% to Implementation grants
\-       An unlimited number of applications can be submitted.
\-       Timeline for all grants is “up to 30 months.”
\-       All funds needs to be liquidated by Dec 31, 2029 (after that they disappear from the EHCRP budget).
\-       No limit on the amount of submissions per county: Braden only said that a quota needs to be met for each of the California Climate Adaptation Strategy (CCAS) *regions* (listed p. 51, appendix E) before going back and distributing the rest of the money. But that there could be, conceivably, regions that receive more help than others.
\-       Tribes do *not* need to be federally recognized to be considered as eligible partners.
\-       Deadline for both “Application pre-proposals” and for the full submissions are currently listed as “Spring 2024” (p. 10)
\-       “Expected notice of recommended awards” posted “Summer 2024” (p. 10)
\-       Expected Start and End dates for grants, as well as invoice deadline still TBD (p. 10)
