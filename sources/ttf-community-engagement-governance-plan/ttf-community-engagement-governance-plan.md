Definition of Target Community

The focus of the Green Spine for Health and Equity Project is the Southwestern Medical District (SWMD) and its adjacent neighborhood, Arlington Park, in Dallas Texas. This target community faces substantial and complex challenges in the areas of environmental, climate, economic, transportation, and public health. Therefore, those who live and work in this area bear disproportionately higher burdens related to exposure to pollution, hazard risks, heat exposure, and mobility challenges, which are directly and indirectly linked to numerous preventable health problems and increased health and economic disparities.

This EPA grant builds on the innovative Green Spine Project, led by the Texas Trees Foundation (TTF)—the grant’s lead applicant—in collaboration with the City of Dallas and the C-HEaRT team at Texas A\&M University. The Green Spine project involves significant investment to improve the major arterial (Harry Hines Boulevard) running through the SWMD to become a greener, cleaner, cooler, and safer thoroughfare designed for human and environmental health. This is an ambitious effort involving many years of planning, numerous stakeholders, and over $33 million to date of funding already committed by sponsors such as the North Central Texas Council of Governments, City of Dallas, Oncor, Lyda Hill Philanthropies, and the Meadows Foundation. However, its current scope is limited to improvements along Harry Hines Boulevard and excludes surrounding residential neighborhoods like Arlington Park. This EPA grant offers a critical opportunity to address these gaps by expanding the Green Spine Project to include adjacent disadvantaged communities. While long-term strategies are needed for the complex challenges facing our target community, there are critical short-term place-based strategies that can bring immediate and meaningful changes to this community that has suffered from sustained disinvestment and deteriorating infrastructure. This EPA grant targets those strategies, including the expansion of the Green Spine project which is being developed via an evidence-based process and the Arlington Park Green Connector project which will be co-developed and co-implemented with the Arlington Park residents.

The SWMD serves as a regional epicenter for healthcare, cutting-edge biomedical research, and nationally ranked education, located just 3.3 miles northwest of downtown Dallas.Three major hospital systems―Parkland Health and Hospital System, Children’s Health Dallas, and the UT Southwestern Medical Center (SMC) anchor the district. They employ over 42,000 people, enroll over 3,700 students, and attract more than 3.3 million patient visits each year.[1](#footnote1) Since 2009, there has been a 1.4-fold increase in the number of employees and patient visits to these three hospital systems and the upcoming addition of the Children’s Health Dallas Pediatric Campus will only energize this increase. This growth greatly impacts the surrounding communities as both an important employment center and a resource for social and health services. Over 3,200 current hospital employees live in the three zip codes surrounding the SWMD and demographics would suggest they are primarily low-wage workers in healthcare support roles.[2](#footnote2) Parkland serves as Dallas County’s public hospital that treats a large portion of disadvantaged populations and serves as the primary teaching hospital for the UT Southwestern Medical Center.

The SWMD includes eight census tracts, four of which are classified as disadvantaged, with a total population of 24,277. Of these, over 11,000 residents (45%) live within a 1-mile radius of the Harry Hines-Inwood intersection, including Arlington Park. Demographically, 69% are people of color, 30% are Hispanic, and 38% are low-income.[3](#footnote3)

Arlington Park (CT 10001) is designated as disadvantaged by the Justice40 CEJST, EPA EJScreen, and DOT ETC Explorer tools.3 Census data highlights multiple barriers faced by this community, including elevated poverty levels, linguistic isolation, low educational attainment, and a median household income 44% below the national average. Despite its proximity, Arlington Park is disconnected from the SWMD by the Harry Hines Corridor. The neighborhood experiences extreme economic and transportation challenges, with an unemployment rate of 18.7%, and 29.5% of households lacking access to a vehicle.[4](#footnote4) Transit options are minimal, reflected in the 0% public transportation usage reported for commuting.3 The neighborhood has low rates of walking and biking to work despite being transportation-burdened – likely a reflection of the poor infrastructure and hot weather conditions making these modes unsafe and/or unpleasant. Pedestrian and bicycle infrastructure is largely missing in this neighborhood, and existing infrastructure is in severely deteriorating conditions.

These modifiable barriers underscore the critical need for targeted interventions that not only improve connectivity and resilience of the environment but also enhance the health and quality of life of the residents. The focus on Arlington Park is intended to engage directly with the community to identify solutions to these challenges while improving environmental conditions. The result will be a community that is better connected to the resources, economic opportunities, and amenities located within the SWMD.

Project Partners

The Lead Applicant, Texas Trees Foundation (TTF), is a nonprofit organization focused on making spaces cooler, greener, and cleaner through urban forestry and encouraging others to do the same through educational programs that focus on the importance of building and protecting the urban forest today as a legacy for generations to come. TTF is leading the Green Spine project, an innovative urban design project in the SWMD. Mixed-methods research has informed the design plans for this transformative initiative, where the arterial road will be reimagined as “the Green Spine”, a greener, cleaner, cooler, and safer thoroughfare designed for human and environmental health.

Statutory Partner, Texas A\&M University (TAMU) opened as a land-grant institution in 1876 and later received sea-grant and space-grant designations. With these designations, TAMU serves as a leader in research and education regarding how we interact with and impact our environment. TAMU is a community of problem-solvers, scholars, and creators with a service mission whose purpose is to develop leaders of character dedicated to serving the greater good. TAMU is home to a newly established Center for Healthy, Equitable and Resilient Transportation in Texas (C-HEaRT), an interdisciplinary team of 21 experts from 10+ disciplines funded by Texas A\&M University, aimed at advancing the current status of knowledge about the impact of climate change, both direct and indirect, through modifiable environmental factors and by influencing social determinants of health. C-HEaRT targets vulnerable populations (e.g. older adults) and regions (e.g. heat islands) lacking climate-resilient infrastructure and being disproportionately affected by climate change, exacerbating health inequities. The C-HEaRT team brings both content and methodological expertise to this EPA project allowing immediate and meaningful benefits to the SWMD and the Arlington Park neighborhood. The study community serves as an ideal setting for this research given the high prevalence of health problems linked to climate and transportation, along with contextual factors like extreme weather and automobile dependency.

Serving as a hub for global scholars, communities, professionals, and policymakers, the goal of C-HEaRT is to offer versatile and immediately actionable solutions to diverse local communities to improve resilience, health, and equity related to transportation and climate change at various geographic and administrative levels. The EPA grant will enable TAMU to extend its innovative problem-solving to Arlington Park.

Arlington Park Neighborhood Association will be the connector and convening entity for the Arlington Park community, hosting meetings, co-design sessions, and opportunities for hands-on environmental actions/interventions. This group has been involved in TTF’s community engagement efforts to guide the Green Spine’s design for several years. They have, at times, shared reservations about potential indirect impacts of the Green Spine project to their neighborhood, but are largely enthusiastic about the improvements and the benefits it will afford the larger SWMD community The TTF/TAMU team understands the urgency to show results for these residents and hopes to create stronger connections to the Green Spine and the medical district for the benefit of the community.

1.  # Past Community Outreach and Engagement Conducted

Alarmed by studies identifying the Southwestern Medical District (SWMD) as a health-threatening extreme heat island, the Texas Trees Foundation (TTF), began convening a group of stakeholders to study the issue and look for solutions. To date, TTF has used diverse outreach methods to gather input and facilitate open dialogue from multiple stakeholders throughout both community engagement phases for the project. To ensure equity in outreach, TTF leveraged culturally relevant methods, including bilingual events, informal neighborhood gatherings, and targeted outreach to underrepresented populations. These methods were designed to reach residents who face barriers to participation in traditional planning processes.

Multiple focus groups, public visioning workshops, community pop-up events, and door to door canvassing were conducted to reach traditionally underrepresented communities. In the Fall of 2023, TTF launched Phase II Equitable Engagement to involve the community in the ongoing design process and inform them about the upcoming steps towards implementation. Stakeholders were asked for their desired improvements to the SWMD streetscape; such as adding street trees for shade and traffic protection or protected bicycle lanes and sidewalks, current and desired modes of travel around the SWMD, and to pinpoint areas on a map where they presently faced discomfort due to urban heat. Arlington Park community members were a key group involved in Phase II Engagement. TTF participated in multiple community meetings and events at the Arlington Park Recreation Center. Most notably, they hosted a joint event with Councilmember Jesse Moreno and various departments from the City of Dallas, such as Urban Forestry, Water Utilities, and Public Works, in Spring 2024. This event format effectively addressed community concerns raised at previous meetings that were outside the scope of the Green Spine project, while also demonstrating how the community's input was incorporated into the evolving design.

Through these engagement efforts, approximately 1,942 community participants engaged in the project process in Phase 1[5](#footnote5) and over 1,500 in Phase 2 between 2021 and 2024.[6](#footnote6) The input and feedback from these activities have fundamentally shaped the vision for the Harry Hines Green Spine transformation. Recognizing that there are many stakeholders in and surrounding the SWMD, TTF has worked to garner support from Green Spine project effort as evidenced by the letters of support found in Attachment C. These letters include nonprofits, philanthropic foundations, workforce development, health and education institutions, local citizens and government agencies.

In addition, 24 students from the Bachelor of Landscape Architecture program at Texas A\&M University collaborated with TTF and the City of Dallas to work with the SWMD community as part of their senior capstone design studio class in Spring of 2021. This has allowed diverse stakeholder groups in the target community, including the residents and tenants, to share their feedback and vision for the Green Spine project. While this was carried out as an academic exercise, this semester-long collaboration led to the development of initial design concepts of the Green Spine project reflecting the feedback from the community. Texas A\&M University has strong community service and outreach programs, fostering meaningful interactions between students and communities. Such academic-community collaborations not only enrich the students' educational experiences but also provide the SWMD community with opportunities to engage in the design process and co-develop innovative design solutions tailored to their specific needs and aspirations. This TTF-TAMU partnership proposed for this EPA grant exemplifies the potential for academic institutions to contribute meaningfully to societal progress through community-focused projects.

She made the initial connection between the SWMD Project and the nearby residential neighborhood of Arlington Park in need of greenspace investment. This initial idea evolved into a $300,000 investment funded by the U.S. Forest Service and managed by TTF into the community’s neighborhood park, Sleepy Hollow Park, including existing tree maintenance, planting 30 new trees, and installing nature-inspired playground equipment and educational signage in 2023.

1.  # Community Engagement Plan Implementation

Community Engagement will be carried out in two parallel tracks. TTF will be conducting an external strategy of general public awareness, continuing relationships begun during the design and development of the Green Spine project through its construction phase. The TAMU team will be pursuing an intensive community design approach with Arlington Park residents. In both cases, communications will be open and transparent, making all data and findings accessible following the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) principles for data integration and interoperability.

The TTF activities will include quarterly project updates and events through the following channels:

  - Bilingual flyers (English and Spanish)
  - E-blasts (mass emails)
  - Pop-up outreach events
  - Hard Hat tours (print and online)
  - Website updates
  - Press releases
  - Social media posts (Facebook, Instagram, and X\[Twitter\])

The Texas A\&M University (TAMU) team will implement the Green Connector Project in collaboration with the Arlington Park community, adopting a Community Co-design approach to promote environmental justice. This approach aims to empower communities to advocate for themselves using data, science, and evidence provided by the TAMU team and co-created with community members through the Resident Researcher program. The TAMU team will offer technical assistance, environmental monitoring, and design solutions to support Arlington Park and other impacted communities. By working alongside activists, researchers, government agencies, and local stakeholders, the Arlington Park community will gain the tools and resources necessary to achieve policy advancements in pollution reduction, climate resilience, and green infrastructure.. During the Green Spine engagement, Arlington Park residents expressed concerns about the lack of safe walking and biking infrastructure within their neighborhood and the inability to safely access the planned greening improvements in the nearby medical district, despite its proximity of only a few blocks. A preliminary connectivity study was conducted and will be further developed in partnership with resident researchers, who will serve as community ambassadors for the project. To prioritize critical locations for initial interventions, the team will conduct surveys, environmental audits, field observations, and hold meetings with the community. These efforts will ensure that solutions are data-driven, equitable, and aligned with the community’s most urgent needs..

The team brings their expertise in using advanced tools for hyperlocal mapping/modeling on environmental threats such as air, soil, and water contamination, as well as health risks like extreme heat. The evidence gathered through rich data and advanced modeling will drive hyperlocal design interventions, ensuring that solutions prioritized through the community engagement process are property informed by the data and precisely tailored to the needs of the community. The Arlington Park Neighborhood Association will utilize the adaptOS platform to design and simulate environmental interventions—such as urban greening, rerouting heavy vehicle traffic, or building digital tools to protect vulnerable populations. Insights from the community will form a crucial part of the evidence base, integrating local knowledge and lived experiences into the scientific analysis to ensure solutions are both effective and community-driven. This approach puts scientific tools and data directly in the hands of the community, shifting the balance of power from regulatory agencies to the people most affected by pollution.

During the Evidence-based Design process, residents will be actively engaged through community meetings and workshops to gather input, present findings, and co-design solutions. The team will develop accessible and engaging communication materials, including infographics and illustrative visualizations, to help community members understand complex data and participate in decision-making. The participatory design process will be designed to empower community members to contribute their knowledge and perspectives, fostering a sense of ownership and increasing the likelihood that projects will be supported and maintained in the long term. The process will include the following steps: (1) an initial kick-off meeting with the residents to provide an overview and timeline of the proposed activities; (2) a brainstorming session where the TAMU team will present the initial data and background information and participants are invited to freely share their initial ideas/visions without any constraints; (3) a workshop where the TAMU team will present initial ideas based on their feedback during the brainstorming session and participants will join roundtable discussions engaging in the critiques of presented ideas and in the initial co-design process; (4) the TAMU presents at least three design/intervention alternatives developed from the initial co-design process and residents evaluate those and further develop a consolidated option; (5) the entire project team collaborates with the residents to implement mock-ups (temporary installation) of the proposed intervention and gather feedback from the broader community; and (6) finalize the intervention plan incorporating the feedback and lessons learned from the mock-up exercise. In addition, throughout the project, quarterly open-house events will be organized for casual “meet & greet” type interactions between the project team members, the local residents, and other stakeholders.

In addition to the strong community engagement process throughout the project (e.g. the Resident Researcher program), significant capacity-building efforts will also be made. Those will include training workshops on environmental monitoring, mobility advocacy, and urban greening, empowering residents to take active roles in project planning and implementation. Members of the community will also be offered an opportunity to become certified Community Health Workers, “non-medical public health workers who connect communities to health care and social service providers,” a program available through the Texas Department of Health and Human Services (REF). This provides valuable professional training opportunities which can lead to job opportunities for the many residents who are unemployed in our target community. Their training includes communication skills, interpersonal skills, service coordination skills, capacity-building skills, advocacy skills, teaching skills, organizational skills, and knowledge base on specific health issues. In addition to facilitating residents’ participation in this existing program, we will also provide a "Tree Stewards" training program, where Arlington Park residents will learn essential urban forestry skills such as tree planting techniques, tree care and maintenance, and heat-resilient species selection. Experts from TAMU will train the residents, who will gain hands-on experience in various aspects of site selection and post-planting care during these workshops and field sessions. To ensure long-term success, we will train and certify at least 12 community members as Tree Stewards who can then train other residents, creating a sustainable knowledge transfer system within the neighborhood. As we anticipate that the community change intervention project in Arlington Park will involve tree planting, these Tree Stewards will also learn to use basic monitoring tools to track tree health and growth, collect data on temperature differences in planted versus unplanted areas, and document changes in microclimate conditions, neighborhood walkability, and physical activity patterns as the urban canopy develops.

To ensure the initial success and long-term sustainability of these capacity-building projects, we will help form support groups and organize regular check-in meetings through our Community Ambassadors. A dedicated project hotline will also be available to ensure continuous support for the participants, resident input, and transparency throughout the project lifecycle. We will also explore opportunities to work with local schools/students with these training programs.

The project focuses on clear and measurable goals to ensure its success. By Year 1, we plan to engage 100 residents through outreach efforts and certify 12 local residents as Community Health Workers or Tree stewards. We will continue these programs in Year 2 when the actual community interventions will be implemented and aim to certify another 12 residents. The cooling benefits of trees reduce temperatures by up to 15 degrees in Dallas.[7](#footnote7) The environmental interventions of the Green Spine and Green Connector aim to lower surface temperatures in the affected areas by at least 5°F and boost walking and biking rates in the community by 20% by Year 3. These are among the goals of this project designed to track tangible progress in improving mobility, enhancing climate resilience, and empowering residents.

## Clear Methods for Engagement and Transparency

The modeling process will be used to communicate the potential impacts of the project to the community in a visually engaging and understandable way. This fosters transparency and encourages community involvement in the design process.

While the modeling approach provides valuable technical insights, it's crucial to integrate it with community engagement to ensure that the proposed interventions align with the community's vision and needs. The sources emphasize the importance of involving the Arlington Park community throughout the project, from the initial planning stages to the final design development. This can be achieved through:

  - Community meetings and workshops: Providing opportunities for residents to share their concerns, priorities, and ideas for improving the neighborhood's environmental health.
  - Participatory mapping: Using social cartography, mapping tools and activities to allow residents to identify areas of concern, propose locations for interventions, and visualize the potential impacts of the project.

<!-- end list -->

  - Interactive mapping sessions will allow residents to identify priority areas for interventions, visualize proposed designs, and provide feedback in real time. These sessions will include both digital and paper-based tools to ensure accessibility
  - Participatory mapping sessions will allow residents to identify problem areas and suggest solutions, with findings integrated into design plans. These sessions will be paired with social cartography activities to capture emotional and cultural connections to specific places, ensuring that interventions align with the community’s values and priorities.

<!-- end list -->

  - Feedback mechanisms: Establishing channels for ongoing communication and feedback, allowing residents to provide input on the evolving design and ensure that their voices are heard.

The combination of robust data collection, sophisticated modeling techniques, and meaningful community engagement forms the foundation for the evidence-based design approach used in the Arlington Park project. This holistic approach aims to create a healthier, more sustainable, and resilient community that reflects the aspirations of its residents.

## Mitigating Barriers

As described in the methods above, the project team will work to meet community members where they are, providing information about the project and promoting open channels of communication for input and feedback. Direct engagement such as going door to door will also be important to reach community members who do not regularly attend community meetings and events. The C-HeaRT team will designate a contact person to address public concerns or questions regarding Green Spine activities and development; create and make available informational fact sheets about the program; use plain language when communicating both verbally and in writing; provide translations of all outreach documents into Spanish; make project documents readily accessible to the community, use a variety of media tools (paper copies, online, etc.), and hold public meetings at times and places in the community that afford the public a good chance of attending.

## To reach residents without internet access, the project team will provide printed materials, door-to-door outreach, and in-person workshops at accessible locations within the neighborhood.

## Government Involvement

The Green Spine project is the result of strong interdepartmental collaboration and support within the City of Dallas, Dallas County, North Central Texas Council of Governments, and TXDOT. The project has also been reviewed and supported by the Urban Design Peer Review Panel.[8](#footnote8) In addition to a MUA between the City and TTF to share responsibility for the Green Spine implementation, the City, County and NCTCOG have all committed funding to its construction. Because the SWMD is served by a TRE station and several DART stations, as well as numerous bus stops, DART and TRE have demonstrated substantial interest in this project and have organized staff teams dedicated to participating in this project as well. They believe there are mutually beneficial advantages gained from working together to improve transit services for District users and solve “last mile” challenges to improve access to and use of public transit. Eliminating the current physical barriers, the Green Spine Project will open safe, shaded, pleasant off-road routes between transit stops and destinations to all quadrants of the SWMD with a goal to reduce GHG emissions from commuters needing to drive. These established governmental relationships will help to leverage resources and/or approvals for any Arlington Park interventions in the public right of way.

## Project Partners, Roles, and Responsibilities

#### Lead Applicant:

As the lead applicant, the Texas Trees Foundation (TTF) will take responsibility for organizing formal monthly strategy sessions with Texas A\&M University (TAMU) and its C-HeaRT team, the Arlington Park Neighborhood Association, and the City of Dallas, as needed. These sessions will focus on reporting progress toward milestones, coordinating communications, and addressing any issues that arise..Additionally, the strategy sessions will include performance reviews of each partner's responsibilities, with outcomes shared publicly to ensure transparency and accountability.. TTF will also be responsible for grant management and reporting to EPA. Furthermore, TTF will ensure that TAMU complies with all programmatic and financial reporting requirements, meeting all terms and conditions of the grant...As part of the Green Spine vision and design development, TTF has already conducted extensive community outreach and will continue engaging with the community through the established channels outlined above.

#### Statutory Partner Organization

The Texas A\&M University (TAMU) team will lead community engagement, capacity building, and impact evaluations for this proposed project. Leveraging expertise from the newly established C-HEaRT: Center for Healthy, Equitable, and Resilient Transportation in Texas, the TAMU team will collaborate with the Arlington Park community to co-design and co-implement both temporary and permanent interventions. These efforts will include assessing pre- and post-intervention impacts, focusing on community and health outcomes within Arlington Park, as well as environmental outcomes across the broader Southwestern Medical District.

Dr. Chanam Lee will lead the team, which comprises members from Texas A\&M University, Texas Transportation Institute, Hyphae Design Lab, and the Arlington Park neighborhood (via the Resident Researcher program). Their efforts will be organized into three specialized sub-teams: (1) Community Engagement Team, (2) Intervention Design Team, and (3) Impact Assessment Team. The Texas A\&M team brings a wealth of experience and expertise across these domains.

#### Collaborative Entities

Arlington Park Neighborhood Association will lead communications with Arlington Park residents and guide the TAMU/C-HeaRT team in supporting the community and ensuring all voices are heard. The Texas Trees Foundation (TTF) and its partners, including Robin Williams of J. Williams Group, have built a strong, ongoing relationship with the Neighborhood Association through engagement activities for the Green Spine project and the successful Sleepy Hollow Park Revitalization project. This partnership, grounded in trust and effective communication, is demonstrated by the Letter of Collaboration attached as Attachment C of this application.

The City of Dallas and local elected officials, such as Councilmember Jesse Moreno, who represents both the Medical District and Arlington Park, will be regularly updated on project activities. The Dallas Department of Transportation will assist with necessary coordination related to public rights-of-way in the City of Dallas.

Southwestern Medical District, a major stakeholder representing the three large hospital institutions, Children’s Health, UT Southwestern Medical Center, and Parkland Health, will also be kept informed of all project activities and provide access to their extensive network for communications during the construction of the Green Spine.

Project directors from each partner organization will form a leadership team that meets monthly to oversee progress. Roles and responsibilities for sub-recipients and contractors will be clearly outlined in contracts, with adjustments discussed during these meetings or expedited through virtual platforms such as Zoom or Teams. Final decisions will be approved by TTF and, if necessary, the EPA. To ensure transparency, all activities involving the Arlington Park community will be shared with the Neighborhood Association, and team members will attend the association’s monthly Zoom meetings throughout the project duration.

## Processes for Replacing Collaborating Entity

The Texas Trees Foundation (TTF) will ensure that all collaborating entities comply with the terms and conditions of their subawards under the EPA Environmental and Climate Justice Community Change Grants Program. This includes adherence to all programmatic and financial reporting requirements. A key partner in this project is the Arlington Park Neighborhood Association, a small organization with limited capacity. To support their involvement, the project will build on existing relationships within the community and hire a dedicated community coordinator and local residents to lead engagement activities, alleviating the burden on the association and its volunteers.

Additionally, two similar neighborhoods, Love Field West and Oak Lawn, could be recruited to participate in community-based work if necessary. To ensure the project remains on track, Go/No-Go decision points and milestones have been integrated into the timeline, ensuring sufficient community participation to achieve the project’s goals, outputs, and outcomes. The decision to focus on Arlington Park for this grant reflects a commitment to delivering meaningful, in-depth results and generating lessons learned that can be customized and applied to other neighborhoods in the future.

If any changes to the project scope or community focus are required, TTF will seek prior approval from an authorized EPA official in accordance with 2 CFR 200.308(e)(6).
