PART 1. COMMUNITY DRIVEN INVESTMENTS FOR CHANGE 1.1 Community Overview

Community Description The Southwestern Medical District in Dallas, Texas is a vitally important regional healthcare, biomedical research and education hub located 3.3 miles northwest of downtown Dallas. Three major hospital systems anchor the area, which employ over 37,000 people, enroll over 3,600 students and attract over 3.3 million patient visits each year. (Southwest Medical District, 2021) The district and its surrounding neighborhoods within a two-mile radius are home to over 43,000 people. The neighboring population consists of 55% people of color and 36% low income, compared to 39% and 33% for the US, respectively. Community Challenges

Harry Hines Boulevard, built in the 1940s, was one of the first “highways” built in Texas and the first divided highway in Dallas. Originally designed as a rural freeway, over the past three decades the surrounding land use has changed to a higher-density urban environment. The grade- separated cloverleaf design of the intersection with Inwood Road – which cuts the medical district into quadrants – was designed solely for motor vehicles prohibiting the use of alternate forms of travel like walking and cycling. Furthermore, the interchange has constrained the development of a district-wide mobility system for non-motor vehicles with the SWMD plagued with sizable gaps in sidewalks, few formal bike lanes/paths, and no buffers between pedestrians and fast-moving vehicle traffic. As a result, most travel within the SWMD is currently made up of short trips in private cars, which increases GHG emissions and contributes to increased traffic congestion and the need for redundant parking.1 Approximately 82,000 vehicles travel through

Safety and Resilience Challenges in Arlington Park

The sources describe several problems impacting the safety and resilience of residents in the Arlington Park neighborhood of Dallas, Texas:

Lack of Pedestrian Infrastructure and Connectivity: Arlington Park has inadequate pedestrian infrastructure, with sidewalks that are discontinuous and frequently absent entirely, making it unsafe to walk or bike within the neighborhood. Residents have expressed safety concerns and a desire for better connectivity, including safer crossings and access to existing

This lack of connectivity isolates residents and limits their access to essential services, recreational opportunities, and employment centers.

Flooding: The neighborhood experiences significant flooding problems, which threaten residents' safety and property. Flooding events can disrupt daily life, damage homes, and lead to health risks. There is an urgent need to identify flood-prone areas and develop green infrastructure solutions to mitigate the impacts.

Heat Island Effect: Like the Southwestern Medical District adjacent, this area experiences a significant heat island effect, with temperatures in developed areas being considerably higher than surrounding, more natural areas. The lack of green spaces and tree cover in Arlington Park exacerbates this problem, creating a less comfortable and potentially dangerous environment for residents, particularly during hot summer months. Industrial Corridor Impacts: Arlington Park is located near industrial zones and faces challenges related to its proximity to these areas. Residents are likely exposed to higher levels of air and noise pollution from industrial activity and traffic, which can negatively impact their health and well-being. Additionally, the aesthetic appeal of the neighborhood may be diminished due to its proximity to industrial sites.

Limited Access to Green Space: The limited green space within Arlington Park further reduces its resilience to climate change impacts like heat and flooding. Access to green spaces offers residents a respite from urban stressors, provides opportunities for recreation and social interaction, and contributes to overall well-being. The proposal suggests that solutions should prioritize community engagement, evidence-based design principles, and a holistic approach that considers the interplay of these various challenges.

Community Vision

With the belief that health and healing should stretch beyond the footprint of the four walls of its remarkable healthcare institutions, the SWMD Urban Streetscape and Park Transformation Project was created through a collaborative, multi-disciplinary partnership model between TTF, the SWMD District anchor-hospital partners, local government entities and community resident and workforce. Its vision is to become a new model for what a medical district should be: a more holistic, green campus that unites the community, promotes health, healing, and safety, and acts as an ‘intersection of health and nature’. This vision includes:

• EQUITABLE ENGAGEMENT: Cultivating conversations where all people have a direct bridge to express their needs, goals, desires, and incorporate their voices.

• EVIDENCE-BASED DESIGN: Marrying the disciplines of design and science by modeling, optimizing, and validating design concepts to create a space that maximizes environmental and health benefits.

• GREEN INFRASTRUCTURE: Creating an urban, nature-based solution to mitigate the urban heat island effect, improve air quality, decrease stormwater runoff, and reduce carbon footprint.

• ONE HEALTH APPROACH: Designing a public space that heals, restores, and relaxes, provides opportunities for physical movement, and is an iconic, people-friendly place that meaningfully integrates nature. 1.2 Selected Strategies

Strategy Overview Greening 2 miles of Public Right-of-Way along Harry Hines Boulevard

In tandem with road infrastructure improvements by the City of Dallas, TTF will transform the public space with sidewalk improvements, on-street bikeways, native plantings, rain gardens as well as the planting of at least 4,000 trees able to reduce the heat island effects and reduce GHG emissions in the project area as well as other traffic risks such as pedestrian and bicyclist crashes.

Sensor Deployment; Measurement of Environmental and Health Indicators The sensor network will be central to the Evidence Based Design methodology, allowing for before and after evaluation of the effectiveness of the Greening and Active Transportation improvements and therefore a critical piece to the success of this project's mission. Results of this analysis will be shared in report form along with data insights showing results of microclimate and occupancy fixed sensor arrays.

Co-Design and Multimodal Connectivity Planning for Arlington Park A key component to reduce GHG’s and increase community wellbeing and social cohesions is to have a community that has active transportation which includes multimodal means of circulating through and between neighborhoods including walking, biking, and public transportation. Our Co-Design approach aims to empower Arlington Park residents to advocate for themselves using data, science, and evidence by providing technical assistance, environmental monitoring, and design solutions to help community members understand complex data and participate in decision-making,

Showcase Greening Projects for Arlington Park These limited shovel-ready interventions aim to demonstrate innovative and impactful greening solutions specifically tailored to the unique challenges of under-resourced neighborhoods like Arlington Park. The projects will focus on creating tangible improvements while also serving as a model for future greening efforts in similar communities. This approach aligns with the EPA's focus on community-driven solutions and creating replicable models.

Measuring and Evaluating Impact To demonstrate the effectiveness of the Showcase Greening Project, a robust monitoring and evaluation plan will include pre- and post-intervention data collection, Community surveys and publicly available reports and presentations that will share findings, the lessons learned and inspire other communities to adopt similar approaches. Empowering communities like the Arlington Park Neighborhood Association to collect their own data and advocate for change— backed by advanced modeling capabilities—can facilitate significant environmental justice achievements nationwide.

Texas Trees Foundation – Dallas TX ATTACHMENT E: COMMUNITY ENGAGEMENT AND COLLABORATIVE GOVERNANCE PLAN Definition of Target Community

The focus of the Green Spine for Health and Equity Project is the Southwestern Medical District (SWMD) and its adjacent neighborhood, Arlington Park, in Dallas Texas. This target community faces substantial and complex challenges in the areas of environmental, climate, economic, transportation, and public health. Therefore, those who live and work in this area bear disproportionately higher burdens related to exposure to pollution, hazard risks, heat exposure, and mobility challenges, which are directly and indirectly linked to numerous preventable health problems and increased health and economic disparities. This EPA grant builds onto the innovative ongoing effort, called the Green Spine project, led by the Texas Trees Foundation (TTF, lead applicant of this EPA grant) in partnership with the City of Dallas and the C-HEaRT team at Texas A\&M University. The Green Spine project involves significant investment to improve the major arterial road (Harry Hines Boulevard) running through the SWMD to become a greener, cleaner, cooler, and safer thoroughfare designed for human and environmental health. This is an ambitious effort involving many years of planning, numerous stakeholders, and over $33 million to date of funding already committed by sponsors such as the North Central Texas Council of Governments (NCTCG), City of Dallas, Dallas County, Oncor, Lyda Hill Philanthropies, and the Meadows Foundation and numerous philanthropic partners. However, the scope of this existing effort is limited to selected interventions along the Boulevard and does not include adjacent residential communities like Arlington Park. This EPA grant provides a unique opportunity to expand this work to address the critical needs of this disadvantaged residential community. While long-term strategies are needed for the complex challenges facing our target community, there are critical short-term place-based strategies that can bring immediate and meaningful changes to this community that has suffered from sustained disinvestment and deteriorating infrastructure. This EPA grant targets those strategies, including the expansion of the Green Spine project which is being developed via an evidence-based process to include the Green Connector project which will be co-developed and co-implemented with the Arlington Park residents.

The SWMD is a vitally important regional healthcare, cutting-edge biomedical research, and nationally ranked education hub located 3.3 miles northwest of downtown Dallas. Three major hospital systems―Parkland Health and Hospital System, Children’s Health Dallas, and the UT Southwestern Medical Center (SMC) anchor the district. They employ over 42,000 people, enroll over 3,700 students, and attract more than 3.3 million patient visits each year.1 Since 2009, there has been a 1.4-fold increase in the number of employees and patient visits to these three hospital systems and the upcoming addition of the Children’s Health Dallas Pediatric Campus will only energize this increase. This growth greatly impacts the surrounding communities as both an important employment center and a resource for social and health services. The team will provide technical assistance, environmental monitoring, and design solutions that empower impacted communities, especially Arlington Park. By collaborating with activists, researchers, government agencies, and local stakeholders, the community will have the means to achieve significant policy advancements in pollution reduction, climate resilience, and green infrastructure. In meetings during the Green Spine engagement, the Arlington Park community members communicated that it is unsafe to walk and bike within their own community as well as getting from their neighborhood to the planned medical district greening improvements, even though it is only a few blocks away. A preliminary connectivity study was conducted and will be further developed in partnership with the resident researchers who will serve as Community Ambassadors for this project. Surveys, environmental audits, field observations, and meetings with the community will be carried out to prioritize the most critical locations for initial interventions. The team brings their expertise in using advanced tools for hyperlocal mapping/modeling on environmental threats such as air, soil, and water contamination, as well as health risks like extreme heat. The evidence gathered through rich data and advanced modeling will drive hyperlocal design interventions, ensuring that solutions prioritized through the community engagement process are properly informed by the data and precisely tailored to the needs of the community. The Arlington Park Neighborhood Association will utilize the adaptOS platform to design and simulate environmental interventions—such as urban greening, rerouting heavy vehicle traffic, or building digital tools to protect vulnerable populations. Insights from the community will form a crucial part of the evidence base, integrating local knowledge and lived experiences into the scientific analysis to ensure solutions are both effective and community- driven. This approach puts scientific tools and data directly in the hands of the community, shifting the balance of power from regulatory agencies to the people most affected by pollution. During the evidence-based design process, residents will be actively engaged through community meetings and workshops to gather input, present findings, and co-design solutions. The team will develop accessible and engaging communication materials, including infographics and illustrative visualizations, to help community members understand complex data and participate in decision-making. Clear Methods for Engagement and Transparency The modeling process will be used to communicate the potential impacts of the project to the community in a visually engaging and understandable way to foster transparency and encourage community involvement in the entire process. While the modeling approach provides valuable technical insights, it's crucial to integrate it with community engagement to ensure that the proposed interventions align with the community's vision and needs. This method emphasizes the importance of Arlington Park community involvement throughout the project, from planning to the final design. This can be achieved through:

• Community meetings & workshops: Providing opportunities for residents to share their concerns, priorities, and ideas for improving the neighborhood's environmental health.

• Participatory mapping: Using social cartography, mapping tools and activities to allow residents to identify areas of concern, propose locations for interventions, and visualize the potential impacts of the project. 1. Mitigating Barriers

As described in the methods above, the project team will work to meet community members where they are, providing information about the project and promoting open channels of communication for input and feedback. Direct engagement (e.g., going door to door) will also be important to reach community members who do not regularly attend community meetings and events.
