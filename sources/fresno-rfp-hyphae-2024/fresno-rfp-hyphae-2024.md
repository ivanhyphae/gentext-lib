C. OVERVIEW

Fresno County faces a growing public health crisis from extreme heat, with 64 days in 2024 exceeding 100°F. On October 3, 2024, the county recorded a scorching 105°F—the highest October temperature in Fresno's history. Climate projections indicate this trend will worsen, with annual average maximum temperatures rising by 5.3°F to 8.7°F by the end of the century. The number of “extreme heat days,” defined as those surpassing 94.3°F, is expected to increase from the historical average of five per year to as many as 66 under high emissions scenarios.

These rising temperatures exacerbate existing disparities. Over 55% of Fresno County census tracts rank above the 75th percentile on CalEnviroScreen 4.0, reflecting burdens such as poverty, air pollution, and health inequities like asthma and cardiovascular diseases. Many residents in low-income communities lack access to cooling resources, such as air conditioning, shaded pedestrian routes, or energy-efficient housing, leaving them disproportionately exposed to heat-related illnesses.

However, in Fresno County census tracts are typically spatially large enough that their attributes tell us little about the relationship between built environment parameters such as shade from trees, and heat stress impacts. Disadvantaged communities, particularly in South Fresno, suffer from shade inequity; research using high resolution geospatial LiDAR data and metrics like Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT) reveals that low-income neighborhoods experience MRT levels as high as 122.7°F, compared to 115.7°F in wealthier areas1. This disparity intensifies health risks for agricultural workers, who endure prolonged exposure to extreme heat with minimal shade.

To address these inequities, our Fresno County Extreme Heat Analysis will combine advanced heat vulnerability assessment with robust community engagement. Using high resolution data from ERA5 satellite, ECOSTRESS satellite imagery, Google StreetView, aerial surveys, LiDAR and other geospatial datasets, we will analyze both urban and rural heat island effects, tree canopy coverage, and socioeconomic indicators to identify vulnerable hotspots. These areas of interest will then be taken back to the Working Group and wider community to select the five Project Areas designated for detailed planning and analysis as specified by the RFP.

To ensure a comprehensive understanding of heat as a lived experience, and actual heat stress, we also employ advanced metrics such as the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI), which incorporate factors like wind flow, radiant temperature, and land use in addition to traditional heat and humidity indicators. This approach captures the multifaceted nature of thermal stress, providing a nuanced view of how environmental conditions affect daily life. By integrating these metrics, our analysis moves beyond static temperature readings to develop interventions that reflect the complexity of local microclimates and the lived realities of Fresno County's most vulnerable populations.

Our methodology integrates technical rigor with community insights through multilingual outreach, interactive workshops, and iterative feedback. This process ensures localized strategies for increasing tree canopy and shade access are informed by those most affected. Working collaboratively with Fresno COG, local jurisdictions, and community organizations, our project will deliver actionable, evidence based plans to mitigate extreme heat impacts and promote environmental justice. Task 3: Extreme Heat Vulnerability Analysis

Approach

The Heat Vulnerability Assessment employs a highly detailed and community-focused approach to understand and address the complexities of extreme heat exposure. Traditional heat mapping tools often lack the resolution necessary for actionable, community-level interventions, limiting their utility in rural and mixed-use environments such as Fresno County. To overcome these limitations, our analysis integrates advanced data sources, modeling techniques, and iterative community engagement to deliver unprecedented precision in identifying heat-vulnerable areas and populations. By synthesizing

meteorological data and land use classifications with innovative population disaggregation methods and community insights, the assessment provides a detailed understanding of how heat is distributed and experienced across diverse local contexts.

Our core strategy is to to bring together data from multiple sources and resolutions to create ultra-high resolution insights. We also leverage this process for both environmental and social variables. The workflow development, software automation and hardware server infrastructure for this work is funded by our NSF SBIR and other grants.

This advanced methodology moves beyond surface and air heat mapping by incorporating thermal comfort variables such as wind flow, humidity, and radiant temperature into neighborhood-specific simulations. These models are further enhanced through continuous engagement with community stakeholders, ensuring the integration of hyperlocal knowledge about demographic patterns and daily behaviors. The analysis also employs state-of-the-art tools such as digital twins for simulations that model the effects of sun, shade, and heat reradiation, creating a three-dimensional understanding of the built environment. By integrating technical rigor with community relevance, our dual emphasis ensures the delivery of practical, targeted solutions tailored to Fresno County’s unique challenges.

Methodology for Extreme Heat Vulnerability Analysis

Resolution and Scope

Because lower spatial resolution analysis requires less time and computational resources than high resolution per unit of area analyzed, our approach is to conduct lower resolution analyses over larger areas, and then to use those analyses to target smaller areas, where higher resolution analyses are performed at an economical scale. For this purpose we will describe three “levels” of analysis: a) low resolution, which can be done at the whole county scale and can cover many years of historical or future projections, b) high resolution analyses, which can cover just the 5 target project areas, covering each hour of a few days at different times of year, and c) medium resolution data, which will fall somewhere between high and low resolution in its scope.

Low resolution thermal comfort data

Broadly available high temporal resolution but low spatial resolution data from earth and climate modeling data is in the 1-30km resolution. This data is useful because it is specific to health heat risk and thermal comfort variables but is too broad for targeted action. Examples include ERA5-Heat from Europe, which is global scale and NWS Heatrisk which is CONUS scale.

Although low resolution spatially, this data is modeled daily or weekly and across a wide temporal scale, ERA5 for example goes back 40± years and also has data for future scenarios based on climate change models. This can provide a reliable mosaic of large swaths of area that have and will regularly have heat stress into the future.

While we consider this data low resolution it is actually far more advanced, detailed, relevant and helpful than current county and census derived maps like CHAT which are currently used by most counties, for things like heat warning, because they are topographically , meteorologically and environmentally driven, rather than delineated by political boundaries.

12

They aren't however high enough resolution for more intervention based work which needs to happen at the town, neighborhood, block and property level scale. We are an innovation driven firm and will continue to incorporate new metrics and models and resolutions of data. New metrics Thermal Stress Duration (TSD) was just published in October 2024 and expands on UTCI to look at how long over the days and days people are exposed and have greater risk. 2

Medium resolution heat data

We then gain further insights with medium resolution (1km down to 30m) satellite derived heat data like ecostress, landsat, which are heat specific, but only include surface or air temperature, not thermal comfort and therefore don't tell the true health heat risk. These maps can be used to identify more specific hotspots at the neighborhood and block scale that will also likely have heat stress.

This data should be used with some caution because of the spatial resolution and surface characteristic quality of the data. For example, building roofs might make it hot, but people aren't normally on the roof, they are next to more shaded and cooler building walls or under trees, where a surface measurement of the top of the crown, might be quite hot, but that's because it's blocking the sun from the cooler and understory which is not being sensed by the satellite.

But if these limitations are known, considered and not ultimately used for final intervention scale analysis, this dataset is really useful because it is relatively high temporal resolution, often gathered weekly or monthly and because it can be used to estimate unmeasured variables like air temperature which can then be fed into our higher resolution models discussed later.

Other medium resolution data

Local climate zones (LCZ) data is calculated at 30 meter resolution for a single time period and reflects 17 categories of local topology concerning feature types (buildings and vegetation) heights (tall, medium, short, none) and spacing (dense, medium, sparse). This can be used to stratify heat data (such as surface temperature) to prioritize where interventions are most likely to be plausible and effective. Similarly we use 100 meter dasymetric population density data and Cropscape crop mapping to estimate where people live or are likely to be working outdoors to adjust risk factors for heat stress estimates at the 30 meter scale.

Hyper-local high resolution environmental modeling.

After choosing neighborhoods or corridors to target interventions, discussed in task description, we come back to further modeling work where we then utilize our own novel high resolution modeling to develop meter scale simulations. This includes shade and UTCI models at 1-10 meter resolution.

This work combines the development of 3d digital twins of trees, buildings, terrain and other features from lidar data and simulation modeling of UTCI variables including air temperature, humidity and radiant temperature. These are novel datasets that we generate from our software pipeline.

To conduct a simulation of 1-meter scale mean radiant temperature in the 5 target project areas, we will take aerial LIDAR data from the USGS 3D Elevation program gathered from 2019-2023, and use it to generate 3D models of the target areas, including building heights, and canopy leaf area index. We will then find selected historical days in which there was minimal cloud cover to simulate, which correspond to

Social and infrastructural vulnerability

Before doing further environmental mapping and modeling, it is generally better to use this data in combination with social vulnerability data to further prioritize neighborhood and town sized hotspot areas to target for interventions because heat vulnerability is both a physical and social variable.

Census Data

We then conduct social and infrastructure vulnerability assessments. This is normally unfortunately done with census data because that's all that exists to identify things like age, occupation, race and income level of the population. Census, census tract and census block group data can be helpful in larger dense cities, but is extremely challenging in low population dense rural areas, providing a disservice to some of the most impacted rural farming communities. For example the City of Fresno may have 40 census tracks then a giant rural area is one, which doesn't allow us to spatially target a population. We will use census data to begin, but we then also disaggregate the census data to provide a more nuanced estimation of these aspects of heat-risk needs.

Census Disaggregation

We utilize new methods developed by health and emergency response researchers to gain further granularity into the social fabric of a community. One method (Popgrid) is from Harvard researchers who utilized census data in combination with building footprint and land use data to "estimate" the location of people to 100 meter resolution. The method basically says there's x thousand people in this census tract,

● NSF SBIR Phase I and II Grants: AdaptOS Development (2020–Present)

Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform, developed with funding from NSF SBIR Phase I and II grants, enables community-based organizations (CBOs) and municipal partners to address environmental risks such as extreme heat, air pollution, and flooding with precision and scalability. This platform bridges gaps in traditional environmental planning by combining hyperlocal data collection, modeling, and design to ensure interventions are both cost-effective and impactful.

The AdaptOS framework is iterative, cycling through measurement, simulation, implementation, and assessment. Modeling is at the core of this process, serving two essential functions. First, it optimizes sensor placement by predicting pollutant dispersion patterns, ensuring accurate monitoring and data collection. Second, it allows communities to simulate the effects of proposed interventions—such as urban greening, truck rerouting, and shade structures—before implementation, providing clarity on which solutions will yield the greatest impact and benefit-to-cost ratio.

This approach addresses a persistent challenge faced by many environmental initiatives, as exemplified by Hyphae’s work on the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan (WOCAP). While WOCAP mandated significant regulatory concessions and outlined 89 pollution mitigation strategies, decision-makers lacked the empirical tools to determine which interventions would have the most significant impact at the hyperlocal level. AdaptOS fills this gap, demonstrating how interventions like tree canopy, vegetation, and traffic patterns can be configured to protect environmental health effectively.

By combining rigorous modeling with actionable design strategies, AdaptOS transforms environmental data into solutions tailored to community needs, ensuring measurable public health and environmental benefits. Hyphae has successfully deployed this platform in projects across Oakland, Stockton, and Dallas, delivering scalable frameworks that can guide future environmental resilience efforts.

● The Green Heart Project: Pioneering Urban Greening for Public Health (2018 – Present)

The Green Heart Project began in 2018 as a collaborative effort between the University of Louisville, The Nature Conservancy, and multiple local and national partners, including Hyphae Design Lab. With funding from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and support from The Nature Conservancy, the Green Heart Project is the first clinical trial to study the effects of urban greening on air quality and its direct impact on cardiovascular and systemic health.

Key aspects of the project included planting over 8,000 mature evergreen trees and shrubs across a designated four-square-mile area in Louisville, Kentucky. This effort targeted neighborhoods with historically low tree canopy coverage and high exposure to environmental stressors, including air pollution and urban heat.

The project implemented a quasi-experimental design:

○ Baseline (2018-2019): Collection of extensive health and environmental data from 745 residents, including biomarker analyses (e.g., high-sensitivity C-reactive protein or hsCRP), air quality measurements, and noise pollution monitoring.

○ Intervention (2019-2020): Large-scale greening, focusing on high-traffic corridors and residential zones.

○ Post-intervention Assessment (2020-Present): Continued monitoring of participants to evaluate health improvements linked to reduced systemic inflammation and improved air quality.

Impact:

○ Participants in neighborhoods receiving greenery saw 13–20% lower levels of hsCRP, corresponding to a 10–15% reduction in risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes.

○ Significant reductions in particulate matter (PM2.5) levels and urban noise pollution were documented, highlighting the multi-dimensional benefits of increased urban greenery.
