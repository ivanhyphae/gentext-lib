g. Project Summary:

Abstract Objectives of the Study: The primary objective of the AirSense project is to enhance urban air quality monitoring, modeling, and management through a comprehensive approach that integrates advanced sensor data processing, Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) modeling, and community engagement. We aim to test two key hypotheses: (1) the integration of air quality data from low-cost sensor networks with regulatory monitor data can enhance the verification and validation of CFD models of urban air quality, and (2) verified and validated CFD model results can provide compelling evidence for policymakers to take action on air quality issues in underserved communities. Experimental Approach: The proposed project will be implemented through three main tasks: (1) Identification of Areas of Interest (AOIs) and Relevant Monitoring Networks, which involves selecting neighborhoods with high pollution levels and deploying sensor networks, (2) Air Pollution Sensor Data Integration, Processing, and Analysis, which includes applying advanced techniques like Kalman filtering to integrate and enhance data from various sources, and (3) CFD Modeling: Simulation, Validation & Verification, and Uncertainty Quantification, which focuses on developing and validating high-resolution CFD models to simulate pollutant dispersion in urban environments. Throughout these tasks, we will engage with community- based organizations (CBOs) to incorporate local knowledge and ensure the project's relevance and effectiveness. Expected Results: The expected outcomes of the AirSense project include improved spatial and temporal resolution of air quality data, accurate pollution dispersion models, and data-driven insights for informed policy decisions. By integrating low-cost sensor data with regulatory data and validating CFD models, we anticipate a significant improvement in the precision of air quality predictions. This will empower underserved communities with robust evidence to advocate for targeted interventions, thereby addressing the research needs identified in the solicitation. Ultimately, the project aims to enhance risk assessment and risk management of urban air pollution, leading to better health outcomes and environmental justice for vulnerable populations. h. a. Research Plan

1\. Introduction

Motivation - Air quality monitoring is crucial for protecting public health, particularly in underserved urban areas where pollution levels are often higher and the impacts more severe. Poor air quality is linked to a range of health problems, including respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, and disproportionately affects vulnerable populations such as children, the elderly, and those with pre-existing health conditions. Research has shown that pollutants like particulate matter (PM) and ozone can exacerbate conditions such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and heart disease, leading to increased hospital admissions and even premature death (US EPA, 2024; American Lung Association, 2024; NIEHS, 2024).

Underserved communities often face a dual burden: they are more likely to be located near pollution sources such as highways, industrial areas, and power plants, and they often lack the resources and political influence to effectively advocate for cleaner air. This can lead to persistent health disparities and lower quality of life for residents. Therefore, enhancing air quality monitoring in these communities is not only a public health priority but also a matter of environmental justice.

To address these challenges, there is a pressing need for more comprehensive air quality monitoring systems that leverage both advanced technology and community engagement. By integrating existing low-cost sensor data with regulatory sensor data, along with Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) advanced modeling techniques (Buccolieri et al., 2011; Pantusheca, 2022; San Jose, 2021), it is possible to create high-resolution (1 meter scale) maps of estimated air pollution under existing and proposed built-environment scenarios. These maps can be used for pollution risk assessment and for proposing high-performance risk mitigation strategies. This approach empowers communities with the information they need to advocate for better air quality and health outcomes.

Objectives - The AirSense project brings together air pollution and fluid dynamics experts from University of South Florida (USF) and scientists and engineers from Hyphae Design Laboratory engineering firm and various community-based organizations (CBOs) to enhance urban air quality monitoring, modeling and management through a multi-faceted approach that combines advanced processing of existing sensor data, CFD modeling and community engagement. Specifically, the project seeks to:

1\. Utilize Sensor Networks: Utilize existing community-led low-cost air quality sensor networks and integrate their data with regulatory air quality data. This will provide a detailed, real-time picture of air quality across multiple underserved urban neighborhoods in the State of California. Cooperation with these neighborhoods will be facilitated through Hyphae’s extensive CBO network throughout the state. 2. Develop Advanced Models: Create high-resolution CFD models to simulate the dispersion of air pollutants in urban environments. These models will be verified and validated and refined using combined data from both low-cost sensors and regulatory monitoring stations. The project will develop best practices and guidelines for integrating CFD models into decision-making processes. 3. Engage Communities: While community members will not be the primary source for data gathering, they will play a critical role in the project through engagement activities. This includes participating in community forums to discuss air quality issues, providing feedback on data interpretations and model output, and using project findings for advocacy.

2\. Background Limitations of air quality monitoring systems - To inform community and urban planning decisions, reliable air quality information is needed at high spatial and temporal resolutions (hundreds of meters to neighborhood scale; hourly or more frequently) over extended periods (months to years). Although regulatory air monitoring networks provide long-term, high-quality air pollutant data for governmental compliance, they have limitations for neighborhood and near- source community uses. Regulatory reference networks, due to their cost, space, and personnel requirements, have limited spatial resolution and do not capture variations at neighborhood or smaller spatial scales, even for commonly measured pollutants such as fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and other criteria pollutants (Snyder et al., 2013). For pollutants of specific interest near sources impacting communities, such as black carbon (BC), ultrafine particles (UFP), and hazardous air pollutants (HAPs, such as benzene or toxic metal compounds), the network is even sparser. Short-term intensive research campaigns using specialized instruments or passive sampling have traditionally been employed to measure air quality in neighborhoods or near sources (Steinle et al., 2015). However, the short-term nature of these campaigns limits their utility for long-term decision-making.

Recent advances in small sensor-based air quality measurement devices (air sensors, e.g., Purple Air II (PurpleAir, 2023); microAeth (AethLabs, 2024)), Aclima (Aclima, 2024), and satellite data (e.g., TEMPO (NASA, 2024)) offer alternative measures of air quality that can supplement traditional monitoring, supporting both community decision-making and the evaluation of models diagnosing pollution patterns or predicting urban planning impacts (Morawska et al., 2018). Due to their accessibility, air sensors are increasingly used by individuals, community groups, and governmental planning organizations not traditionally involved in air quality management (Shatas & Hubbell, 2022). This project aims to utilize these crowd-sourced networks, offering a valuable opportunity to provide communities with reliable, long-term air quality data.

Challenges in CFD model validation - Traditional CFD model validation relies on high- quality data from regulatory monitoring networks, which are often sparse and not representative of localized air quality conditions, especially in underserved communities. To address this, our project aims to validate CFD models using data from low-cost sensor networks. These networks, while more readily available and capable of providing high spatial and temporal resolution data, present challenges in terms of data quality and accuracy.

We aim to develop community-specific validated CFD modeling. This approach ensures that the models are directly relevant and appealing to the community and local policymakers. However, special care must be taken with low-cost sensor data due to potential issues with accuracy. Techniques such as Kalman filtering can enhance the reliability and accuracy of data collected from low-cost sensors by filtering out noise and recovering missing information (Grewal and Andrews, 2014).

To support communities in utilizing low-cost sensor data effectively, we plan to provide guidelines including best practices for sensor placement, calibration, data correction, and quality control checks. For example, collocating low-cost sensors with regulatory monitors can help quantify accuracy, and periodic maintenance and data review are essential for ensuring reliable data collection (EPA, 2023; JRC, 2022).

3

By implementing these practices, we can improve the quality of data used for CFD model validation, making the results more robust and trustworthy. This approach not only empowers communities with actionable air quality information but also enhances the credibility and impact of CFD models in local decision-making processes.

Challenges in stakeholder engagement in CFD modeling - Communication and education about the value and outcomes of CFD modeling depend on clear and consistent presentation standards and an accessible framework for interpreting and weighting conclusions suggested by model results. Different stakeholders respond in diverse ways based on their frames of reference, expertise, and agendas. For example, it has been the experience of Hyphae Design Laboratory members that scientifically trained professionals typically show skepticism, while community activists may accept computed results at face value. A transparent framework for presenting validated results in a compelling and legible way would help bridge this gap and facilitate productive conversations between impacted public members and policy players.

This proposal includes both the publication and review of mathematical and observational support for data and CFD analyses protocols, as well as the development of interactive visualization tools. These tools will empower moderately resourced communities to use open- source communication media to inform and influence air quality decision-making. Hyphae has prototyped web-based interactive and narrative tools for sharing local insights based on computer analyses and continues to develop these tools with in-house programming staff.

Task 2: Air pollution sensor data integration: Processing and analysis We will utilize existing GIS (Geographic Information System) and PostgreSQL (and open- source relational database management system) infrastructure developed by Hyphae to overlay various datasets, including satellite data. This approach will allow insights into any significant convergence of readings. By integrating sensor air quality data with regulatory air quality data, a comprehensive understanding of air quality will be achieved.

Available air quality data from different sources have distinct spatial and temporal support structures and uncertainties. For instance, measurement sites often provide point measurements irregularly located in space at regular intervals in time, with reference networks providing high- quality (low uncertainty) data and air sensor networks providing data with higher uncertainty. Conversely, models and satellites typically provide data averaged over spatially and temporally regular or irregular space-time grids of different resolutions, with uncertainties often estimated. Additionally, different datasets measure various pollutants or other air quality-related entities, such as temperature and relative humidity, which cannot be directly compared. However, by leveraging the well-known Kalman filtering technique (Grewal and Andrews, 2014) as well as other data processing approaches, we can utilize the uncertainty structures, such as covariances in the spatial, temporal, and pollutant spaces, to synergistically combine these diverse types of data. This approach allows for the integration of multiple datasets into a unified state estimate, improving the overall accuracy and reliability of air quality assessments.

In addition to using the processed air pollution data for validating planned CFD simulations, we will employ this data to develop artificial neural network (ANN) models of air quality at various locations within the selected AOIs. These models will not only help corroborate CFD model predictions but also provide communities with a rapid prediction tool, as CFD simulations are computationally intensive. However, like other machine learning (ML) models, the ANN model is considered a 'black box' and offers limited insight into the physical processes and interaction mechanisms related to pollutant dispersion within neighborhood settings. This is where the strength of CFD lies.

Subtask 2.1: Kalman filtering Well-established Kalman filtering techniques (Grewal and Andrews, 2014) will be employed to combine data sources, allowing measured data on different pollutants and with distinct space- time locations to inform each other. Kalman filtering significantly enhances the reliability and accuracy of data collected from low-cost sensors by filtering out noise and recovering missing information. This process transforms unreliable and noisy sensor data into more accurate and use- ful information.

Low-cost sensors often produce data with significant measurement noise due to their lower quality and sensitivity, such as calibration errors or environmental interference. Kalman filtering addresses this issue through a series of prediction and update steps. The filter begins by predicting the next state of the system using the current state estimate and a model of the system dynamics. This prediction includes both the state and its uncertainty:

The regular Kalman filter described above assumes that the system dynamics and measurement processes are both linear. It is designed to provide optimal estimates for linear systems but strug- gles to handle nonlinear dynamics effectively. To handle nonlinear system dynamics, variants like the extended Kalman filter have been developed by linearizing the system dynamics and measure- ment processes around the current state estimate. For example, Metia et al. (2021) developed an extended Fractional-Order Kalman filter and applied it with success to air pollution measurements. The air-quality datasets obtained for the AOIs to be identified in Task 1 may require diverse methods of processing. Thus, if necessary, other methods beyond Kalman filtering will be explored as part of this subtask. For example, deep learning models, including convolutional neural net- works (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) like Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks.

Subtask 2.2: Machine learning model: A promising technique for supplementing CFD predictions of air quality at the urban neighborhood scale is artificial neural network (ANN) modeling, a subset of machine leaning or ML. In Subtask 2.2, we will develop and apply an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) models to predict air quality at the locations (or sites) of high pollutant concentrations identified in Task 1. This approach is inspired by the successful application of ANNs in air pollution prediction demonstrated by Wai and Yu (2023) with respect to both sensor data and CFD analysis. Furthermore, as emphasized in the review by Yadav et al. (2024), ANN techniques forecast air contaminants more precisely than traditional methods such as statistical regression models and interpolation methods. These traditional methods, while useful,

8

can be limited by their assumptions. For example, statistical regression models often assume linearity between variables and interpolation methods may not capture complex pollutant dispersion patterns effectively (Turias et al., 2007).

The ANN model of Wai and Yu (2023) was formulated to predict the neighborhood-scale PM10 dispersion within the urban canopy layer of a study site in Hong Kong. The model mimics natural neurons in animal brains, with details discussed in prior literature by Haykin (1999). Specifically, a feed-forward neural network was used, which has been successfully adopted in other pollution transport studies by Azid et al. (2013).

Following Wai and Yu (2003), the training process will involve using the efficient Levenberg– Marquardt algorithm (Nocedal and Wright, 2006). The model will be constructed using MATLAB software (The MathWorks, USA). Historical data, including input variables from multiple monitoring sites near and within the AOIs, will be used to train the ANN. This comprehensive dataset will capture a wide range of environmental conditions and pollution levels, allowing the model to generalize and make accurate predictions for new, unsampled sites. When predicting air quality for a particular site that does not have a sensor, the ANN will use input data from nearby sites, including real-time wind speed, wind direction, temperature, humidity, and spatial coordinates. By leveraging the learned relationships and spatial information, the ANN will forecast pollutant concentrations accurately for the unsampled sites.

The model’s predictions will be validated against actual sensor data and CFD model outputs, ensuring their reliability. Task 3: CFD modeling CFD has proven invaluable for analyzing dispersion of pollutants in urban flows (Blocken et al., 2013; Tominaga and Stathopoulos, 2016; Di Sabatino et al., 2013; Pantusheca et al., 2022); San Jose et al., 2021; Buccolieri et al., 2011). Meso-scale CFD of urban areas take into account the urban heat island effect, regional wind patterns, and pollutant dispersion across broad urban regions. These simulations typically derive boundary conditions from mesoscale meteorological models (Lucas et al., 2016). Micro-scale CFD simulations of urban areas delve into the detailed flow dynamics around individual buildings, streets, and trees. These simulations are crucial for understanding local variations in pollutant dispersion and the impacts of urban design features such as buildings, street canyons and vegetation (Blocken et al., 2016). In the proposed research, we aim to address urban flows at the meter to sub-meter scales resolving interactions between airflow and urban features including buildings, trees and vehicles. Trees play a significant role in reducing pollution by acting as physical barriers and through deposition processes, while vehicle- induced turbulence can enhance pollutant dispersion, creating complex flow patterns that require detailed analysis.

Subtask 3.1: Determination of digital twin and traffic and meteorological data The CFD modeling process requires inputs of a domain model (or “digital twin”), meteorological data, and traffic data. The digital twin defines the structure of the built urban environment and may represent existing conditions, historical conditions, or a proposed design condition. By comparing different digital twins while keeping other inputs constant, we can use CFD to estimate the effects of design decisions on output metrics related to air pollution.

We will develop digital twins of selected AOIs using various data inputs, including GIS data, LiDAR data, land cover classifications, and tree surveys. This process will output a three- dimensional model representing terrain, buildings, and vegetation. Depending on the input data types and desired simulation method, the output model may be a set of triangular meshes, voxels, or rasters. Terrain models will be developed from digital elevation models (DEMs) for existing conditions and classified with land cover data. Buildings will be modeled using publicly available building footprint polygons and aerial LiDAR-derived height information. Vegetation will be modeled from voxelization of classified LiDAR data. These inputs can be modified using standard design tools for proposed conditions. LiDAR datasets from publicly available aerial scans will be segmented using standardized procedures to yield classified point clouds for terrain, structures, and tree canopy. For simulated comparison of vegetation scenarios, prototypical trees will be included, varying in intra-canopy leaf area density (LAD) and leaf types (needles vs. broadleaf).

Weather data will be filtered to determine the most common and significant wind configurations to implement. Historical weather data will be extracted from sources like the Iowa Environmental Mesonet (IEM) to generalize and bin wind directions over selected periods, accounting for diurnal variations over seasons of interest. It is important to account for these seasonal variations in wind patterns, as wind directions and intensities can vary throughout the year due to changes in weather patterns. Diurnal variations in wind patterns should also play an important factor in determining how well CFD predicts variations in pollutant concentration over times on the order of hours, as discussed earlier in the description of Task 3.

Traffic data will be collected through mobility studies, providing vehicle counts at several intersections throughout the community. These counts will be used to profile traffic quantities over time and interpolate hourly vehicle counts from a few times of the day using a plausible but theoretical curve for traffic ebbs and flows. Fleet percentages for fractions of light, medium, and heavy-duty vehicles will be used to weight emissions factors to correspond to the location. Vehicles produce tailpipe emissions, brake and tire dust, and reintroduce road dust into the atmosphere both mechanically and by their wakes. Emissions factors will evaluate the quantity of pollution generated per unit of travel and are required to set the pollution emission rates.

Designated roadway emission zones will be created to serve as a source of pollution emissions and vehicle-induced turbulence (VIT).

Task 3.2: CFD simulation setup CFD simulations will be performed with OpenFOAM (Weller et al., 1998), an open-source CFD solver based on the finite volume method, commonly used in research and applied publications of air pollution dispersion modeling in urban environments (Pantusheva et al., 2022). OpenFOAM is known for its flexibility and customization which allows users to create tailored models and solvers for complex simulations involving fluid dynamics, heat transfer, and multiphase flow. While it does not inherently integrate the various environmental sub-models for atmospheric conditions, traffic conditions, vegetation, and pollution dispersion, OpenFOAM provides the tools to create these sub-models and couple them as to the main flow (conservation of mass and momentum) solver.

10

The flow equations will consist of the unsteady Reynolds Averaged Navier-Stokes (URANS) with the k-epsilon turbulence model (Launder and Spalding, 1974; Versteeg and Malalasekera, 2007). These equations consist of conservation of fluid mass, conservation of momentum, and conservation of mass of each pollutant species to be considered (i.e. PM10, NOx, etc.). Although neutral atmospheric conditions are commonly assumed for urban CFD studies (Franke et al., 2011), buoyancy effects may be considered through surface heat flux boundary conditions.

To accurately simulate an atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) it is necessary to specify adequate boundary conditions. OpenFOAM provides the flexibility to implement customized boundary conditions (BCs) according to the user's specific simulation needs. The BCs suggested by Richards and Hoxey (1993) provide log-law type ground-normal inflow boundary conditions for wind velocity and turbulence quantities for ABL modeling.

Vegetation obstructs airflow and causes air to move around and above it, which may enhance vertical mixing (Janhall, 2015). Additionally, vegetation can create windbreak effects, reducing wind speed and turbulence behind it, leading to increased pollutant concentrations due to reduced dispersion (Bowker et al., 2007). The presence of vegetation significantly impacts two major atmospheric processes: turbulent mixing and dry deposition of atmospheric constituents (Janhall, 2015; Baldauf et al., 2008; Bowker et al., 2007). These effects among others illustrate the importance of considering vegetation in urban CFD studies. Several approaches exist for modeling vegetation effects in urban environments (Janhall, 2015), including the use of drag models (Liu et al., 1996; Sogachev et al., 2006) and porous media models like Darcy-Forchheimer (Holzmann, 2021). These models require measured data from wind resistance experiments for different vegetation species to calculate the vegetation model coefficients. Kastner and Dogan (2022) digitized experimental data (Falkenstein-Smith et al., 2019) to calculate a densest and least dense vegetation coefficient for any vegetation volume. Vegetation drag models (Liu et al., 1996; Sogachev et al., 2006) require leaf area density and a drag coefficient for each vegetation volume. e. Community Engagement Plan

Introduction

AB 617, California’s landmark environmental justice bill of 2017, mandated the implementation of community-led air quality monitoring and emissions reduction programs, placing environmental data and scientific tools into the hands of community organizers that had formerly been handled exclusively by experts. Long before AB 617, however, Hyphae Design Laboratory, founded by Brent Bucknum in 2008, had been practicing this collaborative, community-driven, evidence-based form of environmental justice advocacy. Hyphae has provided technical assistance to hundreds of local community-based organizations in collecting data, implementing environmental design solutions, and advocating for their communities before state regulatory agencies. For almost fifteen years, from West Oakland to Stockton, Los Angeles, and the country at large, their partners have fought to address unfair pollution burdens faced by low- income communities and communities of color. By coordinating activists, technical assistants, academic researchers, air districts, city governments, and federal agencies, they have transformed their environments and achieved significant policy changes in pollution reduction, green infrastructure, and climate resilience.

Using hyperlocal mapping and measurement tools developed with funding from the National Science Foundation and the National Institute of Health, Hyphae helps CBOs identify air, soil, and water contamination in their communities, as well as other risks to human health such as extreme heat. These groups then deploy Hyphae’s software-based adaptOS platform to simulate, design, and implement environmental mitigation solutions in response. Such interventions range from urban greening to truck-rerouting to the design of software applications protecting sensitive receptors during extreme heat events. A persistent weakness hampers the optimal implementation of their combined environmental justice vision, however: the lack of reliable modeling tools.

Although low-coast commercial air sensors can now be reliably calibrated by co-locating them with reference monitors, estimating the state of a dynamic system from a series of noisy measurements using techniques like Kalman filtering still lags. Furthermore, the integration of Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) tools for reliable urban airflow modeling and simulation is not yet fully developed, hindering the accurate prediction and analysis of air quality in urban environments. University of South Florida (USF), in collaboration with its contracted partner Hyphae, submits this proposal to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to address these critical methodological gaps. Hyphae’s methods, combining outreach with technical expertise, have already secured for CBOs an empirical foundation from which to advocate for fair regulations, standards, and codes. But without adequate urban air quality modeling and estimation tools, communities and air districts cannot effectively assess the costs and benefits of given interventions. That weakness slows down environmental remediation at all stages of the process, and casts doubt on our collective capacity to resolve our present climate crisis. By redressing this computational liability, the EPA stands to expand California’s participatory environmental justice approach to a nation-wide scale, thus extending the State’s legacy of leading the nation in the formation of ecological policy.

Our partnership structure in this proposed project unites Hyphae’s oldest partners, the West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project (WOEIP) with newer collaborators in Little Manila Rising (Stockton, California) and Climate Resolve (Boyle Heights, Los Angeles). All three organizations actively monitor air quality with in-house sensor networks, united in their goal to

effect lasting policy changes through their local air districts and the California Air Resources Board (CARB). But only WOEIP has so far successfully pressured local transportation authorities to make environmental concessions. We thus recommend applying WOEIP’s successful strategy in West Oakland to South Stockton and Boyle Heights. The EPA can rely on the Hyphae coalition’s proven track record of addressing unfair pollution burdens to achieve regulatory goals. Should our collaboration prove successful, it could serve as a case study for CBOs across the nation in this new chapter of environmental justice.

Hyphae’s partnership with WOEIP has grown to include an ensemble of community actors ranging from tree-planting organizations, youth education groups, neighborhood associations, climate resilience activists, local churches, and schools. Together with regulatory agencies, air districts, city governments, and CARB, this Hyphae-assisted coalition is now implementing a multi-year, multi-million-dollar urban planning endeavor that combines vegetated buffers, road dieting, and truck rerouting in the Prescott neighborhood of West Oakland. In addition to the Prescott project, Hyphae and WOEIP are also collaborating on West Oakland’s Sustainable Transportation Equity Project (STEP), another CARB initiative aimed at protecting residents from pollution by reducing transportation emissions. After years of vigorous evidence-based advocacy from environmental justice groups, the Oakland Department of Transportation (OakDOT) and the Oakland Parks and Recreation Foundation (OPRF) finally ceded to community demands and agreed to participate in the project’s urban planning.

Underlying both projects is a conception of West Oakland as a comprehensive ecosystem linking community-based organizations with academic researchers, state and federal agencies, and the private sector. Beyond CARB, the coalition’s most immediate regulatory partner is the Bay Area Air Quality Management District (BAAQMD), the entity tasked with enforcing the Bay Area’s portion of AB 617’s mandate. Having served on the region’s Technical Advisory Group since 2017, Brent Bucknum has worked tirelessly to shape its policy agenda while curating its research goals and slate of funding proposals.

High-ranking air district allies include Stephen Reid, Senior Advanced Projects Advisor of the Assessment Inventory and Modeling Department, Song Bai, of the Assessment Inventory and Modeling department, and David Holstius, Ph.D., Senior Advanced Project Advisor in the Planning and Climate Protection department. Over time, these figures have been progressively integrating Hyphae’s models with those of the air district. Meanwhile, the CBOs directed the next phase of the pressure campaign to force the Department of Transportation and CalTrans to take seriously the pollution risks posed to residents by the Port of Oakland and adjacent freeways.

The West Oakland coalition was finally vindicated with the 2019 passing of the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan (WOCAP) which compelled CalTrans, the Department of Transportation, the Port of Oakland, and local refineries to make substantial regulatory concessions. These included stricter emissions controls on trucks and other heavy-duty vehicles, as well as a mandate to enhance air quality monitoring and invest in green infrastructure to mitigate pollution impacts. Furthermore, the WOCAP obliged future transportation-related projects to incorporate community feedback into their planning processes.

The Plan advanced a list of eighty-nine proposed pollution-mitigating strategies, ranging from urban greening to truck re-routing, optimized building envelopes, and clean electrification. But

due to persistent skepticism among many scientists regarding the accuracy of modeling, the steering committee still lacks an empirical basis by which to effectively simulate which of these proposed solutions would have maximum impact and highest benefit-to-cost ratio. More effective modeling could effectively demonstrate at a hyperlocal level exactly how tree canopy, vegetation, traffic, and shade could be optimally configured to protect environmental health.

In this grant application, we propose to use WOEIP’s successful pressure campaign as a template by which to compel transportation authorities elsewhere. Currently, Hyphae is serving five other AB 617 communities, all of which encounter similar skepticism when presenting models of proposed environmental mitigation scenarios to decision makers. These include Little Manila Rising and Climate Resolve, who together with WOEIP complete the triad of CBOs included in this grant proposal, as indicated through their letters of support. All three have suffered disproportionate pollution burdens due to California’s history of racist environmental policy.

Little Manila, a neighborhood of South Stockton, was demolished over the course of the 1960s by the racially motivated construction of a major freeway (Ong et al.). The community’s vulnerability to vehicular emissions was further compounded by the dramatically low air quality of California’s Central Valley (Garoupa et al). Funded by the Volkswagen settlement of 2016, LMR partnered with UC Merced’s Department of Public Health to pair their air quality data with rates of hospital visitation for respiratory illness and prenatal birth (Ha et al.). When compared with sharply distinct results from residents of privileged neighborhoods, LMR and UC Merced were able to demonstrate on an empirical basis the net health impacts accrued by decades of racist environmental policy. Recently, LMR received $2 million in grant funding from the San Joaquin Valley Air District to plant, with Hyphae support, two vegetated buffers in South Stockton.

In the Boyle Heights section of Los Angeles, where a similar legacy of redlining has placed residents in historically disadvantaged environmental conditions, Climate Resolve is partnering with Hyphae to plant vegetated buffers, implement a truck re-routing plan, and depave a section of the neighborhood. Coordinating with NASA’s Jet Propulsion Lab, the project integrates aerial and satellite imagery with Hyphae’s LiDAR scanning and Climate Resolve’s ground-level heat sensors to identify heat island effects. Trusted Climate Resolve messengers will then carefully track where sensitive receptors gather, collecting door-to-door canvassing information that cannot gleaned from maps. Hyphae will then combine all four data sets to map the area and generate a series of extreme heat mitigation scenarios.

As illustrated in the table above, each CBO will go through a two-year cycle in which all three tasks outlined in the Research Plan of this proposal will be executed. These cycles will be staggered by a period of one year each, allowing the community and researchers to learn from past experiences and course-correct accordingly. In the first year, with the community gathering on a quarterly basis to identify areas of interest to researchers, the relevant CBO data will be meticulously assessed, integrated, and validated. After researchers have generated a high- resolution map of the affected neighborhoods, the community will identify areas of interest and then direct USF-Hyphae to model a given pollution hotspot of one square kilometer in size.

In the second year, the researchers will iteratively refine that model. Then, over the course of three meetings scheduled with local transportation authorities, the CBOs will present those models and state their regulatory demands in the hopes of replicating the successful WOEIP strategy. The multiple meetings with the CBOs, air districts, and transportation departments will enable the USF and Hyphae team to receive feedback from these stakeholders on model output presentation, ensuring effective advocacy for the community. After all three CBOs have completed their cycles, the four-year grant period will culminate in a detailed report, series of academic papers, and plan of action. Our documentation will show how to replicate both our technical and community engagement strategies, thus providing a blueprint for the project’s successful application elsewhere and supporting hypothesis \#2 of this application.

Throughout the process, we have budgeted for in-house Hyphae software and app designers to continually translate complex air quality data into accessible visual forms. This includes generating an online dashboard and creating accessible, open-source web content that is easily shareable. These efforts aim to raise awareness about local air quality risks and inform the community about actionable steps and sound environmental policies to mitigate these risks. By providing clear, user- friendly visualizations and comprehensive data access, we will empower communities to understand their environmental conditions and advocate effectively for necessary changes.

Our approach to community engagement follows the conventions of Community-Engaged Research (CEnR), emphasizing partnership, co-learning, and capacity building. Outreach will include in-person and virtual meetings but also social media updates, email blasts, and door-to- door canvassing. Each CBO will approach their craft differently, however, we defer to their cultural competence as trusted community messengers to execute their vision of the project.

We chose to slot WOEIP at the end of the four-year grant term for the simple reason that their early success in persuading decision makers to cede ground has created a bureaucratic delay that the four-year grant will help mitigate. WOEIP’s ambitions, building on its early success, currently exceed those of our other partners, and thus require more time to realize. In closing, we express

the sincere hope that the combined weight of all three projects will have a watershed effect, inspiring CBOs in other AB 617 communities to adapt our strategy to their own local needs.

Conclusion

In summary, by supporting this project, the EPA stands to make an important intervention, first on a state-wide basis and later a national scale. By initially providing these discrete AB 617 communities with reliable measurement and simulation protocols, these standards can then be extended to the rest of the participating AB 617 districts of the State. This imagined magnification of a refined AB 617 model could repeat California’s history of leading the nation on matters of environmental policy. Government agencies facilitated previous uses of statewide technology in California that later took on a national role. These include CalEnviroScreen, AirNow, and Cal- Adapt, to say nothing of the advanced data analytics and Internet of Things modalities underlying California’s smart agriculture, wildfire prevention and management, water management technologies, smart grids, and renewable energy integration. It is no exaggeration to affirm that the future of climate change research and mitigation now rests on supporting reliable methods of predicting and simulating environmental remediation to meet the scale of the crisis at hand. Both the technological tools for that shift and the organizational method of its dissemination already exist – they simply need adequate support.
