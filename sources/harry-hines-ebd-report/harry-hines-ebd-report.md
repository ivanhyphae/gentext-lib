## **Introduction**
Texas Trees Foundation (TTF) is spearheading the Green Spine Streetscape and Park  intervention in the Southwestern Medical District (SWMD) in Dallas, Texas. The  corridor along Harry Hines Blvd between Market Center and Mockingbird is the focus for the streetscape  intervention which potentially includes  modifications to traffic flow and a massive tree planting initiative, with the goal of improving the health of corridor users. The cloverleaf interchange between Inwood and Harry Hines will be  replaced with a park to turn the district into a destination for gathering. One of the critical goals  for the intervention is to use evidence-based design (EBD) to facilitate and  monitor health-improving interventions, including  setting up the area as a living laboratory for sustainable design. 
 Thermal comfort was selected  as a primary endpoint for two reasons: 1) extreme heat is a pervasive problem in the district and 2) there is a plausible chance that we can measurably improve  thermal comfort in the district using landscape interventions, in particular the planting of trees. Air pollution is another endpoint which has been targeted for EBD.  There  is significant traffic in the corridor, with pedestrian focussed infrastructure unbuffered from traffic-sourced pollution. Tree planting is also a potentially effective intervention for reducing air pollution exposure for users of the district, although effective monitoring for air pollution impacts of vegetation is more complicated than that for thermal comfort. This document presents the results and  methodologies employed during the evidence-based design process of the Harry-Hines redevelopment project in Dallas, TX through the 30% design stage.\[image\]
 The evidence-based design process seeks to make use of evidence from literature, environmental simulations, and environmental monitoring campaigns to inform the design process to maximize the potential benefits, and minimize the potential harms of the built-environment interventions. While these goals apply to the Harry-Hines redevelopment project itself, they are also integrating that project into a platform for generalizing the learnings of the project to other communities. This platform is visualized in figure 1.
*Figure 1: Evidence-based design for environmental health.*
Evidence from previous projects is used to inform design of interventions and studies, which are then implemented, monitored (before and after), and the results fed back into the process. 
 In the case of the Harry Hines corridor, the benefits and harms that we focus on primarily are those that relate to heat stress and air-pollution exposure. From review of the literature we are aware that heat stress can be impacted by shade, where more shade in the summer can reduce heat stress, and less shade in the winter can reduce cold-stress. Wind is also an important component of thermal comfort, with higher winds in the summer reducing heat stress, and lower winds in the winter reducing cold stress. The literature on the effects of built-environment interventions on air pollution exposure is more complex. While interventions such as increasing the distance between a pollution source and the exposed person (receptor), or reducing the amount of pollution produced by the source are relatively uncontroversial approaches, there are also interactions between the structure and form of the built-environment and pollution transport & deposition which have been studied and may be of use, although the exact dynamics are not well defined enough to be certain that a particular type of intervention will necessarily be beneficial. For example tree and shrub foliage placed along a roadway may be able to act as a sort of sponge for airborne particulate matter, providing both a momentum sink and a high surface area, thereby facilitating the collection of pollution on leaf surfaces and thus preventing it from proceeding past the “vegetated buffer”. While this approach is potentially a low-cost, high-benefit design strategy, there are some caveats. For example, trees can also slow down the wind, which would otherwise blow the air pollution away from the ground and up into uninhabited atmospheric strata. This possibility of vegetation trapping air pollution at ground level where people will be more exposed makes it important not to simply plant trees without thought to the aerodynamic consequences… at least when an air pollution source is nearby.
The combination of the heat and air pollution rubrics gleaned from the literature leave designers with a bit of contradictory guidance: Tall canopy trees provide shade, which is good for reducing heat stress, but will potentially trap air pollution near ground level, endangering people’s health. Roadside vegetated buffers may be effective for remediating traffic sourced air pollution, but may slow the wind down (which increases heat stress), and may also reduce sightlines necessary for traffic engineering. To get beyond this limitation of qualitative rubrics, we employ environmental physics simulations to try to find the “sweet spots” where the benefits and harms of intervention design can be balanced to the overall benefit of the community. These simulations enable us to weigh the relative merits of different responses of the design to the constraints of the site, of regulations, and of other stakeholder preferences. By way of some examples; if a pedestrian path must be directly downwind from a highly trafficked roadway, then we might propose a roadside vegetated air barrier. However, we know from the literature that roadside vegetated air barriers are most effective when they are maximally tall (the taller the better) and thick (the thicker the better). However, the plantable area only allows a certain thickness, and the sightline requirements of the transportation regulators call for no more than a 4 foot high buffer. Through simulations, we can see if there is an appreciable difference (on air pollution) between a 4 foot tall buffer and a 12 foot tall one, and if there is, can we still make the buffer useful, for example by adding trees to the median across from it? Similarly, if we are trying to maximize the shade from new trees, we might want to plant lots of tall canopy trees along the pedestrian path. However, if the pedestrian path is right next to a busy roadway, will the shade benefits of the trees outweigh their pollution trapping effects? If they are tall enough, will they create a “wind-tunnel” effect that actually reduces pollution overall in the pedestrian zone? By iterating these potential design scenarios *in silico*, we can compare the relative merits of various design solutions and try to pick the most promising approaches. These approaches can be fed back to the stakeholders, and revised and re-simulated during the design process. 
While the simulations discussed above can provide important insights into the potential health consequences of design decisions, and provide guidance on how to improve specific design scenarios, simulations are inherently not real. The degree to which simulations faithfully represent actual or potential reality can be measured, tested, and validated. In our case the simulation of environmental parameters related to heat stress and air pollution can be measured in the existing environment, and the measurements compared to a simulation of the existing environment. The fidelity of this comparison can give some insight into how reliable a simulation of a hypothetical design might be, even if it cannot itself be verified until after construction. We have deployed a network of 30 low-cost thermal comfort sensors throughout the phase 1 area of the Harry Hines corridor between Butler and Medical District Drive. We have used these sensors to validate the fidelity of the thermal comfort simulations. However, air pollution sensors are more expensive and require greater effort to use effectively, so we are planning to deploy them in future phases. In addition to providing direct insights into the effects of built-environment features on heat and air pollution, and enabling validation of simulation results, the monitoring campaign will allow a pre/post evaluation of the performance of the design once it is installed. In combination with health studies on the users and inhabitants of the corridor, the sensor data will also allow longitudinal studies on the health benefits of the intervention, the results of which can be used to help design effective interventions in other communities. 
## **Design Process, Collaboration and Iteration**
The Texas Trees Foundation assembled a multidisciplinary team of researchers, designers, planners and stakeholders to see this project to completion. Hyphae Design laboratory was selected to integrate the design and intervention process with the EBD framework. The early visioning stages of landscape design were led by Peter Walker Partners with Burro Happold providing traffic analysis, while the 30% design was developed by Field Operation (Formerly James Corner Field Operations) with traffic engineering provided by Kimley Horn. 
The explicit goal of this work is to improve human health in our study areas. Our process includes planting design and engineering completed by Hyphae as well as ongoing collaborative design with cooperating engineers and architects. The specifics of these cooperative procedures are fluid and the subject of our efforts to optimize and manage for maximal real world impact. Courses of simulations are defined collaboratively with the goal of documenting guidelines and informing an understanding of the essential physical dynamics which impact environmental conditions. The objective is to contrast plausible scenarios in an iterative manner that isolates and optimizes variables that are possible for design intervention. The procedure is inherently iterative in that each course of simulations reveals unexpected results or questions which are targeted for further interrogation.
The culmination of this collaboration is a set of predictions regarding the measures of key physical parameters following the preferred design option. As supporting data, this process produces expected values for thermal comfort and air quality variation over space and time. Where the predictions are born out when experimentally verified then a positive impact is supported and the design can conclusively be called evidence-based. To ensure that verification can take place, we simultaneously deploy environmental monitoring programs as the design progresses, so that before and after comparisons can be made once construction is completed. 
## **Simulation**
### Simulation Platforms
This project utilized 3 different simulation platforms. The simplest is the UMEP SOLWEIG simulator, which estimates mean radiant temperature each hour of a day, and can be feasibly run on neighborhood size domains, such as the entire Harry Hines SWMD corridor. To estimate the wind fields and pollution transport in smaller domains, such as a single city block, or the park at Inwood, we use OpenFOAM, which is a computational fluid dynamics solver. To do detailed simulations of both heat, wind and pollution at the scale of a half a city block, we use ENVI-MET, which is a complex multiparameter environmental simulation software. Details of what these platforms do and how they work is included in the methods section of this document.
### Outputs and implementation
The outputs of the simulations include estimates of the spatial distribution of thermal comfort metrics and air pollution concentrations under the proposed or existing conditions of the environment being modeled. The body of results produced by this approach include a set of sets of multi-dimensional numerical matrices in binary format with attending metadata; a corresponding digital representation of an environment for each set. To enable interpretation of the results, a collection of themed graphics showing changes to numerous variables are also output to allow explicitly defined design rubrics or heuristics for real world use. In addition, tooling for executing the analysis and visualizing the results will be documented and published for use by interested stakeholders.
### Simulation results
#### Thermal comfort simulations results
We conducted simulations on thermal comfort across the whole corridor using a model of the existing conditions. We then simulated many iterative intervention scenarios in a representative section of the corridor, and several designs for the park at Innwood to test various design rubrics for efficacy. The results of these iterations produced several key findings:
  - The replacement of large mature trees with younger trees will increase heat stress on pedestrians. 
  - Sparse plantings will be less effective than larger groups of shade trees, which work together synergistically to produce not only more shade but also pockets of cooler air that can cool downwind areas that are not in the shade. 
  - Groves of trees with large trees around the edges and larger open space can result in cooler open spaces than smaller open spaces with a larger number of smaller trees
  - In some areas where wind speed is high due to the presence of building corners, planting shade trees actually makes it hotter, when the benefits of shade are outweighed by the benefits of high wind. 
 The results of these simulations were incorporated into the final 30% design, which was then simulated at the full corridor scale to estimate performance. The full corridor 30% design involved planting some 4000 new trees. We used a species specific growth algorithm to simulate tree growth of the planting plan over 50 years, and simulated mean radiant temperature at year of planting and after 50 years.  Agent based simulations of a pedestrian walking along the whole corridor along the pedestrian path would experience a cumulative heat burden 23% lower under the 50 year landscape condition compared to the existing condition. We also found that the likely average reduction in mean radiant temperature over the entire corridor amounted to around 25 degrees Fahrenheit in the 50th year (relative to no intervention, not adjusted for climate change). This is a potentially high impact amount of cooling, with some epidemiological studies suggesting that every 0.2 degree fahrenheit reduction in air temperature corresponds to a 3% reduction in heat-related mortality (Santamouris & Osmond, 2020), although more research is needed to understand how air temperature effects are related to mean radiant temperature, our monitoring results suggest that a 25 degree F reduction in radiant temperature likely corresponds to several degrees of air temperature cooling, which could amount to profound reductions in heat-related mortality.
#### Air pollution simulations results
Before completion of the 30% design, we simulated several iterations of potential design rubrics seeking to balance the goals of traffic-sourced air-pollution mitigation with the need to maintain pedestrian visibility. Some key results of these simulations were:
  - Dense, high canopy in high-wind roadside areas can actually speed wind up at ground level, thereby enhancing dispersion and reducing pollution concentration on pedestrian paths. However this requires truly tall trees with large clear zones beneath the canopy (like 30 ft to peak canopy density), as lower canopies can trap pollution at ground level and reduce wind speed near the ground, thereby increasing pollution concentrations. Trees this tall are not typically transplanted, so these types of benefits would have to be part of a long-term strategy.
  - Where roadside hedges are allowed, their efficacy at mitigating downwind pollution is enhanced by planting hedges or canopy trees in the upwind median. However, upwind median vegetation in the absence of downind barriers tends to make pollution worse on pedestrian paths. 
  - Where tall hedges are not allowed, a 1 meter berm with tall grass on it may be an effective alternative for roadside pollution mitigation. Furthermore, 6ft tall glass or polycarbonate wall is highly effective at reducing downwind air pollution, and does not reduce sightlines. 
The results of these simulations were used in the iterative design process to produce the 30% design. Most of these specific beneficial rubrics (very tall trees, dense, tall roadside hedges, glass walls) were not ultimately usable in the 30% due to logistics, sightlines, or cost concerns. However, the berms with grass and upwind median planting were not used in the 30% design. We then simulated the 30% configuration in a segment of the corridor and in the park at Inwood. These simulations showed overall increases in air pollution in the pedestrian paths for the 30% design over the existing conditions. This is likely due to low canopy decreasing dispersion of air pollution by lowering ground level wind velocity. However, these results have not been validated with air pollution measurements, so we propose to include air pollution measurement in future phases of the study. 
## **Monitoring and pre-post study**
Our first phase of monitoring has revealed significant benefits of large, mature trees in reducing environmental variables that contribute to heat-stress. Peak mean radiant temperature (MRT) reductions of 43 degrees Fahrenheit are seen between the shade of a large established tree and sun-exposed areas. Average summer afternoon globe temperatures are almost 14 degrees F lower in the shade of large trees than out in the sun. Small trees also have such an effect, but smaller (only 6.3 degrees F cooler on average) and over a much smaller area. Bus shelters are shown to have much higher air temperatures than even out in the sun, although more study is needed to determine the effect of this on total thermal comfort. Correspondence between sensor readings and concomitant simulation results suggest that the simulations of mean radiant temperature are valid, with R2 values up to 0.93 for some sensors, with additional  improvements expected as new data is incorporated. The monitoring network continues to gather data which will be available to researchers for further study, and can be used for future simulations as the built environment of the corridor changes.  
Provocative results of air pollution simulations suggest a need for air pollution monitoring in addition to thermal comfort monitoring, and a phase 2 monitoring program is proposed to include this as well as biodiversity monitoring, and studies of human health and lived-experience in the corridor.  A set of cross sectional and longitudinal hypotheses are presented on the impacts of the intervention on the environment and human health for further studies which will establish the corridor as a high-performance intervention, and as a living laboratory for validation of evidence-based design principles.
# Brief Literature review
## **Strategies for air pollution**
### Urban Vegetation for Air Quality Improvement:
 The use of vegetation as an urban air pollution mitigation strategy has focused on two general categories of observed mitigation scenarios that are addressed below; roadside vegetated air barriers and ambient removal by dispersed trees. In both cases the mechanism of mitigation can proceed through both deposition of pollutants on the surfaces of vegetation, and by dispersion of pollutants by vegetation-induced turbulent mixing of polluted ground level air with cleaner air from higher altitudes (Janhäll 2015). Additional air quality improvements by vegetation from heat island mitigation (for example by reduction of heat-induced ozone formation) are well documented (Akbari, 2005), and this is potential synergy of design strategies that focus on heat reduction.
### Vegetated Air Barriers for Roadside Pollution Mitigation:
 The design of roadside vegetated air barriers for effective air pollution mitigation has been discussed in Fuller et al. (2009). The primary design rubric is to facilitate a high surface area of vegetation to engender particulate deposition, with no gaps in the buffer to allow unmodified polluted air to pass through. This can be implemented with dense plantings of tall coniferous genera such as Thuja, Abies, Pinus, Taxus, and Juniperus, although species selection should be appropriate to a given site. In locations where a clear-zone is required by the side of the road, these large trees can be planted outside the clear-zone, while the clear zone can be planted with allowed species, such as tall grasses and shrubs with thin branches posing no threat to off-course automobiles.  The reduction in pollution concentration near the source of pollution by nearby vegetation has been studied under different conditions (see for example, Baldauf et al. 2008 & 2013, Bowkler et al. 2007, Brantley et al. 2014, Fuller et al. 2009, Gallagher et al. 2015, Hagler et al. 2011, Islam et al. 2012, Jeanjean et al. 2015, Steffens et al. 2012),
Studies on air pollution mitigation by roadside vegetated air barriers have found that as much as 37% of road sourced ultra-fine particulates can be captured or dispersed during crosswind conditions when the trees are planted close together and have dense foliage (Al-Dabbous & Kumar 2014). However, when vegetation is sparse and the vegetated buffer discontinuous, inconsistent effects are seen (Hagler et al. 2012). Tong et al. (2016) simulated particulate dispersion and deposition on roadside vegetated air-barriers, and found increased performance with increasing buffer depth and leaf area density, with 12 meter deep buffers performing much better than 6 meter deep buffers (where depth is the horizontal dimension perpendicular to the roadway length). Fuji et al. (2008) conducted wind tunnel experiments with different species of tree foliage and found lower particulate capture broadleaf species than by conifers.. Hwang et al. (2011) posit that needle-leaved species (such as conifers) are better at remediating air pollution than broad leaves. Taking these factors into consideration, the US Environmental Protection Agency has developed design guidelines for roadside vegetated buffers (Baldauf 2017) that we have incorporated into our methodologies.
### Urban Trees for Ambient Pollution Mitigation: 
The reduction of ambient pollution by urban vegetation has been studied extensively (see for example Bealey et al. 2007, Beckett et al. 1998 & 2000, Buccolieri et al. 2009 & 2011, Escobedo & Nowak, 2009, Escobedo et al. 2008 & 2011, Freer-Smith et al. 1997, Irga et al. 2015, Jim & Chen 2008, McDonald et al. 2007, Nowak 2006, Nowak et al. 2006 & 2013 & 2014, Setälä et al. 2013, Speak et al. 2012 and Yang et al. 2005). However it can be appreciated that further from the source of pollution, the pollution will be less concentrated, and hence filtration will be less effective. Hence roadside vegetated air barriers are generally more cost effective (per kg of pollution removed from the atmosphere) than targeting ambient pollution with dispersed vegetation. 
## **Strategies for Heat Mitigation**
A systematic review found that from 308 studies on the effectiveness of urban greening on air temperature or thermal comfort, urban trees consistently reduce daytime air temperatures and improve thermal comfort indices (Knight et al. 2021). The same study found evidence that trees can have a humidifying effect, and that grassy areas tend to be cooler than non-grassy areas, but that shading from trees was likely a key driver of temperature reductions. The overall average air temperature under urban trees distilled by Knight et al. (2021) was 0.8 °C cooler. While this may seem like a small change, in the context of existing epidemiological literature it is not. For example, Santamouris & Osmond (2020) did a meta-analysis of 55 studies and concluded that a 0.1 °C reduction in peak temperature decreases heat-related mortality by 3%. 
## **Strategies for Biodiversity**
The benefits of trees for air pollution mitigation and thermal stress reduction are discussed above, however there are additional benefits that may be supported by design rubrics. For example, greenness measured by NDVI was found to correlate with lower cancer mortality, with a minimum of confounding due to PM2.5 exposure (Coleman et al. 2021), suggesting the possibility of non air-pollution mechanisms for protective effects of vegetation. Some such mechanisms have been putatively identified, for example plant biodiversity has been linked to a decreased risk of asthma, where overall greenness measured by NDVI increased risk, when controlling for air pollution as a covariate (Donovan, Landry, & Gatziolis, 2021). Plant biodiversity was strongly correlated with decreased childhood leukemia (Donovan et al. 2021), with a hypothesized mechanism involving immune system regulation via the biodiversity hypothesis of inflammatory disease, which posits that decreased microbiome diversity contributes to chronic inflammatory and autoimmune disease through undereducation of the immune system (Haahtela et al. 2013). The leaf surface area metrics (like LAD and LAI) that our design rubrics emphasize intuitively relate to such hypotheses by providing the substrate for the foliar microbiome (Stone et al. 2018), and indeed leaf area has been correlated with biodiversity in general (Peng et al. 2017). Other structural properties of vegetation (vertical stratification and complexity), have been shown to strongly correlate with nearby airborne microbial biodiversity (Robinson et al. 2021). Indeed, diversity of street-tree species has recently been shown to correlate with reduced risk of cardiovascular and stroke mortality (Giacinto et al. 2021).  Thus in addition to optimizing designs around air pollution mitigation and thermal comfort our evidence-based design strategies also seek maximization of biodiversity, which beyond maximizing surface area includes facilitating structural diversity, species and genera diversity, and, to further ensure both structural diversity and resilience, diverse ages of plants to encourage continuous succession.
There are several methods of increasing biodiversity beyond maximizing leaf area. With regards to plant species diversity in the landscape, simply choosing to plant a diverse palette of species may be sufficient to garner the benefits described in Giacinto et al. (2021). The commonly cited 10/20/30 benchmark proposed by Frank Santamour (2004) states that urban forests should be comprised by no more than 10 % of any particular species, 20 % of any genus or 30 % of any single family, although more recently many cities are changing this to a more stringent 5/10/15 rule (Galle et al. 2021). A list of species existing or proposed can be used to calculate metrics of diversity, such as the Shannon Diversity Index, which is used to assess the health of urban forests (Love et al. 2022).  Similar attention must be paid to the structural diversity of the forms of the plants to address the mediators discussed by Robinson et al. (2021). 
Beyond species selection and specimen form, the importance of habitat connectivity cannot be understated. Biodiversity over many scales and trophic levels is supported by interconnections of different types of habitat, and higher metrics of connectivity have been associated with higher biodiversity (Kong et al. 2010). Connectivity metrics are many and varied, but several have gained traction as ecologically important in the context of urban forestry. The methods of Saura & Torne (2009) use graph theory to derive several metrics of connectivity between habitat patches, and that paper has been cited at least 450 times in the ecological literature. Other graph based metrics also consider the habitat patches or diversity supporting elements (such as pixels weighted by LAI) as nodes in a graph, and by network analysis on the graph, for example by computing the eigenvector centrality (discussed in the landscape ecological context by Estrada & Bodin 2008), we can assign relative importance to each node. In the design context, this means that we score the habitat patches and prioritize their size and position by moving them around and changing their size to optimize their local connectivity. There are also global connectivity measures for subregions that could be increased using the iterative patch placement algorithm of Clauzel et al. (2015). 
## **Simulation Validation**
Our simulation process seeks to discern the impacts of built environment features on thermal comfort and air pollution. Both of these impacts can be simulated in the ENVI-met platform. ENVI-met simulations can take into account the solar radiation, material heat capacity, emissivity, reflections, shading and evapotranspiration, and wind-vectors under various meteorological inputs to estimate the mean radiant temperature, humidity, and wind-speed in a 3 dimensional grid throughout the simulation domain. Air pollution sources (point, line and area) can also be simulated, and transport, dispersion and deposition of pollutants throughout the domain estimated using ENVI-met’s pollution modeling functions. 
While the benefits of such a holistic microclimate simulation are readily apparent to those seeking to develop evidence based, health performing designs, there are important questions regarding the validity of the simulation approach; most importantly “do the simulation results accurately reflect the real-world impacts of built-environment features on thermal comfort and air pollution?”. Several studies can be found in the literature that seek to answer this question. In most cases, the typical approach used in such studies is as follows: A study area is selected, and modeled as a domain in ENVI-met. Then real-world sensors that measure thermal comfort parameters or air pollution are placed in the study area, and some real world data is gathered. Then the ENVI-met domain simulations are run using meteorological inputs that are related to those that occurred during the sensing period. The results of the sensor readings can then be compared to the results from the same locations in the simulations, and the correlation or error between the sensor and simulation results is used to evaluate the performance of the simulation. 
Several studies using the approach to validate the thermal comfort metrics (primarily air temperature, humidity and radiant temperature) have had some success. Jamei et al. (2019) found that with some modifications to the model inputs, ENVI-met provided reasonably accurate air temperature predictions compared to field measurements, and was suitable for comparing the relative impacts of different urban growth scenarios. Ayyad & Sharples (2019) found very high correlations between measured air temperature and simulation results, although the results for wind speed were less consistent. Song et al. (2014) similarly obtained good results for temperature but not for wind speed. This apparent greater fidelity to temperature measurements than to wind vectors may be explained by the large contribution of solar insolation to thermal conditions; which may be less complicated to simulate than air turbulence. Consequently validations of thermal comfort indices, which combine temperature and wind speed have shown correspondingly neither Heshanihigh nor low correlations, for example Dain et al (2015) validated predicted mean vote (PMV, a thermal comfort index that combines temperature and wind) simulations with measurements and got r2 values between 0.480 and 0.614. Not all attempts to validate ENVI-met’s thermal simulations have been successful. For example Yang et al. (2021) concluded that ENVI-met, while useful, was not satisfactory for accurate predictions of temperature in an urban domain. <comment_start id=kix.fuhiq3k9nkoe>Jiang (2017)<comment_end id=kix.fuhiq3k9nkoe> was not satisfied with the simulation fidelity when compared with temperature measurements, although this may have been due to the large domain grid size (14 meters) relative to the size of actual built-environment features. Crank et al. (2022) found the ENVI-met struggled to accurately model MRT, with errors \>25 degrees for engineered shade structures such as tunnels, overhangs, & arcades. Clearly, further study is warranted to determine which aspects of model domain configuration, simulation parameters and sensor configuration allow for better or worse simulation performance. 
Attempts to validate the air pollution simulations have been less consistent than those of temperature. Heshani and Heshani & Winijkul (2022) modeled an extant park with various roadside vegetation structures, and monitored PM2.5 at various distances from the roadway. In some simulations, both the monitors and simulation show an effect of roadside hedges on air pollution, accelerating the reduction in concentration with distance from the roadway. However, in many cases, where ENVI-MET showed a decrease in pollution with distance from the roadway given a vegetated air barrier, the monitors did not corroborate this. The authors note that this may be due to background air pollution and interference from outdoor cooking operations in the neighborhood. Yang (2021) showed errors between 2% and 9% between simulation and monitoring of PM2.5, however their approach did not use ENVI-met’s particulate deposition—rather Yang developed a separate particulate deposition model in Vensim (a flexible system dynamics simulations environment) and used ENVI-met to get the wind speed as an input to Vensim. 
Not all attempts to validate ENVI-met’s air pollution simulations have been unsatisfactory. Nikolova et al. (2011) showed high correlations between measurements of ultrafine particulates (UFP) and models, although total errors in some cases were high enough to be of concern for simulating built-environment interventions. Hofman & Samson (2014) used a novel biomagnetic method to measure total deposited particulates on leaf surfaces, and found significant (though low) correlations between simulated and measured deposition. Paas & Schneider (2016) tested simulations in ENVI-met (and Austal2000, another pollution simulation software) against PM10 measurements. While the correlation between the actual values of the simulation and the measured values fell outside of the author’s performance threshold (plus or minus 2X), the direction of concentration dependency appears to be correct and consistent so it may be possible to develop calibration standards for correcting ENVI-met simulation data in some cases. 
Overall the validation of ENVI-met simulations with real sensor data is an active area of research. The benefits to such validation are far-reaching, as the holistic simulations provided by ENVI-met can meaningfully inform design of the built-environment if they can be confidently asserted to be accurate. While validation studies of the temperature simulations have been very promising, more research is needed to determine how valid ENVI-met simulations are for air pollution studies.

## **Simulation of thermal comfort**
### Metrics being simulated
Thermal comfort, or heat stress can be estimated from various physical metrics that can be measured or simulated, for example air temperature, radiant temperature, or surface temperature. While we use these metrics for monitoring and simulations, we also like to calculate the indices which capture more of the human experience of temperature, for example UTCI and dPET. The Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) is used to evaluate outdoor thermal environments and their impact on human health and well-being. It’s useful in understanding how various climatic factors affect human comfort. The UTCI integrates multiple meteorological parameters: air temperature, wind speed, humidity, and radiation -  to provide a single value that reflects the overall thermal stress experienced by an individual. Unlike indices that might focus solely on air temperature, surface temperature, or humidity, the UTCI's inclusion of Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT) allows it to capture the full spectrum of thermal influences over a certain length of time. This makes it a more comprehensive measure, as it considers the direct, diffuse, and reflected radiation from the surroundings, which are essential components of outdoor thermal comfort.
While UTCI is great for mapping wide areas that have variable exposure to sun and wind, for agent based simulations of pedestrian paths we like to use dPET. dPET is based on PET (physiological equivalent temperature). PET calculates the seated indoor temperature resulting in an equivalent energy balance of the human body, assuming an activity level of a person of a given size, and considering (in addition to air temperature, radiant temperature, wind speed, humidity, and pressure,) clothing temperature, sweating, and energy exchanges at the body surface. dPET (dynamic physiological equivalent temperature) calculates step-wise change to PET over a path of travel for a specified individual (Bruse, 1999).

## **Simulation of pollution**
### Design element iterations
Our focus for air pollution has been on right of way interventions and exploring alternatives that might result in a protective effect for pedestrians on the sidewalk. While the work of Baldauf (2017) and others establishes the use of large vegetated buffers many meters thick and tall, implementation in cities faces obstacles from space constraints as well as regulations and expectations that prioritize viewshed over less studied and invisible threats. If a substantial decrease in the pollution concentration at the sidewalk is achievable with smaller interventions that are less objectionable on those points, this would increase the likelihood of broad implementation.
The primary limitations of this study are: we studied only a single wind direction and velocity; and for small design iterations we did not study vehicle induced turbulence. The first can be addressed with further time and effort, the second requires a separate study design, which we have implemented at the block scale for the 30% design.
### Selected buffer hypotheses
#### Dense canopy and wind undercurrent
While there is modeled evidence that canopy can lead to accumulation of particulates below, we found that increasing the density enough reversed the effect. Dense enough canopy can form a wind barrier which causes faster air movement below which actually transports pollutants away more quickly, as shown in figure 25.  
\[image\]
*Figure 25: wind undercurrent simulation results showing significant reduction in PM concentration*
#### Median plantings
Our simulations otherwise confirm the provocative results that canopy can worsen air quality. If there are no downwind barriers (hedges, berms, etc) in the corridor then trees in the median are bad compared to no trees in median (see figure 26 for details). This is thought to be due to slower wind conditions which allow particulates to accumulate downwind. These results are consistent with several published model studies including Wania 2012 that suggest that canopy can worsen air quality if it prevents dispersion.
\[image\]  
*Figure 26. Large overhead canopy in the median depresses and slows dispersion of polluted air. Normalized.*
Our results suggest that although median canopy can reduce dispersion if no other planting exists, even ordinary canopy median planting becomes a net positive when paired with a boulevard barrier. If there are downwind barriers (hedges, berms, etc) then trees in the median seem to attenuate wind speed and improve the efficacy of even more porous boulevard barriers (see figure 27). We hypothesize that slower wind allows the downwind barrier to have a stronger result on both upward dispersion and deposition.
\[image\]  
*Figure 27. Comparing a 2m hedge in the boulevard with the addition of median planting, the hedge is significantly more effective.*
Tall and thick grasses can be advantageous in combination. Given the above, if there are downwind barriers, tall grass in the median (also with canopy trees) helps somewhat. In these simulations we added 1m tall grass (with a leaf area density of 0.8 baseline and a few permutations) and found that the effect is significant.
As shown in figure 28, hedges in the median are doubly effective vs. canopy trees in the median (given downwind barriers in both cases). This is thought to be due to a stronger effect on wind speed due to lower porosity near ground level.\[image\]  
*Figure 28. Comparing a large median hedge and boulevard intervention against only close packed street trees shows a reduction of up to -4 µg/m3.*

While ENVI-met is limited to 1m horizontal terrain increments, we found promising results when simulating terrain as part of a graduated air barrier in the boulevard. As shown in figure 29, a 1 meter tall landform creates a wind-shadow which plays a key role in protecting the sidewalk when combined with tall grass and canopy.

Another intervention which we hypothesize has a substantial positive effect is an impermeable barrier, here a glass wall 1.5m in height (shown in figure 30). This follows work by Hashad et al. (2020) but attempts to support use in a shorter configuration that preserves viewshed.

## **Monitoring**
The monitoring program methodology is described in the methods section. In brief, we deployed 30 thermal  sensors that measure globe temperature, air temperature, and humidity across the study area, along with 5 anemometers to measure wind speed. The thermal sensors were placed in 3 broad categories of locations; out in the open in sunny areas, underneath large, mature trees, and underneath small, young trees. Within these three categories, there were also subcategories related to ground surface, for example, some sensors were over grass, some over pavement, some over dirt. We placed the sensors in the spring of 2023, and they have each been gathering data every 20 minutes since then. 
### Cross-sectional monitoring of thermal impact of built-environment features
We employed a series of mixed-effects linear regression models to separately analyze the globe temperature, air temperature and humidity data with respect to the sensor location category, where location categories include under a big, mature tree, under a small, recently planted tree, and out in the sun. Although no globe sensors were put in bus shelters, for air temperature and humidity we also have bus shelters as a location category. The model treated the location category as a categorical fixed effect and the individual sensor ID as a random intercept. The globe temperature, air temperature or humidity served as the dependent variable. The model parameters were estimated using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method. In all three cases, the sensors in the shade of big trees are considered the reference condition. We focused only on sensor readings from summer afternoons (from June 1st to August 30th, and from 12pm to 5pm Central Time). The results for globe temperature are shown in table 2. Within these time periods, the mean globe temperature is 7.8 degrees C higher in the sun than under a large tree, while the mean temperature under a small tree is 4.3 degrees higher. All differences are shown to be significant. Re-running the model with small tree condition as the reference produced similar results, also significant (not shown).

 Using these 4 sensors as examples, we can identify some specific sources of error. For MRT1, which is located on a light pole in the parking lot (full sun), we see a pretty good correspondence of r2 of 0.82. The main discrepancy seems to be that the sensor reading goes much higher than expected in the early part of the day, and drops off more quickly than the simulation. The higher early sensor readings may be due to the lack of landcover data in the simulation, which treated all surfaces the same, even though in reality the asphalt surface and color of nearby parked cars could increase MRT in the morning over what the simulation expects. The faster cool-off of the sensor data may be because we calculated MRT using the wind data from the sensor in the parking lot, while the simulation used air speed data from the airport, which may be slower. 
For MRT7, which is located underneath a large Oak tree, we see very good agreement between sensor and simulation, with an of r2 0.91. 
For MRT5, which is also located underneath a large Oak tree, we see poor correspondence, with r2 of 0.45. This may have to do with the combination of low geopositioning accuracy of the sensor (which used a cell-phone gps accurate to a few meters) and the fact that the LIDAR data that was used to construct the canopy model is from 2019, while the sensor data is from 2023. This means that the tree in the simulation is smaller than the tree in the sensor data. This can be seen from the temperature spike at 7am in the simulation, which did not occur in the sensor reading (and appears to be the main discrepancy in the data), which suggests that the simulated smaller tree was not providing shade over the modeled sensor location at that time. This highlights the need for high precision geolocation of sensors in the field, and up-to-date canopy models for simulation domains.
For MRT8, which is located on a small tree in the parking lot, and represents the medium to low shade condition, the correspondence with simulation is low, with r2 of 0.54. Here again the 2019 LIDAR acquisition date throws off the experiment, as those small trees had not been planted yet in 2019, so the simulation is treating the MRT8 location just like an open, unshaded area, hence the simulation shows continuously higher temperatures than the sensor reading. As we know from table 2, a small tree does provide a cooling effect for globe temperature, and so given the lack of canopy in the simulation, the results here (of the simulation showing a higher MRT than the sensor) is to be expected. 
Examination of these inputs in the context of the results indicates two principal reasons for some of the sensors being very close to the simulation and others being off: 1.) the canopy model in the simulation is from 2019 LIDAR, so over the last 4 years some trees have grown larger, some trees have been cut down or recently planted, and this means that some sensor readings will be in the shade or sun while simulations of the same location are not. 2.) The sensor positions were recorded with cell-phone GPS, which may be inaccurate to the tune of 3-10 meters, while the simulation is performed at the 1m scale. This means that a sensor that is actually under a tree might be simulated as out in the sun, or vise-versa.
 To remedy these inconsistencies in the simulation process, we have collected summer 2023 LIDAR data, using a more accurate GPS, so that a new 3D model can be created which has contemporaneous tree canopy and shade information, and the sensor locations can be placed specifically in the model, as the LIDAR scans were conducted after sensor installation, so the sensors are visible in the new LIDAR scan. We will reformulate the simulation parameters using the updated LIDAR, and expect to obtain higher correlations between sensor readings and simulations. ## **Collaboration process**
### Opportunities
The success of this project is a product of collaboration between planners, designers, scientists, engineers, political officials, project managers, community members and many other people. The monitoring campaign brought together scientists who designed sensors and networking architecture with property owners in the corridor to create a first of its kind high spatial resolution low-cost radiant temperature monitoring system. The simulation efforts were a coordinated effort between the design team, who created many iterations of landscape configurations, and the simulation team who turned the design iterations into CFD modeling domains for wind, heat and pollution simulations. The design process sought feedback from community members living and working in the district, government officials, property owners and other stakeholders, who weighed the results of the design and simulation loops with project goals. The research team presented methods to peer reviewers in the scientific community and incorporated their feedback into the method development.  
As a result of the work so far, including the ongoing monitoring, and the corpus of novel simulation data we have begun collaborating with health researchers at Texas A\&M university, University of Virginia and USDA Forest Service on health studies specific to the SWMD corridor intervention. These collaborations involve study design and grant-writing, leveraging the living laboratory to determine the health benefits of the intervention and to build scientific progress in the field. 
### Challenges
Many of the challenges in evidence-based design have origins in the cross-disciplinary and multi-agency nature of the collaboration process. The integration of simulation and monitoring results into the design can be subject to dilution or opposition based on a number of mitigating circumstances. For example, our simulations suggested that dense, tall vegetated hedgerows both in the median and alongside the roadway can have beneficial effects on pollution concentrations in the pedestrian paths. However, the prevailing consensus from local planners was that this would obstruct sightlines both for navigation purposes (the view of the pedestrian path from motor vehicles and the view of vehicles by pedestrians) and for crime, (security and unhoused populations), and that no amount of pollution mitigation would be worth this lack of visibility. Thus the most high performance design elements found in the simulation iterations for air pollution mitigation were not used in the 30% design. This lead to an effort to simulate shorter hedges combined with berms, which simulations suggest might be partially effective at pollution mitigation, while maintaining sightlines. These elements did make it into the design in some places, and so their efficacy may be assessed post construction using air monitoring campaigns. 
Another outcome of the both simulations and the monitoring campaign was the benefits of large, mature trees. Monitoring of existing trees found that the larger trees performed far better than the smaller and medium sized trees. Simulations confirmed this, with growth simulations of the 30% design showing the most benefits from the largest planted trees and the simulations at the longest timelines of growth (50 years). However, the consensus among local arborists was that the existing largest trees in the median would need to be removed as they would be unlikely to survive the construction process. This single decision could set the temperature reductions of the project back by several years. 
Another challenge in the collaboration process was the interagency coordination required for installing the sensor network in the corridor. Discussions with Parkland hospital (the owner of the tallest building in the study area) for placement of a LoRaWAN gateway on their roof took around 6 months from initial outreach to hub installation. This was due to both bureaucratic obstacles such as getting approval from leadership in multiple departments, and logistical constraints; with multiple site visits to establish signal propagation, and the final installation of the gateway requiring the drilling of a hole through an 8” thick concrete wall. The process for getting approved to install sensors on city-owned utility poles took even longer, with first outreach to final approval being an 18 month process. This required coordination between multiple departments to get MOUs signed and permits finalized, multiple site visits with city engineers to get plans approved, and long periods of waiting for understaffed city agencies to turn around permits and communications.  
### Synthesis
The collaboration at the heart of this project powerfully illustrates how diverse skill sets, perspectives, and institutional backing can converge to propel a transformative urban intervention. Engineers, designers, property owners, community organizations, and public officials each brought critical lenses and resources: from developing innovative low-cost sensor networks, to integrating simulation outputs into design iterations, to securing the political and financial support needed to realize a living laboratory in the heart of Dallas. Interdisciplinary camaraderie was equally vital on the research front, where data-sharing led to deeper inquiries into the health impacts of green infrastructure; sparking new projects with academic partners at Texas A\&M, the University of Virginia, and the USDA Forest Service. These early successes underscore the synergy possible when stakeholders unite around a shared goal: creating an urban landscape that prioritizes both human well-being and environmental resilience.
Even amid the hurdles of multi-agency coordination and sometimes-competing design priorities, this collaborative framework has fostered a sense of collective ownership and problem-solving. Rather than allowing challenges such as balancing visibility with pollution mitigation or preserving mature trees through invasive construction to stall progress, project partners leveraged scientific evidence and stakeholder feedback to find practical, if imperfect, compromises. In so doing, they laid the groundwork for future validation studies and phased improvements, including the planned post-construction air quality monitoring that will further refine vegetation strategies. As a model of evidence-based design, this collaboration shows that genuine partnership not only enriches a project’s scientific rigor but also imbues it with the flexibility and resilience to *adapt*, ultimately ensuring that the Southwestern Medical District’s streetscape transformation remains both ambitious and attainable.
# Discussion
The Harry Hines corridor redesign project is a powerful opportunity to employ evidence-based design, measure the benefits of evidence-based built-environment interventions, and to develop evidence-based tools to allow further benefits to be provided to other communities. The site is afflicted by extreme heat and problematic traffic-sourced pollution, both of which may be mitigated by well designed interventions. Simulations demonstrate that where the shade of large trees can be intersected with pedestrian paths, thermal comfort indices can be improved by more than 20 degrees C. The phase 1 monitoring campaign bears this out, with observed differences in globe temperatures exceeding 16 degrees C between sunny areas and areas shaded by large trees, with average summer afternoon differences being around 7.8 degrees C. The monitoring campaign also shows a benefit from small, recently planted young trees, which produced an average of 3 degrees cooler areas in their shade, although in addition to the lower average temperature benefit, *the amount of that shade is also much lower*, so emphasis should always be placed on planting the largest trees possible, and avoiding cutting down old trees unless absolutely necessary. 
While the shade of trees both large and small had an effect on air temperature, the most noticeable effect on air temperature (as opposed to radiant temperature) was seen with bus shelters. Inside the bus shelter on a summer afternoon the air temperature is on average 3.5 degrees C hotter than out in the sun, and 7 degrees C hotter than under a large tree. Further research is needed to determine what impact this has on thermal comfort. Bus shelters provide much needed shade, and so we would expect them to have reduced globe-temperature compared to out in the sun, however they are painted black and are made of metal, so they may absorb sunlight and reradiate thermal photons into the shelter more so than other sources of shade. Humidity readings are on average 16 units lower than under a large tree, and 9 units lower than out in the sun, which may reduce the heat stress impacts of higher air temperatures. However, they also block wind effectively, thereby increasing heat stress… so to determine the total effect of the bus shelters on overall thermal comfort (which is a combination of radiant temperature, air temperature, humidity and wind speed) would require perhaps temporary installation of globe and wind sensors, or a periodic monitoring campaign with mobile equipment. 
Validation of the simulation methods with the monitoring data show promising results. Where the simulation domain fidelity to the 2023 conditions is high, we see R2 values comparing sensor to simulation of \>0.9, which is as high as we would hope for from this type of work.  However, in areas where the domain fidelity is low (for example, where the sensor is hanging on a tree which had not been planted yet when the simulation domain inputs were created in 2019) the R2 values are down below 0.5. Helpfully these factors seem consistent, and so we expect to get much better validation results when we update the simulation domain with the new 2023 LIDAR data that we collected in September 2023. 
Air pollution simulations indicate that sufficiently tall and dense canopy trees can help reduce air pollution via a “wind-tunnel” effect by increasing wind speeds beneath the canopy, although further validation with higher resolution models and monitoring is recommended as this goes against conventional wisdom, which suggests that tall canopies can trap particulates at ground level by preventing vertical mixing. Additional simulations suggest that up to 4 microgram per cubic meter reductions in PM1 may be possible with carefully considered combinations of downwind buffers (employing berms and grasses to maintain sightlines) and median plantings. Here again additional validation with higher resolution models and air pollution sensors are recommended, as existing literature validating ENVI-met’s pollution transport results have not been consistent. Furthermore, our own models demonstrate that placing trees in the median without a downwind buffer can have the opposite effect, and so the sensitivity of the effect to small changes in the design should be vetted before making any strong conclusions. 
While the confidence in the air pollution simulations can be increased by sensitivity analysis and additional simulations, the gold standard for simulation validation is monitoring. We have proposed to make use of air pollution sensors in a second monitoring phase to accomplish this. The second phase of monitoring will also employ a larger number of thermal comfort sensors to fully integrate landcover and wind-speed data into the calculations, and also biodiversity sensors and studies of human experience, perception, mental health metrics and biomarkers. We have proposed this to intersect with a set of both cross-sectional hypotheses regarding the impact of built-environment parameters, and longitudinal/pre-post hypotheses regarding the impact of the corridor redesign intervention itself. We have developed a methodological framework for addressing each of these hypotheses either with the phase 2 expanded sensor network or with the help of our local, national and international collaborators in the health and social science fields. This will instantiate the Southwestern Medical District as a prominent living laboratory that can serve as a platform for future researchers to develop and disseminate high-performance evidence-based design.
# Methods
## **Monitoring**
### Sensor Hardware
 We installed a Long Range Wide Area Network (LORAWAN) hub on the rooftop of the 18th floor of the Parkland Memorial Hospital. This provides line-of-sight radio communication across the whole corridor from Market Center to Mockingbird. The LORAWAN hub connects over the internet to a cloud service running a LORAWAN network server which routes sensor messages via ‘MQ’ Telemetry Transport (MQTT) protocol to our cloud MQTT clients, which decode the LORAWAN packets and store the sensor readings in several time-series databases. We deployed two overall types of sensors predominantly; temperature sensors and wind sensors. The temperature  sensors employ a LORAWAN radio-module and microcontroller based on the Dragino LSN50-V2 module, which provides a unified communications platform to which we can attach a variety of sensor types. The LSN50-V2 has a battery which can last up to 10 years if messages are configured to be transmitted only every 20 minutes, as we have done for our temperature sensors. To the LSN50-V2 module we attach 2 cables, one of which connects to a SEN0385 weatherproof SHT31 temperature and humidity sensor, which we immobilize underneath a white radiation shield. The second cable attaches to a waterproof DS18B20 which we affix inside a 6 inch diameter hollow copper sphere, which is painted matte black. The DS18B20-based “globe sensor” provides radiant temperature measurements, while the SHT31 provides air temperature and humidity measurements. Our wind sensors are Barani Meteowind IoT Pro units, which have a small solar panel, battery, LORAWAN radio, elliptical-cup anemometer and direction vane. The wind sensors send data every 10 minutes, including average wind speed, peak wind speed, average and peak wind direction, minimum and maximum wind speed,  and direction confidence interval. ## **Pre/Post & Cross-Sectional Study Methodology**
In order to create an evidence base for this and other designs, the high impact variables that can be adjusted by the intervention must be monitored and modeled before and after the intervention. Such a pre-post study design requires both planning data collection prior to the construction phase and also maintaining data collection after construction. In addition to pre-post studies, other types of longitudinal studies can be conducted that assess the impact of change on the environment and the health of the community over time as a result of the intervention. Furthermore, in service of the design process itself in the pre-construction phase, we can conduct cross-sectional studies using monitoring and modeling data to determine the relative impacts of different built-environment features and intervention typologies. Based on the goals of the designers and the scope of monitoring and modeling plans, we have established the following set of cross-sectional and longitudinal hypotheses.
### The cross-sectional hypotheses are:
1.  Landscape features will have an effect on thermal comfort parameters. Specifically the shade trees will reduce daytime radiant temperature, but also wind speed, resulting in a balancing act between the cooling effects of shade and the warming effects of wind velocity reduction. Additionally the thermal comfort will be impacted by various other surface materials, for example we hypothesize that radiant temperatures will follow an increase in proximity to grass \< cement \< asphalt.
2.  Landscape features will have an effect on air pollution. Specifically roadside lollipop trees will increase roadside air pollution concentrations by trapping traffic sourced emissions at pedestrian level. Buffer-shaped trees will increase concentrations on the traffic side, and reduce them on the other side.  
3.  The simulation results will be associated with the monitoring results. The simulation results will include wind speed and direction, as well as radiant temperature levels for each sensor location on specific dates at specific times, allowing the accuracy of the model to be tested.  
4.  Landscape features will have an effect on biodiversity; with bird, bat and insect populations being more diverse in areas with higher leaf area, more terrestrial-aquatic ecotones, and higher habitat patch connectivity. 
5.  Pedestrian behavior and perceptions will be associated with landscape features. Pedestrians will report more comfortable walking in areas with lower summer UTCI, which is mediated by landscape features according to hypothesis \#1 above. Where pedestrian routes show equivalent utility (for example given two paths that connect the same destinations with equal length) pedestrians will more frequently take the path with lower UTCI in the summer. 
### The longitudinal hypotheses are:
1.  The intervention will have measurable impacts on thermal comfort (**pending intervention design**). Before and after measurement of thermal comfort parameters will reveal that the intervention reduces extreme heat in the summer, and reduces extreme cold in the winter. 
2.  The intervention will have an impact on air pollution (**pending intervention design**), and while it is unlikely to be measurable using PM1, PM2.5 or PM10 as a proxy for traffic pollution, it may be detectable by measuring ultra-fine particulates and NO2, and possibly black carbon.
3.  The intervention will have measurable impacts on bird, bat and insect biodiversity (**pending intervention design**), with before and after measurements indicating an increase in biodiversity after the intervention, possibly with a lag period depending on how much construction of the intervention disrupts the existing ecosystem.
4.  The intervention will have a measurable impact on pedestrian activity and perceptions (**pending intervention design**). A larger percentage of pedestrians will report an enjoyable pedestrian experience, and a larger number of people will use pedestrian routes to traverse the district after the intervention vs before.
5.  The intervention will have a measurable impact on human health outcomes (**pending intervention design**). These may be physiological (for example cardiovascular disease, asthma, heat stress) or psychological (for example stress, anxiety, depression). 
### Quantitative methods 
 The cross sectional hypotheses are already being addressed in the pre-construction phase and are discussed in the results section. These studies will form the basis of the pre component of the pre-post longitudinal studies. The ongoing cross-sectional monitoring work is based on the roughly 30 temperature sensor locations being studied. These are yielding results into cross-sectional & longitudinal hypotheses \#1, that landscape features will impact the atmospheric variables related to thermal comfort, and cross-sectional hypothesis \#3, that simulation results will be correlated with measurements, although only in the case of thermal comfort related metrics, as no air-pollution monitoring has been commenced. In order to address the remaining hypotheses a second phase of monitoring is envisioned. This second phase will employ a larger number of thermal comfort sensors to improve statistical power enough to resolve the effects of trees on wind, and the effect of surface materials on radiant temperature, and the effect of building-sourced shade. While some of these sensors will be colocated with thermal comfort sensors, others may be uniquely focused on other biodiversity-pertinent locations which relate to connectivity metrics which will be calculated for the neighborhood. The connectivity metrics, such as probability of connectivity (PC), interaction flux (IF), and eigenvector centrality (EC) quantify the importance of the habitat patches for facilitating interaction across the habitat surface. A sub-hypothesis that habitat patches that have higher calculated connectivity have higher biodiversity can be tested, and the results use to improve calculations of the benefits of design decisions that impact connectivity. 
While bird and bat biodiversity, and total insect and traffic noise are key performance indicators of the intervention, the collection of this data will also allow research on the interaction between these variables to help understand the mediators between built-environment features and biodiversity. For example, while increasing total canopy coverage may provide direct habitable space for birds and bats, increasing pollinator gardens and diverse shrub and groundcover conditions might increase insect populations which may enhance bird and bat habitat by providing more food sources. 
In addition to the thermal comfort and biodiversity monitors, phase 2 will also include an air pollution monitoring campaign to address cross sectional and longitudinal  hypotheses \#2. An air monitoring cart will be walked through the pedestrian accessible areas of the corridor at regular intervals (6 runs per season, 3 during rush hour and 3 at night). The cart will carry measurement equipment for PM1, PM2.5 and PM10 mass (optical sensor), ultra-fine particulates (UFP) counts (condensation particle counter), black carbon (BC), NO, NO2, temperature, GPS coordinates, sound and video. Passive samplers will also be deployed throughout the neighborhoods to collect NO, NO2 and O3 and certain VOCs from 30 stationary sites. Point and line pollutant source predictors will be geographically represented with the air monitoring data and built-environment measurements to perform spatiotemporal modeling starting with land use regression (LUR) modeling. LUR is a predictive modeling approach that enables estimation of intra-urban air pollution spatial contrasts from air monitoring data gathered at a limited number of locations within the area (Hoek et. al 2008). The resulting LUR model can be associated with the outputs of the evidence-based design process to establish an impact of the design on air quality. 
To address cross-sectional hypothesis \#5 and longitudinal hypotheses \#4 & \#5, which relate to pedestrian usage, satisfaction and health, we will employ both quantitative measurements and qualitative assessments. Quantitative measurements will employ both pedestrian counting sensors, survey instruments and biomarkers. Pedestrian counting sensors will be used to determine cross-sectional impact of microclimate on usage patterns & longitudinal change in usage patterns due to changes in built-environment parameters. The pedestrian counting sensors will be placed at key locations on pedestrian paths, including the most common destinations (hospital entrances & exits, bus-stops, major pedestrian road-crosswalks) and paths between destinations. A total of 12 pedestrian sensors will be used for this purpose. 
Studies of the effects of the intervention on people will target both in-situ participants and prescriptive participants. In-situ participants are exposed to environmental parameters naturally as they go about their normal lives. Prescriptive participants are instructed by experimenters to go to particular locations at particular times to create a standardized exposure surface for study. In both cases we will employ a control condition, which can be both temporal and spatial. For example, in pre-post studies, the pre-intervention condition can serve as a temporal control. In both pre-post and cross-sectional studies we will also use spatial controls, where a group of in-situ participants who are not exposed to the intervention naturally can also be studied. Similarly in prescriptive studies, a group of participants can be sent to a placebo exposure site, or there can be a cross-over design where participants are measured after exposure to both an intervention site and a placebo site.
 Survey instruments will be used to quantify the human experience of working and living in the corridor cross-sectionally as well as before and after the intervention. Survey parameters will include long-term assessments of health, quality of life, and satisfaction with the environment, as well as short term metrics of mental health and cognition. Long-term parameters such as depression scales, and anxiety scales can be used both as direct measures of intervention or cross-sectional feature efficacy, as well as covariates for short-term metrics. Short term metrics can include both subjective responses to the circumstance via likert scales of mood, anxiety, depression etc., as well as indirect measures via psychometric tests, for example using cognitive reaction time or memory recall tests as a proxy for stress. These studies will be coordinated by academic project partners who specialize in each method.
Where participants are enrolled in in-situ or prescriptive study, we may also collect biomarkers and other clinical metrics to assess the physiological impacts of the intervention. This data may include patient health evaluations and biomarkers, for example blood pressure, arterial stiffness, circulating epithelial progenitor cells, cortisol, volatile organic compound metabolites, catecholamine metabolites, and microbiome diversity. For all participants we will address available covariates as potential confounders or mediators (and use them to establish synthetic controls), for example age, race, smoker/nonsmoker/never-smoker, alcohol drinking, socioeconomic status (income, parental occupation/education level, marital status) for both respondents and their geographic population unit median, body mass index, exercise frequency/duration/intensity (normalized to metabolic equivalents if possible) & sedentary behavior, self-reported social engagement duration/frequency, and estimated exposure to air pollution, thermal stress, and other built-environment parameters that are salient to the intervention (which, pending the final design, likely also include biodiversity, vegetation, traffic noise, wind, and shade). Pending development of sample sizes and power calculations, we plan on using difference in differences (DD) methods to quantify the impact of the interventions on longitudinal parameters. In the cross-sectional analysis we will use Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) to associate differences in built-environment parameters with health outcomes.

## **Simulation**
The simulation methods include those of 3 platforms: UMEP, OpenFOAM, and ENVI-MET. Each platform has some inputs and parameters which are similar to the others, and some which are unique.
### ENVI-met
The ENVI-met software suite is unique for its holistic feature set and ease of integration into architecture and urban design workflows while also being well studied and substantially validated in peer reviewed publications. The method outlined here is a blend of technical strategies and environmental analysis study design. As such it is both design oriented and scientifically founded.
ENVI-met is holistic in that it is a coupled solver incorporating models for: radiance; atmospheric conditions such as wind and humidity; particulate transport and deposition, active chemistry in the atmosphere; interaction of vegetation and soil with atmosphere; properties of artificial materials and structures including building metrics and others. These models are solved in coupled parallel over a time series on a serialized uniform voxel grid. Numerous other simulation platforms exist which are widely used and informative for one or more of these purposes at this scale, but ENVI-met uniquely covers this breadth of parameters in a single program.
The work of the present method is to integrate the simulation execution into an urban design iteration process. This entails compiling appropriate and sufficiently accurate inputs for all physical parameters, representing and generalizing the physical domain faithfully, individually varying inputs with care to investigate the predicted impacts of specific dimensions of proposed interventions, defining and refining consistent metrics to get at the quality of each proposal, visualizing the resulting data in an intuitive and honest narrative structure.
### Inputs
For each of the three simulation methods, the inputs consist of a digital twin (3d model of the domain of study), meteorological inputs (the regional weather conditions for the time of simulation) and numerous simulation specific parameters, such as traffic counts, vehicle speed etc. The faithfulness and level of detail of the numerous inputs are necessarily variable. On the one hand good input data is not always easy to source or develop, while on the other hand more detail and better accuracy is not always advantageous. The balance lies in achieving sufficient quality inputs where needed for the particular study questions of interest. Because our hypotheses are most commonly investigating the relationships of vegetation with environmental stressors on human health, we are most interested in the variables to which thermal comfort and air quality are sensitive. Essential input data include spatial, meteorological, botanical, materiological, and mobility information. Each of these realms is the subject of ongoing research and development and optimization in this methodology.
Spatial datasets are leveraged to generate a series of 3D matrices which make up the domain modeled by ENVI-met, OpenFOAM or UMEP. The basic discretization exercise is to locate physical objects and substances on a 3D grid and store their properties in an accompanying xml-based database that can be compiled at run time. While some of the physics in ENVI-met are solved in parallel, the fundamental operation is 1D over a list of grid cells. Hence the 3D environment of terrain, solid structures, vegetation, and air are resolved from spatial data into a voxel grid at a chosen resolution. The resulting domain is therefore a thoughtful but substantial abstraction from other input formats.
Meteorological inputs are taken from multiple sources and resampled with the intention of supplying model conditions which are most ‘typical’ within the defined design context. Because ENVI-met is a time series and not a steady-state model, the parameters are always prototypical and represent a limited case. This parallels the ‘typical model year’ approach used by EnergyPlus in the EPW format, which is also a primary source for our ENVI-met model parameters. Our approach uses a defined list of hourly temperature and humidity values for each hour of simulated time, as well as defining the boundary condition with accompanying wind vector values derived from public sources and corroborated with our own on-site anemometers. Once defined, this meteorological forcing array is changed only rarely and intentionally so as to serve specific design questions regarding representative climate conditions.
Botanic and materiology information are of basically unlimited possible complexity and constitute opportunities to study the essential variables of urban design. As a general approach we often use default values which are plausible but not necessarily factual, while holding these parameters steady over the course of a study model series removes it from consideration as a dependent variable. With that said, there are physical dynamics which we consciously exclude though we suspect their influence is important. For example, we have often excluded or greatly simplified variation in ground surface albedo although we are aware that it directly affects thermal properties and indirectly affects convection currents. Intentionally introducing this and other dimensions is critical to many of our study arcs. Each study arc selects or excludes variables with purpose and this is an essential subject matter for discussion and review.
As this method focuses on human habitation in the environment, statistics on human mobility directly inform both input parameters and the limits of focus areas. A key data set is traffic, including motorized, non-motorized vehicles, and pedestrian traffic. While studies are useful without these inputs, defining traffic quantity and location allows more specificity regarding changes and impact. We can readily study normalized values over broad areas, and in fact many other studies take this approach.
The built environment simulation requires inputs of a simulated domain model, or “digital twin”, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) discretization (gridding) parameters, meteorological inputs, traffic data and simulation parameters. The digital twin defines the structure of the environment, and may represent the existing conditions, historical conditions or a proposed design condition. Given different digital twins and keeping other inputs constant, the effect of design decisions on output metrics (related to thermal stress or air pollution distribution) can be estimated. 
### Digital twin procedure
The digital twin procedure takes data inputs such as geospatial information system (GIS) data, LIDAR data, landcover classifications, and tree surveys and outputs a 3 dimensional model representing terrain, buildings and vegetation. The output model may be a set of triangular meshes, voxels, or rasters depending on the input data types and desired simulation method. ENVI-met uses a 3 dimensional voxel space to define CFD boundary conditions, and a 2 dimensional grid to define ground surface materials. For existing conditions, terrain can be developed from digital elevation models (DEMs), and then classified with landcover data. Buildings can be produced from a combination of publicly available building footprint polygons and aerial LIDAR derived height information. Vegetation can be modeled from voxelization of classified LIDAR data. For proposed conditions, all of these inputs can be modified using standard design tools. 
#### Different data sources
##### GIS data
Hyphae uses GIS data from typical public sources. We compile, edit, and lay out GIS layers using QGIS software and multiple data stores. This GIS environment incorporates CAD source data as well and provides the capacity to manage multiple geographic coordinate systems. We target sub-meter precision for most datasets as our domain grids are as fine as 0.5m cells.
##### Buildings
OpenStreetMaps building footprints datasets are adequate for basic models but we model complex roof planes for both shadow and wind patterns. We use a combination of algorithmic 3D mesh generation, hand modeling to match LiDAR points, and extrusion of 2D roof profiles.  Building volumes are voxelized at simulation time from 2.5D raster representations that include bottom of overhead structures. The raster matrices are generated either by the ENVI-met QGIS plugin or by another open source plugin for McNeel Grasshopper 3D called Morpho and available on github.
##### Landcover data
Landcover, or land use, data is important because of the substantial differences in material properties of various ground surfaces. We derive this information from various pipelines including hand digitization in a GIS environment based on underlays, or importation of landcover analysis products from other data providers. The coverages define both soil profiles and 2.5D vegetation (eg., ground cover or grass) where applicable.
The sensitivity of results to landcover is a question that we have not studied in enough depth for our own satisfaction. However some impact is evident when we differentiate low vegetation, bare earth, concrete, and asphalt. These impacts are mediated by the thermal properties of the materials and consequent effects on convection and re-radiation of thermal energy. ENVI-met also solves for gas exchange with natural surfaces but we have not made use of that feature.
Our underlying input data comes from a few sources: orthorectified aerial imagery supplied by private vendors including Google, Bing and Nearmap, publicly available aerial imagery such as that from USDA NAIP, and satellite imagery from Worldview3 and Pleiades NEO. We have made use of machine learning based land cover classification resources developed in-house, as well as that published by Lassiter & Darbari (2020), and from EarthDefine and may pursue this further. Misclassification is an ongoing challenge, though the impact differs by the context and the grid size. Digitization of imagery must be reviewed with care as orthorectification is sometimes inconsistent or insufficiently precise (for example, Google imagery is often inadequate and must be checked against an independent source and an offset introduced).
We also make use of survey data when it is available to rectify edges of pavement and define materials. As described below, LiDAR is useful for establishing reliable locations of vertical features as well.
To date, we have used prototypical material properties defined by high level review of typical values for attributes like albedo and thermal conductivity. Further refinement or variation of these parameters is a useful area for future examination.
#### <comment_start id=kix.mufbm52kxd33>LiDAR processing<comment_end id=kix.mufbm52kxd33>
LiDAR datasets from publicly available aerial scans are segmented using standardized procedures to yield classified point clouds for terrain, structures, and tree canopy. Geometric measures are calculated using cloudcompare or python and allow further segmentation following metrics such as planarity, linearity and normal change rate. These procedures must be tuned for each point cloud source and some error is inherent when, for example, trees and buildings overlap. As a rule, larger features are more impactful in the simulation and errors under the minimum grid size for the study are of negligible consequence. 
On some projects we have collected our own terrestrial LiDAR datasets using mobile equipment mounted on a vehicle or a backpack. This data is spatially positioned and transformed using simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) algorithms, geolocated with onboard RTK GPS,  and checked against other known geometry and aerial LiDAR (ALS). Positions of vertical features such as fences and walls are readily derived from this data and subsets of point clouds can be overlaid on our project geographic information system using QGIS features.
#### Tree canopy
##### Surveyed trees
We use point location data and spread and height where available from on-site tree surveys to place trees in the domain. For general level of detail we classify them into a small number (3-5) of generalized voxelized models which are stored in a matrix database and gridded at simulation time. For better accuracy and higher detail we use LiDAR point clouds to infer leaf area density voxel grids per specimen and insert these models into the domain. 
##### Raster processing pipeline for tree canopy 
Raster canopy elevation models are a common data input for GIS based analyses and we use this method to define tree outlines and elevations using a watershed algorithm. The input canopy point cloud is converted using a script to a list of specimens with irregular voxel matrices of leaf area density rotated and placed in domain coordinates. Because ALS weights the top of objects, we make assumptions about the bottom of canopy to distribute leaf matter in a vertical profile. Future use of TLS will yield better models of leaf matter distribution within canopy volumes. These algorithms are under development and detailed elsewhere.
To estimate leaf area index  (LAI) for the voxel matrices, we calculate and LAIe from the aerial LIDAR. Methods for LAI estimation from aerial LIDAR data have been comprehensively reviewed by [Wang & Fang (2020)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=oeyW2Q). We use the Beer-Lambert law as demonstrated by [Solberg et al. (2006)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=IQ16bi). [Richardson et al. (2009)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=F57ZQ7) compared this method to others and determined it to be superior. The method was further validated by [Klingberg et al. (2017)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=fMBcsr) by comparing to field measurements in an urban area. We implement the method using python to classify points as canopy and below-canopy which are subdivided into 2m by 2m horizontal cells, yielding pairs of cells containing below-canopy points and and canopy points. The following equation is then employed to yield the LAI estimate (LAIe):
$LAIe\ =\ -\left({\frac{cos(θ)lidar}{0.5}}\right)\times ln\left({\frac{below\ canopy\ \ returns}{below\ canopy\ +\ canopy\ returns}}\right)$e = -&#25;&#25;cos(θ)lidar&#29;0.5&#27;&#27;&#31;ln&#25;&#25;below canopy  returns&#29;below canopy + canopy returns&#27;&#27;&#30;    Equation 1.
(θ)lidar is the mean reported scan-angle of the sensor for each cell. This operation produces 2m2 LAIe, which is rasterized resulting in the georeferenced 2m2 LAI estimates which can be voxelized to produce 3D LAI tree-canopy for ENVI-met simulations.
#### Meshing algorithms (triangular mesh geometry)
We use meshing algorithms to ‘coalesce’ tree canopy point cloud groups into volumes. This input is essential for supporting OpenFOAM simulations which we have conducted for validation of wind field results.
We have completed automated building meshing from LiDAR for level of detail 2 buildings using an algorithm published by Huang et al (2022). We also hand model building facades and roof lines when extrusions are not adequate. Wind fields are highly dependent on large building faces so this is very important.
Triangulation and decimation of ground points from LiDAR or from raster digital elevation models (DEM) are both completed when a terrain is required for the simulation domain. A terrain surface with smooth or sharp features is used to define an elevation matrix which ENVI-core uses as source for gridding and flagging cells as ‘full’ of soil.
### Gridding (CFD meshing)
#### Scales
We commonly simulate horizontal grids at 3 meter, 2 meter, 1 meter, 0.5 meter. Vertical grids are commonly 0.5m, 0.6m, 1m, 1.2m, and numerous others consequent to the vertical telescoping and subdivision routines incorporated into ENVI-met. We have studied 5m, 6m, and 10m horizontal grids but these loose resolution of trees and small buildings. ENVI-met does not support dynamic grids or subdivision around obstacles.
Our domains are typically 50-300 cells per direction with resulting domains as large as a half-kilometer square with headroom to accommodate taller buildings. We truncate buildings vertically in some cases where the height is not of interest to the specific study and the difference is unlikely to impact the wind field in the study area. 
#### Mesh dependency
We do not run a mesh dependency protocol for every study. We have undertaken a mesh dependency study on a prototypical domain which found that the principal dynamics held steady. We are primarily limited in our grid selection by balancing the computational load with the bounds of the landscape dynamics of interest. We are also limited by the size of the geometric features to study, primarily individual trees. We select the smallest grid size that will allow a simulation cycle on the order of hours or 1-2 days rather than 3+ days or weeks. ENVI-met does not support grid sizes smaller than 0.5m, with the exception noted below.
### Meteorology inputs
#### Wind direction sampling
We generalize wind direction and velocity data to find the most common and most salient wind direction for a study. The resulting wind direction is parameterized by the software based on a wind profile with a defined elevation for the typical velocity.
##### From airport (eg., Love Field in Dallas)
We use the [Iowa Environmental Mesonet (<comment_start id=kix.4vjhgn2qr0nd>IEM<comment_end id=kix.4vjhgn2qr0nd>)](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/) website for extracting historical data and generalizing and binning wind directions to find the most common wind direction and speed. The example used most often in Dallas of Southerly wind from 170 degrees represents 25% of the time, which means that 75% of the time the wind direction was not between 160 and 180 degrees but arriving from another direction. For more on this assumption see the section on limitations below.
##### From sensors
We validate our wind parameters and wind fields using on site sensors. This process is in initial phases as we have limited time periods of data to draw from. Preliminary validation of simulation wind results are shown in [the windrose analysis document here](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1GFR9snEvxllbiYxf8D9ramCuGsUZ3Eoujbiqm6OvYTE/edit?usp=sharing). Future simulations may use these insights to derive nested assumptions about the prevailing wind inputs most appropriate for each subdomain.
#### Typical temperature and humidity ranges
We use “simple forcing” for most cases. This means that there is a defined short list of temperature and humidity inputs for each hour of the typical days we are studying. The next level of complexity is termed “full forcing”, and involves using an external data structure for defining meteorological values at every time increment of the simulation.
In general we simulate an ‘averaged extreme’, meaning we characterize the edge of a normal range over the data available. For example, August 1st is typically among the hottest days of the year; 5pm is both the hottest part of a typical summer day and the highest traffic quantity. Given these parameters we then average the typical values to define the inputs. We can then credibly assume that is the worst it gets ‘in general’. This heuristic could be formalized by using standard deviations but so far has been informal.
Values used in Dallas, TX are taken from published EPW files for Love Field and cross-referenced with Weatherspark averages.
#### Typical days to study
We find limiting study to prototypical days of the year to be adequate to form an impression of the range of conditions. The duration of the day is not the critical measure, as the environment continues toward temperature extremes for several weeks after the solstices. Rather than study the solstices and equinoxes we study 4 equally distanced days around the year centered on the most extreme hot days of the year.
### <comment_start id=kix.iknumcah4ngr>Traffic data inputs
#### Vehicle counts
An available mobility study conducted in the neighborhood provides vehicle counts at several times of a given day for several intersections throughout the corridor. We use the real world vehicle counts to profile traffic quantities over time and interpolate hourly vehicle counts from a few times of day using a plausible but theoretical curve for traffic ebbs and flows. We also use given fleet percentages for fractions of light duty and heavy duty vehicles to weight emissions factors to correspond to the location. ENVI-met provides a tool for this procedure but we have take a more in depth approach using available data.
#### Emissions factors
Defining input pollution concentrations based on vehicle counts requires a working method and certain assumptions. Vehicles produce tail emissions, brake and tire dust, and reintroduce road dust into the atmosphere both mechanically and by their wakes. Emissions factors evaluate the quantity of pollution generated per unit of travel. Emissions factors can be recorded under idealized conditions for regulatory purposes or derived experimentally in the wild. Our method took an experimentally calculated typical emissions factor (0.052 g/veh-km for PM2.5) at a tunnel in Los Angeles studied in 1996 by Gillies et al. 2001 as the primary data point. Another study, Vouitsis et al. 2017, laid out values from measurements, models, and a literature review. They set the mass EF for Euro 4 standard cars at a mean of 0.5 mg/km of urban driving: this evidently refers to tailpipe only, but is 1/100th of the value from Gillies. Their maximum for that vehicle class was 2.2. Assuming 10 times as many light duty vehicles and 10% diesel vehicles, the overall EF for urban driving is 2.8mg/veh-km, about 1/20th of the values in Gillies et al. This is in the realm of reason, but it should be noted that Gillies et al. speculated that heavy duty vehicles were responsible for higher than expected pollutants and their exact explanation is inconclusive.
To arrive at a single emission rate for our simulations, the average given in g/vehicle-km is manipulated algebraically along with a locally recorded average value for vehicle counts to calculate the emissions per time increment in mass/distance of road—the units used in ENVI-met linear sources which differ from typical vehicle-km-traveled units.
Because we simulate PM1 and not PM2.5, we applied a fractional constant factor (0.5) assuming half that mass is included in the smaller sieve size. This assumption seems reasonable after review of published particle distributions but it is not authoritative.<comment_end id=kix.iknumcah4ngr>
#### Pollutant characteristics
Particle transport distance and deposition are dependent on particle size. For simplicity we have typically used a single particle size over the course of a study. We are most interested in particle sizes which threaten human health, and research exists to characterize the deposition profile and fall-off for various sizes: plausible extrapolation is therefore possible. Research suggests a direct relationship between fine particle mass and ultrafine particle count is not easily determined while UFP count may be more indicative of health impact. Therefore we have shied from modeling particle diameters below 100 nanometers. These assumptions may limit the validity of the results but we have elected to hold steady particle size for simplicity.
We do not yet vary emission quantities spatially along corridors, for example to increase emissions at intersections. It is technically possible but probably fussy to implement and requires devising classes. While such an exercise would yield insights into the spatial distribution of emissions and the relative mitigation potential of interventions across the study area, for comparing one intervention to another in a given location it may be most expedient to initially assume a typical or worst-case scenario for emissions and hold it steady between intervention design iterations to allow comparison. 
#### General vegetation methodology
A discussion of how we represent vegetation appears above under the digital twin section of the methodology. The key points were that increasing detail is possible with different approaches, from using default or customized generic tree canopy voxel volumes to LiDAR derived individual specimens based on LAI or further specified using mobile derived LAD values. There is some distinction between representation of existing and proposed vegetation. For simulated comparison of vegetation scenarios we include prototypical trees which may vary intracanopy LAD as well as modeling different leaf types (needles vs broadleaf).
We represent proposed vegetation with both 3D and 2.5D matrices. For example, bunch grasses, hedges, or low vegetation are all routinely represented with an extrusion that follows an LAD profile starting at ground level (to date we have kept the profile constant) to a height defined by the coded vegetation object in the database.
Leaf area density varies spatially in our 3D representations. This serves to better model prototypical as well as representational individual plants which are more dense in specific areas. A future effort to expand a database of vetted or experimentally derived tree specimens is anticipated. 
### Output Metrics
#### Thermal comfort - UTCI
Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) is our preferred subjective metric for outdoor thermal comfort. It is common in published studies and incorporates information on wind as well as radiant temperature. We also make use of Physiological Equivalent Temperature (PET) as the software provides functionality to plot dynamic PET over time of travel along a walking path.
#### Mean Radiant Temperature - MRT
MRT is a physical quantity that can be measured using the types of sensors that we have deployed in the corridor. MRT calculation  takes as inputs the globe temperature, air temperature, humidity and wind-speed. Because of the influence of radiant heat on the MRT, MRT varies by a much larger amount between landscape conditions than does air temperature. For example, our pilot studies found the difference in summer afternoon air temperature between a sunny parking lot and a shady spot underneath a grove of large trees to be around 1 degree, while the MRT differed by 16 degrees. This gives MRT a greater dynamic range for assessing the contribution of landscape features to thermal comfort. 
#### Change in particulate concentration
We look at changes in PM in several ways. Our preferred metric measures absolute change to micrograms per cubic meter between two physical configurations or other parameter differences. This requires confidence in the presumptions defining the emission quantities and any traffic input information used. The rationale for attempting an absolute measure of change is as follows: 
  - Quantifiable health and economic impacts
  - Readily falsifiable by presumed future validation
  - Comparable from one scenario to the next
Other approaches we have used are less concrete: normalized values use a scale lying about 1 to show changes without implying real world magnitude; percentages are intuitive but can be deceptive when the baseline value is near zero.
#### Other metrics of use
Wind speed, Turbulence kinetic energy (TKE), Pressure perturbation: these variables are instructive in understanding the air movement which influences the accumulation of particulates and the influence of wind impacting variables on thermal comfort. For example higher wind speeds are cooling in the summer, and also help remove locally produced air pollution, although slower wind speeds can increase the beneficial deposition of particulate pollution on vegetation surfaces.
Particulate Deposition: ENVI-MET calculates the mass quantity of particulates deposited on leaf, ground and building surfaces, and since deposition on leaf surfaces is one of the ways that landscape interventions can improve air quality,  we are interested in using these to calculate  mass balances but have so far not spent much effort on this metric. As our vegetation models become more accurate we may focus on this more.
### Limitations
#### Platform limitations
##### Validation of pollution model
ENVI-met implements the Eulerian dispersion model of suspended particles in the context of the Reynolds Average Navier-Stokes equation. Direct validation studies using sophisticated experiments are few, in part because of the inherent complexity of excluding pollution from external or background sources.
Hyphae has a goal to reproduce a validation study to profile particle transport in a simplified configuration following Steffens, 2013 and Finn, 2010.
##### Particle mass vs. particle number
ENVI-met does not model particle number. For ultrafine size ranges studies have concluded that particle number is the more useful measure for impact on human health.
##### Vehicle Induced Turbulence (VIT)
ENVI-met does not model Vehicle Induced Turbulence (VIT). Because the wind field in these simulations is quite deterministic and small changes to direction and pressure can have strong effects on PM accumulation, it is likely that the results would not be as tidy if there were a strong injection of turbulence at the drive aisles. It is likely that this would generally blur results. 
Our objective is to corroborate our conclusions using another platform that would allow injecting the VIT term into the solver. Using a spot-checking approach would allow validation of ENVI-met results in general, and for VIT, may allow us to experiment with approximating VIT by dividing the source between multiple line sources at several heights above the typical tailpipe height. 
##### Wind direction selection and averaging
The boundary condition does not vary spatially except according to a specific vertical wind profile<comment_start id=kix.xcjgsbjofbqj>.<comment_end id=kix.xcjgsbjofbqj> Full forcing mode has in the latest version (5.x) reduced the limitation of hourly increments.
#### Boundary conditions
##### Out of domain wind obstacles
Domains in urban environments invariable exclude significant wind-shaping obstacles which lie outside the domain. We complete broader scale simulations of wind fields in OpenFOAM or ENVI-met to characterize the wind field and then change the incident wind direction of the nested model to account for these changes. True nesting is not technically possible and is one significant limitation we intend to address by adding alternative methods to our analytical approach. Specifically, we intend to add particle transport to a custom OpenFOAM solver.
##### Spatial variation in incident wind field
##### Edge compression
We often use a bare minimum of empty marginal cells because of the computational load required to complete numerous simulations. Rather than complex nesting grid schemes or large margins we typically treat values as less reliable when they are at the margins of the domain and may crop results to exclude obviously inaccurate edge conditions.
We have observed flows being compressed and less turbulent near edges.
#### Validation
ENVI-met model results for microclimate have been well validated by numerous papers, while the model has been iteratively improved with recent changes to the Mean Radiant Temperature model and the longwave calculations which are important in night-time urban heat island calculation.
Pollution quantities are less well validated as discussed in the literature review section. It is important to note that the underlying mathematical models for Eulerian dispersion have been validated in other software (Ansys Fluent, Steffans, 2013).
See also main literature review section and monitoring for thermal comfort sections above.
Hyphae’s approach is to undertake independent validation study as well as incorporating permanent sensor networks in our design and engineering funding. Due to the high price of reliable air quality sensors, funding has been more difficult to secure for that purpose.
### OpenFoam methods
1.  Introduction 
A computational fluid dynamics (CFD) methodology is proposed to simulate wind flow and pollution transport in an urban environment consisting of buildings, roadway structures, and vegetation. The Reynolds Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) model is used, requiring a turbulence model. The k-epsilon turbulence model is employed. Turbulence effects are represented by including turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) and its dissipation rate (epsilon) as variables in transport equations, which are solved alongside Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations for steady-state, incompressible fluid flow. The RANS equations involve continuity, momentum, and pollutant concentration conservation. Buoyancy effects were not taken into consideration. 
Vegetation is represented as a porous medium to model the effect on the wind field. The presence of vegetation significantly impacts two major atmospheric processes: turbulent mixing and dry deposition of atmospheric constituents. For turbulent flows, turbulent diffusion, due to random particle movement caused by turbulent eddies, should be considered as it is a critical process in the dispersion of pollutants. Dry deposition onto vegetation is a crucial process in removing atmospheric particles, influenced by the deposition velocity, vegetation density, and particle concentration. 
Additionally, vehicle-induced turbulence (VIT) and atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) turbulence impact the dispersion of traffic-related pollutants on and near roadways. VIT refers to the turbulence generated by the movement of vehicles on roadways. These turbulent mixing mechanisms are considered in the proposed formulation. Prior knowledge of the number of vehicles on the road segments (traffic count) is required to calibrate the VIT and pollution emission rates.  Designated roadway emission zones are created to serve as a source of particle emissions and vehicle-induced turbulence (VIT).
A neutral atmospheric boundary layer (NABL) is enforced over the urban area. Weather data is filtered to determine a set of wind configurations to implement. The set of wind and pollution fields are weighted and averaged by considering the frequency of occurrence of each wind configuration. 
1.  Reynolds-averaged equations and turbulence model
The Reynolds-averaged Eulerian formulation is used in this study. The approach consists of solving the Reynolds-averaged continuity and Navier-Stokes equation:
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| $\frac{\partial 〈{u}_{i}〉}{\partial {x}_{i}}=0 $ | (1) |
| $\frac{\partial 〈{u}_{i}〉}{∂t}+〈{u}_{j}〉\frac{\partial 〈{u}_{i}〉 }{\partial {x}_{j}}=-\frac{1}{\rho }\frac{\partial 〈p〉}{\partial {x}_{i}}+\nu \frac{{\partial }^{2}〈{u}_{i}〉}{\partial {x}_{j}^{2}}-\frac{\partial 〈{u}_{i}^{'}{u}_{j}^{'}〉  }{\partial {x}_{j}}+{S}_{i}$ | (2) |

where $〈\ 〉$ indicate Reynolds averaging; $〈{u}_{i}〉$ is the Reynolds-averaged velocity component in the i-th direction; 〈p〉is the mean pressure; ρ is the density of air; $\nu $ is molecular kinematic viscosity; $〈{u}_{i}^{'}{u}_{j}^{'}〉$ is the Reynolds stress; ${S}_{i}$ is a momentum sink term imposed by the vegetation in the i-th direction; ${x}_{i}$ is the coordinate in the i-th direction (i.e., ${x}_{1}$ and ${x}_{2}$ are the horizontal directions, and ${x}_{3}$-vertical direction); $t$ is time. The Reynolds-averaged flow field $〈{u}_{i}〉\ $consists of the wind-driven flow and the large-scale turbulence. Meanwhile, the fluctuating components ${u}_{i}^{'}$ possess the smaller unresolved scales that correspond to the unresolved turbulence.
The Reynolds stress $〈{u}_{i}^{'}{u}_{j}^{'}〉$ in Equation (2) is modeled as:
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| $-〈{u}_{i}^{'}{u}_{j}^{'}〉= {\nu }_{t}\ \left({\frac{\partial 〈{u}_{i}〉 }{\partial {x}_{j}}+\frac{\partial 〈{u}_{j}〉 }{\partial {x}_{i}}}\right)-\frac{2}{3}k{\delta }_{ij}\ $ | (3) |

where ${\delta }_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta function which takes the value of one when subscript $i=j$, and zero otherwise; ${\nu }_{t}$ is the eddy viscosity that is calculated using the standard k-epsilon turbulence model:
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| ${\nu }_{t}={C}_{\mu }\frac{{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon } $ | (4) |

where $k$ is the turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) and $\varepsilon $ the TKE dissipation rate. The standard k-epsilon model (Launder and Spalding, 1974; Versteeg and Malalasekera, 2007) requires solving the transport equations for $k$ and $\varepsilon $:
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| $\frac{∂k}{∂t}+〈{u}_{j}〉\frac{∂k }{\partial {x}_{j}}=\frac{\partial }{\partial {x}_{j}}\left[{\left({\nu +\frac{{\nu }_{t}}{{\sigma }_{k}}}\right)\frac{∂k}{\partial {x}_{j}}}\right]+{G}_{k}-ε+{S}_{k}$ | (5) |
| $\frac{\partial \varepsilon }{∂t}+〈{u}_{j}〉\frac{\partial \varepsilon }{\partial {x}_{j}}=\frac{\partial }{\partial {x}_{j}}\left[{\left({\nu +\frac{{\nu }_{t}}{{\sigma }_{\varepsilon }}}\right)\frac{\partial \varepsilon }{\partial {x}_{j}}}\right]+{{C}_{1\varepsilon  }\frac{\varepsilon }{k}G}_{k}+{C}_{2\varepsilon  }\frac{{\varepsilon }^{2}}{k}$ | (6) |
| ${G}_{k}=-〈{u}_{i}^{'}{u}_{j}^{'}〉\frac{\partial 〈{u}_{i}〉 }{\partial {x}_{j}}$ | (7) |

where ${G}_{k}$ is the TKE production composed of production by mean shear; ${S}_{k}$ and ${S}_{\varepsilon }$ are turbulent source/sink terms accounting for vehicle induced turbulence (VIT). The model constants (${C}_{\mu }$, ${\sigma }_{k}$, ${\sigma }_{\varepsilon }$, ${C}_{1\varepsilon  }$, and ${C}_{2\varepsilon  })$ in Equations (5), (6), and (7) have the following values ${C}_{\mu }=0.09$, ${\sigma }_{k}=1$, ${\sigma }_{\varepsilon }=1.3$, ${C}_{1\varepsilon  }=1.44$ and ${C}_{2\varepsilon  }=1.92$ (Wilcox, 1994). 
1.  Vegetation Model
The Darcy-Forchheimer model has been implemented in recent CFD studies (Kastner and Dogan, 2020; 2022) of urban areas to capture the effects of vegetation on fluid flow (i.e., model considers a momentum sink term imposed by the vegetation). The Darcy-Forchheimer model treats the tree as a porous medium. The Darcy component primarily accounts for viscous resistance to flow (represented by shear forces) through a porous medium under laminar flow conditions; the Forchheimer component extends this representation to include additional pressure losses (due to factors such as inertia, turbulence, and flow non-uniformity) that become significant under turbulent (non-laminar) flow conditions. This model defines ${S}_{i}$ the momentum sink term imposed by the vegetation as:
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| ${S}_{i}=-ν{D}_{i}〈{u}_{i}〉-0.5\ {F}_{i}U〈{u}_{i}〉 $ | (8) |

where ρ and $\nu $ is the density$\ $and the kinematic viscosity of air; $〈{u}_{i}〉$ is the local velocity in the i-th direction; $U$ is the magnitude of the velocity; ${D}_{i}$ and ${F}_{i}$ correspond to the Darcy and Forchheimer coefficient in the i-th direction. These coefficients are direction dependent. Measured data from wind resistance experiments for different vegetation types (e.g., Falkenstein-Smith et al., 2019) is required to calculate ${D}_{i}$ and ${F}_{i}$ (Koch et al., 2020; Kastner and Dogan, 2022). Koch (2020) reports that ${D}_{i}$ is sensitive to plant species, while ${F}_{i}$ is not. Note that the additional pressure losses captured by the Forchheimer term can be considered analogous to the drag force experienced by the fluid. When fluid flows through a porous medium, such as around obstacles like tree branches and leaves, the fluid experiences resistance, which can be related to drag.
Alternative vegetation models consist of momentum and turbulent source terms (Liu et al., 1996; Sogachev et al., 2006). The momentum source is defined in terms of leaf area density and drag coefficient. The turbulence model considers the additional turbulence generated by vegetation and the turbulence destruction caused by the cascade from larger shear-induced eddies to smaller and weaker eddies (Liu et al., 1996). It is recommended to include sources for turbulent kinetic energy (k) and TKE dissipation.
1.  Vehicle Induced Turbulence (VIT) Model 
Previous studies (Hashad et al., 2022; Tong et al., 2015; Steffens et al., 2012) have utilized a method (Baumer et al., 2004) that implicitly models vehicle-induced turbulence (VIT) in a roadway. The TKE method requires creating a traffic zone representative of the traffic lane(s). TKE is injected into the zone to model the turbulence generated by vehicle motion over the traffic lane(s). This approach reduces the mesh resolution and computational cost. The size of the zone influences the magnitude of TKE produced in the traffic zone. Thus, it is crucial to consider the number and size of the vehicles in the roadway when selecting the traffic zone size (width, length, height). 
Hashad et al. (2022) propose categorizing vehicles into three categories (passenger cars, medium-duty vehicles, and heavy-duty vehicles). Each vehicle category contributes differently due to differences in size, shape, and moving speed. TKE contributions per vehicle category (${S}_{i}\ $) are calculated by:
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| ${S}_{i}\ =\ \frac{1}{{V}_{T}}{\ \left({U\ F}\right)}_{i}$ | (9) |
| ${F}_{i\ }=0.5\ \rho {C}_{d\ }A{U}^{2}$ | (10) |

where VT is the volume of the traffic zone, U is the speed of the vehicle, F is the mean drag force, $\ \rho $ is the density of air, ${C}_{d\ }$ is the drag coefficient, and A is the frontal area of the vehicle. Note that subscript i indicates the vehicle category (PC indicates passenger car, MD medium-duty, and HD heavy-duty). To calculate the total TKE contribution, the number of vehicles for each category ${N}_{i}$ over the traffic lane(s) must be determined:
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| ${N}_{i}=N\ \times \ {P}_{i}$ | (11) |

where $N$ is the total number of vehicles on the roadway and ${P}_{i}$ is the percentage breakdown for each vehicle category.  The total TKE production in the traffic zone is calculated by the sum of contributions from each vehicle category:
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| ${S}_{k}\ =\ {N}_{PC}\ {S}_{PC\ }+{N}_{MD}\ {S}_{MD\ }+{N}_{HD}\ {S}_{HD\ }$ | (12) |

where ${N}_{PC}$ is the total number of passenger cars and ${S}_{PC\ }$ is the TKE contribution per passenger car. Note that this method requires prior knowledge of the number vehicles on the road segment and percentage of each vehicle category. 
To determine the average number of vehicles on a road segment at any given time the following information is needed: hourly traffic count, the speed of moving vehicles, and the length of the roadway. The relationship between traffic count (vehicles per time, TC), average speed of moving traffic (U), and traffic density (vehicles per unit road length, TD) is used.
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| $TD\ =\frac{TC}{U}$ | (13) |

Convert the moving speed into consistent units if necessary (e.g., if speed is in miles per hour, convert it to meters per hour or keep it consistent with the unit for length of the roadway). Finally, calculate the average number of vehicles ($N$) present on the specified length of the roadway ($L$).
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| $N\ =\ TD\ \times \ L$ | (14) |

Note that if hourly traffic count is provided, one may obtain an average number of vehicles and VIT for each hour of the day. Additionally, if the pollution emission factor (quantity of pollutant per vehicle per distance traveled) is known, the pollution emission rate (quantity of pollutant per second) over the roadway length may be obtained.
1.  Neutral Atmospheric Boundary Layer (NABL)  
The boundary condition suggested by Richards and Hoxey (1993) is used for an atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) profile: 
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| $u=\frac{{u}_{*}}{\kappa }ln\left({\frac{z+{z}_{0}}{{z}_{0}}}\right) $ | (15) |
| $k=\frac{{u}_{*}^{2}}{\sqrt{{C}_{\mu }}}$ | (16) |
| $\varepsilon =\frac{{u}_{*}^{3}}{\kappa \left({z+{z}_{0}}\right)} $ | (17) |

where $z$ is the distance from the ground; $\kappa $ is the von Karmen constant; ${u}_{*}$ is the friction velocity; ${z}_{0}$ is the roughness length. Weather data (i.e., ${u}_{ref},\ \ {z}_{ref},\ $and ${z}_{0}$) is obtained to set the ABL profile. Richards and Hoxey (1993) found that (12)-(14) are satisfied when ${\sigma }_{\varepsilon }=1.11$. Blocken et al. (2015) recommend distinguishing the different roughness zones in the domain. For example, ${z}_{0}$ of 1 m corresponds to a suburban environment (Blocken et al., 2015; Kastner and Dogan, 2022); ${z}_{0}$ 0.03 m corresponds to an environment with low vegetation (Blocken et al., 2015). 
To produce suitable near-wall turbulence modeling, the wall/ground adjacent cell height (Δz) is recommended to satisfy Δz \~ 10 z0 (Zhang, 2009; Martinez, 2010) for ABL, where z0 is the roughness length. This general rule compromises the cell size constraints and the need to resolve the near-wall flow. Note that it is not physically meaningful to have grid cells within the roughness length z0. Also, having a very large ground adjacent cell height compared to the roughness length is not recommended because it leads to poor resolution of near-wall flow features, inaccurate representation of boundary layer dynamics, and may not adequately capture the turbulence dynamics near the surface. 
1.  Weather Data Filtering 
A wind analysis is proposed to determine the set of dominant wind configurations that are used in the study. Note that computational cost and time constraints should be considered in determining the total number of configurations. Several factors should be considered to ensure a comprehensive representation of atmospheric conditions. An initial step is to analyze historical weather data for the study area over a significant period, such as 20 years. This analysis involves examining the frequency distribution of wind directions and speeds to identify the most common and relevant conditions.
It is important to account for seasonal variations in wind patterns. Wind directions and intensities can vary throughout the year due to seasonal changes in weather patterns. Therefore, understanding these variations is vital for accurately representing the different wind conditions that may occur in different seasons. Identifying dominant wind directions is another critical aspect. This involves determining the predominant wind directions based on their frequency of occurrence in the historical weather data. Both prevailing wind directions, which are the most frequent, and secondary wind directions, which can also significantly impact local airflow patterns, should be considered.
Conducting model sensitivity analyses may be considered. This involves running simulations with contrasting wind directions and intensities to assess the sensitivity of the results to variations in these parameters. The goal is to evaluate how changes in wind conditions can affect airflow patterns and pollutant dispersion, ensuring that the model accurately reflects potential scenarios.
Finally, stakeholder input should be considered. Input from stakeholders or regulatory agencies involved can provide valuable insights, as they may have specific concerns or priorities regarding certain wind directions. Engaging with these stakeholders helps ensure that the selected wind directions align with regulatory requirements and local priorities.
1.  Weighted Averaging Approach  
A simple way to average a set of steady-state solutions with different wind configurations is to use weighted averaging based on the frequency of occurrence of each wind configuration. The frequency of occurrence for each wind direction is determined by sampling weather data over a period of time, as outlined in the proposed wind configuration analysis.
Each simulation is given a weight corresponding to the frequency of occurrence of the respective wind configuration. The weighted sum of the scalar solution (e.g., pollution concentration) is calculated at all grid locations from the set of steady-state simulations using the previously determined weights. Finally, the weighted average solution is obtained by dividing the weighted sum by the total weight (sum of weights).
This weighted averaging approach ensures that the resulting average solution reflects the combined influence of different wind directions, with each direction weighted according to its frequency of occurrence in the historical weather data.
1.  Pollutant Dispersion Model 
A passive scalar transport solver based on the scalar-advection diffusion equation is implemented. The following assumptions are considered: (1) the tracer is passive (i.e., follows the flow field); (2) the steady-state flow field is held static; (3) background pollution is neglected and only one pollutant is considered; (4) The pollutant is released at a fixed rate. The advection-diffusion equation is used to simulate the movement of dispersed entities (pollutants). The steady-state advection-diffusion equation for concentration is expressed as:
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| $\frac{\partial }{\partial {x}_{i}}\left({〈{u}_{i}〉\ {C}_{p}}\right)-\frac{\partial }{\partial {x}_{i}}\left({{D}_{t}\frac{\partial {C}_{p}}{\partial {x}_{i}}}\right)={S}_{p}+{S}_{dep}$ | (18) |

where ${C}_{p}$ is the concentration of the dispersed entity; and $〈{u}_{i}〉$ is the local flow velocity component in the i-th direction. The transport of the dispersed entity due to turbulence is modeled with the eddy diffusivity term ${D}_{t}={\nu }_{t}\ /\ {S}_{c}$ ; where ${\nu }_{t}$ is the turbulent (eddy) viscosity, and ${S}_{c}$ is the turbulent Schmidt number. ${S}_{p}$ is the source term representing a fixed rate of pollutant emissions. The roadway emission zones are used as a source of particle emissions.
Deposition may be considered in the model. The dry deposition model proposed by Zhang et al. (2001) is implemented. A sink term is included in the advection-diffusion equation to represent the effect of deposition. 
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| ${S}_{dep}\ =\ -LAD\ {V}_{d\ }\ {C}_{p}$ | (19) |

where LAD is the leaf area density, Vd  is the dry deposition velocity, and C the concentration of pollution. The dry deposition velocity ${V}_{d\ }$  is expressed as: 
|  |  |
| :- | :- |
| ${V}_{d\ }=\ {V}_{g\ }+\ 1/{R}_{s}$ | (20) |

where Vg  is the gravitational settling velocity and ${R}_{s}$ the surface resistance. ${R}_{s}$ depends on the collection efficiency of the surface and is determined by the various deposition processes, the size of the depositing particles, atmospheric conditions and surface properties.
1.  Computational Setup 
The CFD workflow proposed by Kastner and Dogan (2020) is followed. A cylindrical computational mesh for the background mesh was implemented. This allows for the mesh to be reused with different wind directions (Kastner and Dogan, 2020). The computational domain inlet, outlet, and top faces should be located far enough from the urban region to avoid the boundary influence. Kastner and Dogan (2020) recommend a domain size of height 6H and a clearance of 15H between the inlet/outlet boundaries and the region of interest. H is the height of the tallest building. This constraint will allow the inflow atmospheric boundary layer to develop fully before reaching the urban area. It also enables the development of the full wake between the urban area and outflow. 
The mesh is created with two OpenFOAM utilities: (1) blockMesh to define the background cylindrical mesh of purely hexes and (2) snappyHexMesh to add the terrain and buildings/structures by creating a mesh containing hexahedra (hex) and split-hexahedra (split-hex) from triangulated surface geometries in Stereolithography (STL) format. In RANS simulations, certain regions, such as those around complex geometries like urban areas or specific areas of interest (AOIs), may require further mesh refinement to capture important flow characteristics accurately. The refinement regions may be defined by cylinder, box, or custom shape (STL file is required) that enclose the urban region or the area of interest (AOI). 
The following mesh settings are highlighted: (1) block size for background cylindrical mesh; (2) surface refinement level for buildings and terrain; (3) refinement region shape (box, cylinder or STL), size, location, and refinement level; (4) number of cells between refinement levels; (5) number of layers around buildings, first layer height, and expansion ratio. Note that a refinement level of i corresponds to a cell size of 1/2i of the block size. For example, a refinement level of 3 corresponds to a cell size of 1/8 the block size. Creating a precise mesh at the intersection of building and terrain was an issue requiring extensive manual model cleanup; this issue was also reported by Kastner and Dogan (2022). 
According to guidelines for CFD simulations of urban environments (Franke and Baklanov, 2007), the height of the first cell should be a few decimeters from the wall (ground) for atmospheric boundary layer modeling in urban areas. For studies of pedestrian-level wind speed, a height of 1.5 – 2 m should coincide with at least the 4th cell above ground level. They advise using at least 10 cells per cube root of the building volume and 10 cells in between every two buildings. A grid-convergence study needs to analyze the overall resolution. The appropriate grid resolution specification heavily depends on the chosen turbulence model and corresponding wall function. The resolution near the walls is determined by the non-dimensional distance y+ and wall roughness. There are restrictions for all models near the walls with respect to resolution. The minimum cell size near buildings and the ground needs to be different due to the larger roughness height of the ground for atmospheric flows. Therefore, defining the appropriate grid is not straightforward.
The domain inlet was set to an atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) profile for $u$, $k$, and $\varepsilon $. At the outlet boundary, constant pressure is assumed, and other variables are assumed to be zero-gradient. The top of the domain is set as a symmetry boundary for all variables. The ground and the buildings use the same boundary conditions: a no-slip condition for velocity, a zero-gradient condition for the pressure, and wall functions for $k$ and $\varepsilon $. For turbulent viscosity ${\nu }_{t}$, the Spalding wall function is applied to buildings, which constrains the turbulent viscosity based on the velocity. This wall function suits low- and high-Reynolds number turbulence models. On the other hand, the ground implements a boundary condition that imposes a constraint on the turbulent viscosity based on the turbulent kinetic energy (k) specifically for atmospheric boundary layer modeling. 
Eddy3D (Kastner and Dogan, 2020; 2022) is used to output the Darcy and Forchheimer coefficient vectors for each tree based on a selection between a coarse and fine tree definition. Kastner and Dogan report that the model requires specific vegetation measurements and point out the lack of such input data. 
## **<comment_start id=kix.3sedyjbt3crq>Design Process, Collaboration and Iteration<comment_end id=kix.3sedyjbt3crq>**
The monitoring and modeling procedures outlined in this method directly inform the basis of design toward stated outcomes established in concert with the primary landscape design team. We describe here a protocol for close collaboration with design and engineering efforts that aims to reliably connect design decision making to environmental modeling results. This involves stages of work beginning with bringing all model inputs up to date (including monitoring data, surveys, outputs from the engineering team, and current assumptions of the designers), then establishing baseline conditions, setting performance benchmarks, and finally making predictions about the performance of various design proposals. The targeted outcome supported by this process will be design schematics that are vetted through computational simulations for their predicted environmental metrics.
### Stages of Collaboration
#### Baseline Model
The fidelity of the digital twin depends on reliable integration of the latest drawings from the civil engineers as well as documentation of existing conditions, including tree survey. Any changes to the traffic alignment and edges of pavement are especially important for simulating corridor scenarios. We often start preliminary models using publicly available data but make use of monitoring data, traffic counts and improved surveys as they are delivered. Quantifying results depends on maintaining a baseline model as a reference scenario that is up to date and as accurate as possible.
#### Benchmarking, goals and studies
To characterize the range of possible outcomes we develop benchmarking simulations: for example on the impact of a maximal planting scenario, investigating evergreens vs deciduous and small vs large trees. These benchmarks will be useful because the impact of detailed design scenarios can be compared to reference impact ranges. This stage allows the core team and other research partners to engage in a practical, achievable goal-setting exercise that establishes performance metrics in accordance with some of the previous modeling, current literature and expert input. Our process prioritizes design charrettes which can be instrumental in orienting the team around these goals. These open-ended collaborative brain-storming sessions also provide an opportunity to set intermediate goals as well as grander research objectives.
#### Design evaluation
We propose a choreographed collaboration schedule to avoid one team waiting for the other. The exact sequence is to be refined as part of the collaboration.  For example, the design team begins by prioritizing work on the best defined and highest priority geographic areas. In some cases we have already generated preliminary models that can be of use. During this period, we complete baseline models at broader scale.
When certain design options and typical sections are drafted we can begin modeling them while the design team shifts attention to other segments of the project. When their designs are ready for modeling we have results for the first group of designs to pass back to them for iteration.
We repeat this hand-off procedure again and then recapitulate our goal-setting exercise for any useful changes made clear during the work to date.
We provide interim recommendations based on the first course of simulations in each condition. These will be sketches and design recommendations as well as analytical exhibits. Additionally, we often jump to another topic to run quick study models on pressing questions as needed.
#### Design optimization
As designs take shape, we revise our models based on iterations provided to us by the design team and refine our queries to target the particular dynamics interesting to the team. We provide results in preliminary/raw form along with revised sketch recommendations for the designers to incorporate in their schematic design documents. These sketches might be scaled hand drawings, 3D massing models, illustrated sections, or any format which is most effective in communicating an intervention typology.
#### Impact Estimation of Final 30% Design Development Plans
A third round of simulations is necessary to project the impact of the final design decisions at both the corridor and the intersection scale. The results of this are used to formulate targets for future longitudinal studies in future project phases.
#### Design Development
Subsequent to the submittal of 30% design, in our experience, there are still many decisions to be made that can significantly affect the performance of the design. First, value engineering from the 30% set requires redesign and strategic decision making. Additionally, as projects get bid and built, many changes occur, for example, in plant species and size availability which also affects the design and the performance.
We make ourselves available for responding to Requests for Information with clarification, drawings, models, or additional simulation as requested. We will stay engaged in order to review designs before each submittal for fidelity to the agreed performance objectives.

# Conclusions
The Southwestern Medical District’s Green Spine Streetscape and Park intervention has the potential to become a national model of evidence-based design, revealing how targeted landscape strategies can improve both environmental conditions and public health. Through a rigorous process of data-gathering, simulation, and on-site monitoring, this project has already demonstrated how carefully placed large-canopy trees, vegetated buffers, and strategic landscape configurations can substantially reduce thermal stress while potentially mitigating traffic-related air pollution. Early findings underscore the outsized influence of mature canopy trees on lowering mean radiant temperature, the importance of thoughtful species selection for air quality and biodiversity benefits, and the potential of carefully placed vegetation in improving pedestrian and cyclist thermal comfort.  When integrated into a cohesive framework for collaboration by connecting local stakeholders, designers, engineers, planners, and researchers, these insights can guide decision-making at every stage of design and implementation.
Challenges remain that will require ongoing refinement and validation. Air pollution modeling, especially for particulate matter, is highly sensitive to meteorological inputs, background pollution levels, and microclimatic variables, underscoring the need for more robust measurement campaigns and higher-resolution simulations. Similarly, future studies should incorporate updated LiDAR data and improved sensor geolocation to refine thermal comfort projections and confirm the true magnitude of vegetation’s cooling benefits. By advancing to a second phase of monitoring, one that embraces additional air quality sensors, acoustic biodiversity monitors, and deeper human health analyses, the project team can strengthen causal links between built-environment interventions and measurable health outcomes such as asthma rates, heat-related illness, cardiovascular risk, cancer, and psychological well-being.
Alongside quantifiable metrics for thermal comfort and pollution, biodiversity stands out as another emerging performance indicator. Preliminary evidence suggests that increasing species and structural diversity of urban vegetation may support broader ecological and health benefits, from reducing inflammatory disease risks to increasing urban wildlife habitat. Bridging these findings with larger-scale efforts to connect green spaces and create ecological corridors can help anchor this district in Dallas’s ambitions for resilience, ecological restoration, and equitable public health outcomes.
Above all, the iterative nature of the work, which is grounded in sensor data, validated simulations, and ongoing stakeholder feedback, positions the Southwestern Medical District as an evolving “living laboratory.” By continually refining methods, hypotheses, and design details, the project can establish clear precedents for other urban environments grappling with extreme heat, high traffic volumes, and uneven green space distribution. As this initiative transitions into later phases of construction and post-construction monitoring, its principal value lies in translating local innovations into scalable best practices. By documenting the lessons learned and further validating the positive impacts of well-planned green infrastructure, the Dallas SWMD project will underscore how evidence-based design can serve as a powerful tool for creating healthier, more sustainable cities.
