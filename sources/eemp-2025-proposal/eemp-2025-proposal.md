### **I. Summary of Key Discussion Points**
#### **A. Caltrans Coordination & RTF Eligibility**
  - **Prescott Area of I-880** identified as a viable planting zone. Caltrans confirmed that the **addition of express lanes within the last 10–30 years** satisfies the **Required Transportation Facility (RTF)** criterion for the EEM grant.  
  - **Secondary justification**: Recent installation of an **Ethernet cable** project along the corridor may also qualify as an eligible transportation improvement.  
  - **Caltrans biologist referred Courtney to appropriate contacts** to draft the official RTF support letter.  
#### **B. CEQA Documentation & Requirements**
  - Initial **mitigation is already complete**, qualifying the project as “**above and beyond**” mitigation.  
  - Courtney will follow up with Caltrans to obtain **existing CEQA documents** tied to the express lane project.  
  - The grant also requires a formal **State Clearinghouse submission** indicating that CEQA is not triggered.  
  - Brent pointed Max to past documents, including **template letters **from the earlier Climate Resolve/Boyle Heights project that included a LoS from Caltrans and the City of LA  
#### **C. Maintenance Requirements and Constraints**
  - The EEM grant **cannot fund long-term maintenance** or **establishment endowments**.  
  - However, early maintenance may be permissible as **“establishment maintenance”** within the grant period (through 2030).  
  - Brent proposed a **contractor-based model**: including 2–3 years of establishment maintenance in the initial contractor agreement, with **Caltrans potentially taking over long-term maintenance** thereafter.  
  - Courtney is awaiting **written confirmation** from Caltrans regarding their willingness to assume maintenance responsibility long term.  
#### **D. Budget and Cost Cap Breakdown**
  - **Max award for development projects**: $750,000.  
  - Key budget caps:  
      - **25% cap** on **non-construction** (design, outreach, permitting)  
      - **15% cap** on **indirect costs** (e.g. administrative overhead)  
      - **10% cap** on **contingency  
        **
  - Brent clarified:  
      - “Indirect” = organizational overhead  
      - “Non-construction” = soft costs like design, permitting, project management  
#### **E. Design Strategy and Constraints**
  - Caltrans imposes a **strict 15-gallon limit** on tree size for this grant. Even **donated trees** over 15 gallons are ineligible.  
  - Discussion explored **strategically using 15-gallon trees** in layered designs or interim infill areas.  
  - A larger planting area may be shown on the plans, but only **trees meeting the criteria** will be counted toward the grant.  
#### **F. Timeline and Deliverables**
  - **Application deadline**: July 15, 2025  
  - **Site visits** for top proposals: Sept–Oct 2025  
  - **Grant funds must be spent** by March 1, 2030  
  - Concept: complete initial planting and maintenance contracts **early in the grant period**, to frontload costs and meet the establishment maintenance window.  
  - RFP is multi-stage; many documents (e.g., **site control letters**) may not be required until **later steps**.  
#### **G. Role Clarification and Next Steps**
  - **RCD will serve as Project Manager**, Hyphae as **Design & Technical Lead**.  
  - Courtney requested a **checklist with assigned tasks**.  
  - Mei and Max to review draft design plans for the Prescott area and identify necessary revisions or additions.  
  - Clarified that site control letters may be needed **only for Caltrans ROW**, not for adjacent city parcels—decision made to **focus solely on Caltrans ROW** for simplicity and maintenance eligibility.

# Budget

<comment_start id=kix.js4k2j7rhpys>[EEMP 2025](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1jTuj-yMgDNpT6Qdu-m1dWleSoYZpOFD7j0NN2tp9JmY/edit?gid=1530453488#gid=1530453488)<comment_end id=kix.js4k2j7rhpys>
To support long-term viability, the budget is structured to reflect a **seven-year maintenance horizon**, aligning with Caltrans’ typical operational assumptions and site stewardship cycles. While EEM funds may only cover **two to three years of establishment maintenance**, the full seven-year cost framework is included to convey the **true scope of ongoing maintenance needs** and to facilitate strategic handoff planning. This approach also provides a transparent foundation for future maintenance commitments by Caltrans or other partners. By frontloading contractor-based establishment work within the grant period and clearly delineating projected costs beyond year three, the budget positions the project for sustained success while remaining compliant with EEM’s restriction on long-term maintenance funding.

Section entitled: Hyphae integrates aerial, ground-level, and public health data  
Description
HDL facilitates integration of LMR, VIP, CCV, and Leap data collection effort with ongoing integration of UC Merced’s and JPL’s ECOSTRESS data sets. Hyphae, UCM and JPL will perform statistical analysis on thermal imagery data, ground sensor data, other remote sensing data, and the health data from local hospitals. This will include pre-post studies of the built-environment interventions, and, where applicable, cross-sectional analysis of built environment features.
Deliverables/Milestones
As sensor data is generated, the resulting data points (on radiant temperature, wind speed, air temperature, humidity, and activity spaces) will be mapped in a spatial time-series database, which will also ingest JPL and UC Merced spatial data on surface temperature and health outcomes/hospital admissions respectively. As the data is populated, the integrated dataset will be used to provide actionable insights for the intervention process. The data will be used as inputs for simulations of sun, shade, and re-radiation of heat from materials. These simulations will be used to estimate heat stress in chosen places and times, and to determine optimal activity spaces (coolest routes at each time of day per season). Surface temperature data and local sensor data will be combined with heat-stress indices to develop a comprehensive method for estimating heat-stress exposure of locations at different times of day and year. This will be combined with health data from local hospitals to estimate the impacts of heat on health outcomes and how much those impacts have been mitigated by the completed built-environment interventions in task 5.  
Section entitled: Hyphae gathers spatial data for the digital twin
Description
HDL gathers aerial LiDAR date for chosen neighborhoods in preparation for modeling digital twins that represent the area's heat stress in hyperlocal detail.  
Deliverables/Milestones
This results in 3 dimensional spatial data on terrain, buildings and vegetation, with leaf area density and land cover. This data will be ground-truthed and corrected by community members.
Section entitled: Hyphae prepares a digital twin and runs simulations
Description
Combining multiple layers of data: JPL’s aerial imagery, and the ground-level monitoring done by LMR, VIP, CCV, and Leap, HDL designs a digital twin of the chosen neighborhoods using the AdaptOS platform. For each of the 8 communities, a digital twin (a 3D computer model of buildings, vegetation and terrain) will be created. The digital twin will be run through environmental simulations of wind flow and sunlight paths to create estimates of universal thermal comfort index (UTCI) for the entire space on representative hot summer days and nights, and dynamic physiological equivalent temperature (dPET)  for common pedestrian routes identified by the community. The models will then be modified to represent proposed interventions on the built environment by the community. These proposed intervention's will then be run through the same simulations, and the relative merits of different design decisions (in the heat stress context) will be quantified, compared and contrasted to facilitate evidence-based design optimization. 
Deliverables/Milestones
The major outcomes of this activity will be 3 dimensional models of the terrain, depicting buildings and vegetation of the targeted neighborhoods at at meter scale, and subsequent simulation results. Meter-scale land cover analyis as well as three-dimensional leaf area density estimations will provide a digital twin that can be used for computational fluid dynamics simulations of wind flow, as well as radiation simulations of thermal absorption and emissions. Various design iterations developed by the community-driven design process will be subjected to environmental simulations, which will provide metrics of their performance for heat-stress mitigation. This will enable the community to make evidence-based design decisions and create interventions that are maximally effective or have the greatest ratio of benefit to cost.
Section entitled “Hyphae designs an extreme heat related app or outreach tool” (Good material on UFMP)
Description
“HDL designs a routing and outreach app for extreme heat events. Our proposal for the app, subject to community review, is as follows. It first alerts residents about imminent extreme heat events. Then, during the heat wave itself, the app generates shade maps that guide users down the coolest routes to their destination: schools, worksites, community cooling centers, and other sensitive receptor sites. App design will use existing geospatial and user-interface libraries to enable users to leverage project database and backend algorithms for making use of sensor data, remote sensing data, modeling & simulation results, as well as network analysis tools. The database will be populated with sensor data, remote sensing metrics, and simulation results. Meanwhile, the backend algorithms will use network analysis to provide the user with routes optimized for thermal comfort based on the user's inputs of origins, destinations, outdoor activity type, and location data.”
Deliverables/Milestones
Development on the app begins Day 1, but with increasingly sophisticated versions deployed from months 12 - 30 as community needs are refined and integrated into app design. The app is designed to alert the community in the event of an extreme heat event, proposing optimal routes to sensitive receptor sites such as schools, community centers, churches, and designated cooling centers. This will include metrics of the cooling potential of existing and proposed greenspace, shade, wind, and facilities, and will use network analytics to direct people to utilize paths and locations with the minimal possible heat stress.  The simulations behind the tool will use local weather forecasts to provide forcing inputs for simulations of heat stress that take advantage of 3 dimensional digital twins of the area. The digital twins are put through simulation process based on the Urban Multi-scale Environmental Predictor (UMEP) tool, which calculates meter-scale mean radiant temperature and wind-velocity, while taking into consideration  time of day and year, the effects of buildings, vegetation, terrain and material properties on the wind flow,  sunlight and radiation. The results of the simulation are meter-scale maps of hot and cool zones, which are then fed into a network analysis algorithm that calculates the shortest path weighted by heat-stress between an origin and destination.  The app will enable community members to minimize their cumulative heat exposure by helping to plan routes and timing of outdoor activities to favor the lowest heat-exposing decisions.  These simulations are computationally intensive, and so will only be available in selected pilot neighborhoods at first. During this pilot period, other neighborhoods will still be able to use the tool, but with a less computationally intensive model, which uses heuristics based on Google-Streetview derived view indices, which calculate skyview index and shade cover by analysis of low-cost Google-Streetview imagery. In both cases, the routing recommendations are based on simulations or heuristics, which present an ethical concern if not backed-up by validation and ground truthing. We will use community deployed sensor networks, and remote sensing thermal imaging to validate and ground truth the simulations and heuristics. These methods of the modeling and validation cycle will then be subject to peer review in the scientific community to ensure validity and rigor. After a period of usage of the app, it will then be possible to discern its impact on health outcomes using hospital admissions data analyzed by the University of Merced affiliated team members.

C. Overview
Fresno County faces an unprecedented challenge from extreme heat that disproportionately affects its most vulnerable residents. In 2023 alone, the county experienced more than 100 days where temperatures exceeded 100°F. Climate projections indicate this trend will intensify  the annual average maximum temperature is expected to rise by 5.3°F under a medium emission scenario, or 8.7°F under a high emission scenario by century's end. The number of extreme heat days (above 94.3°F) is projected to increase from 5 days per year to 43 days under a medium emissions scenario, and to 66 days under a high emissions scenario.
These impacts are not distributed equally. Over 55% of census tracts in Fresno County rank above the 75th percentile on CalEnviroScreen 4.0, indicating communities burdened by multiple environmental challenges including poverty, air pollution, and health inequities. These factors make residents particularly vulnerable to heatrelated illnesses. Many residents lack adequate cooling resources  the average income is 25% below the state average, and over 58% live in lowincome communities where residents may struggle to afford cooling units or adequate housing insulation.
Our approach to the Fresno County Extreme Heat Analysis recognizes that effective planning requires both rigorous technical analysis and deep community engagement. While existing heat awareness tools provide general guidelines with broad countylevel data, they often lack the granularity needed for targeted interventions. Tools like CalAdapt use downscaled climate projections at a 3mile resolution  sometimes representing entire cities as single data points. Our analysis will provide the highresolution data needed to identify vulnerable hotspots and design effective mitigation strategies.

**Community Needs and Priorities:**
Though each of the subcontracted CEJC partners fights its own unique
pollution challenges, we are all united in the fight against extreme heat. In
order to adequately assess the vulnerability of our respective communities,
however, we require data that has enough granularity to a) pinpoint high
risk areas and b) correlate that vulnerability with health outcomes. With this
hyperlocal data provided by our technical partners, we can then direct
decisionmakers to implement projects, codes, standards, regulations, and
policies that protect our populations from extreme heat.
Based on a combination of remote sensing data, environmental
simulations, and communityplaced heat stress sensors, the community will
use Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform to generate meterscale thermal stress
metrics. These calculations first integrate 70 meter regional ECOSTRESS
satellite surface temperature data, weather data, and GoogleStreetview
derived skyview/shadefactor imagery for each region. Based on this heat
data, as well as on an assessment of sensitive receptors and mitigation
potential, CEJC’s collaborative process produces a firstlevel prioritization
of target neighborhoods. HDL will then generate a digital twin from LIDAR
data at the 1 meter scale, which will enable hyperlocal environmental
simulations of heat stress in the target neighborhoods. These metrics are
generated iteratively as the community discusses prioritization and
mitigation strategies to adaptively optimize the priority locations based on
heat stress potential (temperature, population, sensitive receptors),
mitigation potential (suitability for addition of cooling infrastructure or route
planning), and logistics (mitigation costs). Community members will place
thermal stress sensors in the target neighborhoods to validate the
simulations. A cohort of volunteers will also be outfitted with mobile sensors
to track thermal stress throughout the activity spaces of the community.
Trusted community leaders then circulate through the chosen
neighborhoods to confirm that the gathering places of its most vulnerable
inhabitants are carefully mapped out and included in the models.

**e. Community Engagement Plan Introduction **
AB 617, California’s landmark environmental justice bill of 2017, mandated the implementation of communityled air quality monitoring and emissions reduction programs, placing environmental data and scientific tools into the hands of community organizers that had formerly been handled exclusively by experts. Long before AB 617, however, Hyphae Design Laboratory, founded by Brent Bucknum in 2008, had been practicing this collaborative, communitydriven, evidencebased form of environmental justice advocacy. Hyphae has provided technical assistance to hundreds of local communitybased organizations in collecting data, implementing environmental design solutions, and advocating for their communities before state regulatory agencies. For almost fifteen years, from West Oakland to Stockton, Los Angeles, and the country at large, their partners have fought to address unfair pollution burdens faced by lowincome communities and communities of color. By coordinating activists, technical assistants, academic researchers, air districts, city governments, and federal agencies, they have transformed their environments and achieved significant policy changes in pollution reduction, green infrastructure, and climate resilience. Using hyperlocal mapping and measurement tools developed with funding from the National Science Foundation and the National Institute of Health, Hyphae helps CBOs identify air, soil, and water contamination in their communities, as well as other risks to human health such as extreme heat. These groups then deploy Hyphae’s softwarebased adaptOS platform to simulate, design, and implement environmental mitigation solutions in response. Such interventions range from urban greening to truckrerouting to the design of software applications protecting sensitive receptors during extreme heat events. A persistent weakness hampers the optimal implementation of their combined environmental justice vision, however: the lack of reliable modeling tools. Although lowcoast commercial air sensors can now be reliably calibrated by colocating them with reference monitors, estimating the state of a dynamic system from a series of noisy measurements using techniques like Kalman filtering still lags. Furthermore, the integration of Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) tools for reliable urban airflow modeling and simulation is not yet fully developed, hindering the accurate prediction and analysis of air quality in urban environments. University of South Florida (USF), in collaboration with its contracted partner Hyphae, submits this proposal to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to address these critical methodological gaps. Hyphae’s methods, combining outreach with technical expertise, have already secured for CBOs an empirical foundation from which to advocate for fair regulations, standards, and codes. But without adequate urban air quality modeling and estimation tools, communities and air districts cannot effectively assess the costs and benefits of given interventions. That weakness slows down environmental remediation at all stages of the process, and casts doubt on our collective capacity to resolve our present climate crisis. By redressing this computational liability, the EPA stands to expand California’s participatory environmental justice approach to a nationwide scale, thus extending the State’s legacy of leading the nation in the formation of ecological policy. Our partnership structure in this proposed project unites Hyphae’s oldest partners, the West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project (WOEIP) with newer collaborators in Little Manila Rising (Stockton, California) and Climate Resolve (Boyle Heights, Los Angeles). All three organizations actively monitor air quality with inhouse sensor networks, united in their goal to effect lasting policy changes through their local air districts and the California Air Resources Board (CARB). The EPA can rely on the Hyphae coalition’s proven track record of addressing unfair pollution burdens to achieve regulatory goals. Together with regulatory agencies, air districts, city governments, and CARB, this Hyphaeassisted coalition is now implementing a multiyear, multimilliondollar urban planning endeavor that combines vegetated barriers, road dieting, and truck rerouting in the Prescott neighborhood of West Oakland. In addition to the Prescott project, Hyphae and WOEIP are also collaborating on West Oakland’s Sustainable Transportation Equity Project (STEP), another CARB initiative aimed at protecting residents from pollution by reducing transportation emissions. Hyphae Design Laboratory approaches urban forestry and green infrastructure through the lens of environmental justice and community empowerment. While conventional urban forest master plans provide valuable highlevel guidance, we specialize in bridging the critical gap between broad planning documents and actual community implementation. Our experience has shown that the greatest challenges often lie not in the technical planning, but in translating these plans into actionable, communityled initiatives that create lasting impact.
Our approach fundamentally reimagines how tree planting and green infrastructure projects are implemented in environmental justice communities. Rather than following the standard model of filling individual tree wells—which requires extensive outreach for minimal return and often stretches community capacity thin—we identify strategic, highimpact opportunities that maximize environmental benefits while minimizing administrative burden. By focusing on locations such as freeway corridors, school zones, and public parks, we can achieve significant tree canopy expansion through fewer administrative touchpoints, allowing communities to plant hundreds of trees through agreements with just a few key stakeholders rather than seeking approval from hundreds of individual property owners.
Central to our methodology is a commitment to building local economic capacity alongside environmental improvements. Instead of centralizing all project management and implementation within nonprofit organizations, we help communities identify and engage local, minorityowned landscaping businesses to perform the work. This approach not only ensures more efficient project execution but also keeps resources within the community, creating a sustainable model where local businesses become longterm partners in maintaining and expanding urban forestry initiatives. When community members see their neighbors and local businesses leading implementation, it strengthens buyin and ensures more effective longterm stewardship.
Our work extends beyond traditional urban forestry to address the diverse environmental justice challenges facing communities. Drawing from our extensive experience with farmworker communities, warehouse districts, and industrial zones, we understand that effective green infrastructure must be tailored to specific community contexts. This might mean designing vegetative barriers for air quality near warehouses, creating protective greenery near pesticideimpacted areas, or developing innovative solutions for extreme heat mitigation. Our tools and approaches are built from the ground up to address these varied challenges, ensuring that each project not only increases green space but also meaningfully addresses critical environmental health concerns.
Through years of handson experience in communities from Oakland to Los Angeles, we've developed a comprehensive strategy that emphasizes coalition building, maintenance planning, and longterm stewardship alongside initial implementation. By focusing on strategic locations, building local capacity, and maintaining a steadfast commitment to environmental justice principles, we help communities transform highlevel plans into tangible environmental improvements that build both ecological and economic resilience.

**Expertise in Urban Forestry Activities**
Hyphae Design Lab has a strong track record in urban forestry, leveraging expertise in tree inventories, canopy assessments, site preparation, tree planting, and maintenance. We approach each project with a comprehensive understanding of the technical, environmental, and community aspects of urban greening.
**1. Tree Inventory and Canopy Assessment**
  - **Tree Inventory:** We use advanced tools like **TreePlotter** and **ArcGIS Field Maps** to conduct tree inventories, tracking species, health, location, and age of existing trees in urban settings.
  - **Canopy Assessment:** By utilizing **LiDAR** data, including **Leaf Area Index (LAI)** and **Leaf Area Density (LAD)**, we assess canopy cover, which helps prioritize planting areas and measure potential environmental benefits like air quality improvement and temperature reduction.
**2. Site Preparation**
  - **Site Evaluation:** We assess urban sites for tree planting suitability, reviewing soil quality, available space, infrastructure, and potential challenges such as existing utilities. This is aligned with city ordinances (e.g., 811 guidelines) and environmental regulations.
  - **Preparation:** We manage soil preparation, including excavation for **tree wells**, and ensure compliance with city ordinances to ensure longterm tree health. Our team coordinates with municipal agencies to ensure the tree planting follows all regulations.
**3. Tree Planting and Maintenance**
  - **Tree Planting:** Our ISAcertified arborists are trained in proper tree planting techniques, accommodating various types of nursery stock including **ball and burlap**, **container**, and **bare root** trees. We conduct **planting demonstrations** and emphasize the importance of watering, mulching, and pruning.
  - **Maintenance and Stewardship:** We create customized **maintenance plans** for tree stewardship, which outline how trees will be cared for in the long term. These plans are designed to empower community members and organizations to take on stewardship responsibilities.
**4. Workforce Development and Community Engagement**
  - **Youth Training Programs:** We have created **paid youth tree programs** targeting high school and college students from local communities. ### Project Vision
The vision of the California Environmental Justice Coalition is to form a
communityled research observatory and task force capable of monitoring
and mitigating the deadly effects of extreme heat in the Central and
Imperial valleys. Our project, the Communityled Microclimate Observation
Network (COMMON), aims to transform these regions into models of
climate resilience, and brings a unique focus on farmworkerpopulated
agricultural areas that are often left out of extreme heat remediation efforts
directed towards cities.
In this endeavor, CEJC has partnered with environmental justice member
organizations with proven records of championing their community’s health
and environment. These organizations, supported by technical partners,
stand to make a historic intervention in the field by correlating hyperlocal
extreme heat data with population and hospital data that will provide
updated heathealth intervention measures, provide better outreach and
early warning systems, and an empirical basis for updating codes,
standards, and regulations that presently fail to protect vulnerable
populations from extreme heat.
As COMMON is designed not only to monitor but also to actively respond
to extreme heat, both with emergency outreach tools and climate
adaptation solutions, its goals of equity, community leadership, and
scientific rigor are in clear alignment with those of the EHCRP.
### Project Description
COMMON aims to enhance the climate resilience of the San Joaquin and
Imperial Valleys with communitydriven solutions. The following 30month
implementation timeline combines communityled outreach, sensor
installation, data collection, software development, and mitigation solutions.
Year 1: Coalitionbuilding and Citizen Science
 CEJC and Partners conduct rigorous community outreach campaigns
across the San Joaquin and Imperial valleys to identify heatvulnerable
neighborhoods.
 Simultaneously, technical Partners from NASA’s Jet Propulsion Lab (JPL),
Hyphae Design Labs (HDL), and UC Merced (UCM) model the area’s
potential highrisk environments. High resolution modeling, usable within
first year of project, provides communities with data to choose where to
deploy sensors, conduct simulations, and plot mitigation strategies. HDL
then trains monitoring technicians of respective Partners on assembly and
installation of open source thermal stress sensors, as well as in use of
Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform to collect and interpret the data.
Year 2 and 3: Immediate Use of Data & Sensor Deployment
 HDL develops an extreme heat early warning and outreach mobile app
capable of alerting residents about heat events and guiding users to
cooling centers and sensitive receptor sites, avoiding hotspots along the
way.
 By Month 24, Partners and HDL have installed sensor network in selected
neighborhoods,
 Partners assist HDL, JPL and UCM in providing communitydriven data
overlay of community’s primary walking and transit routes, gathering
places, and sensitive receptors.
 Hyphae creates high resolution digital twins of geographical areas
prioritized by communities.
 Partners and community finalize targeted greening sites with Tree Fresno.
Greening Projects and Adaptation Reporting
 In year three, installed pilot greening projects are outfitted with sensors to
monitor impact on local microclimates and evaluate their effectiveness as
natural cooling measures.
 Project culminates in report detailing outcomes/efficacy of interventions,
community feedback, and models for expanding COMMON to other regions.

### Community Needs and Priorities:
Though each of the subcontracted CEJC partners fights its own unique
pollution challenges, we are all united in the fight against extreme heat. In
order to adequately assess the vulnerability of our respective communities,
however, we require data that has enough granularity to a) pinpoint high
risk areas and b) correlate that vulnerability with health outcomes. With this
hyperlocal data provided by our technical partners, we can then direct
decisionmakers to implement projects, codes, standards, regulations, and
policies that protect our populations from extreme heat.
Based on a combination of remote sensing data, environmental
simulations, and communityplaced heat stress sensors, the community will
use Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform to generate meterscale thermal stress
metrics. These calculations first integrate 70 meter regional ECOSTRESS
satellite surface temperature data, weather data, and GoogleStreetview

data, as well as on an assessment of sensitive receptors and mitigation
potential, CEJC’s collaborative process produces a firstlevel prioritization

simulations of heat stress in the target neighborhoods. These metrics are
generated iteratively as the community discusses prioritization and

simulations. A cohort of volunteers will also be outfitted with mobile sensors

neighborhoods to confirm that the gathering places of its most vulnerable
inhabitants are carefully mapped out and included in the models.

### Extreme Heat Conditions and Vulnerability:
If greenhouse gas emissions continue to rise at current rates, the
temperature in downtown Fresno will hit 106.6 fahrenheit 43 times a year.
Classified as Heat Health Events (HHEs), studies have predicted that
these spikes in heat could last up to two weeks long in our region (Thorne
et al., 2018). Adapting to these conditions and durations will require large
scale targeted neighborhood community interventions.Though the risk to
urban environments is wellknown, COMMON also seeks to draw attention
to the less widely understood Rural Heat Island Effect (RHIE).
There exists an abundance of federal, state and local public health
information on extreme heat from fact sheets, websites, and checklists, but
these sources often provide only general guidelines, with broad county
level data. Tools like Caladapt use downscaled daily CMIP5 Climate
Projections but are also at a resolution of 3 miles, so entire cities are
sometimes represented as a single data point. Moreover, existing heat
warning metrics that measure only temperature and humidity fail to
accurately record critical thermal comfort variables such as wind flow,
radiant temperature and land use, that significantly drive heat stress.
These tools also lack critical, subjective, community driven data of how and
where people live, work, gather and move. Therefore the existing heat
awareness tools are inadequate to target impacts and generate effective
solutions. To more accurately warn the public about heat risk, microclimate
variables need to be integrated into community models forecasting and
response services also need to be updated to take into account diverse
populations. Community driven population data can also help improve
healthcare response to target the ripple effect, through which extreme heat
renders the public more vulnerable to mental health emergencies and
chronic health conditions from migraine and rosacea, to lupus and
rheumatoid arthritis, cardiovascular disease, respiratory distress, and
kidney failure.

## Material from EHCRP Interview Process 
The project's technical approach represents a significant advancement beyond conventional heat mapping methods, addressing the fundamental limitation of traditional data sources like EcoStress and CalAdapt that lack sufficient resolution for communitylevel action. The approach integrates multiple sophisticated data sources, beginning with Local Climate Zone (LCZ) classification that provides detailed meteorological land use categories spanning farmland, city areas, and varying densities of subdivisions. This classification is enhanced by two innovative population mapping systems: Harvard's Popgrid, which utilizes Microsoft's building data layer to distribute population estimates at the building level rather than census tract, and RTI's synthetic populations system, which applies mathematical approaches to distribute demographic data with unprecedented precision.
The heart of the technical approach lies in its sophisticated modeling methodology. The team builds detailed simulation models for each neighborhood, incorporating wind data to calculate thermal comfort metrics. The Universal Thermal Climate Index measurements integrate air temperature, radiant temperature, humidity, and wind data to create a comprehensive understanding of how temperature is actually experienced by residents. This approach achieves square meterlevel resolution, producing something akin to wet bulb temperature calculations but at a much more granular level than previously possible. These models are specifically calibrated for and run in each focus community.

The models are validated through strategically placed sensors that measure temperature, humidity, and globe temperature, creating wet bulb globe temperature equivalents at the meter level.

Models are developed in continuous dialogue with communities, incorporating hyperlocal demographic and behavioral input  such as identifying frequently used bus stops or gathering spaces. This integration helps prioritize intervention locations and provides immediately actionable information for community members. The approach effectively avoids the paralysis often induced by traditional "doom and gloom" heat mapping by identifying specific, achievable improvements.
