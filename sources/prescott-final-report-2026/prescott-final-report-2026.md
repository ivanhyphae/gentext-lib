# Introduction
## Project Goal
The Prescott Project was a greening initiative funded by the Metropolitan Transportation Commission (MTC) and the California Air Resources Board (CARB). Led by West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project (WOEIP) with technical support from Hyphae Design Laboratory, it used air quality modeling, monitoring, and evidence-based design to improve human and environmental health in West Oakland. To achieve these goals, partners first measured emissions and exposure risks, and then used that data to model and design greening interventions.
\[image\]
# Background
## West Oakland and AB617
West Oakland still experiences disproportionately high levels of air pollution, more than three times higher than the Bay Area average. And areas within the Prescott neighborhood remain pollution hotspots, with levels of particulate matter 150 to 300% higher than West Oakland's average. In 2018, West Oakland was thus identified as a first-year priority in the state’s Community Air Protection Program under Assembly Bill 617. AB 617, California's landmark environmental justice bill of 2017, mandated the implementation of community-led air quality monitoring and emissions reduction programs, placing environmental data and scientific tools that had formerly been handled exclusively by experts into the hands of community organizers. West Oakland was chosen as a pilot neighborhood for a forthcoming Community Air Action Plan, with WOEIP, supported by Hyphae, leading some of the first community-led emissions and exposure reduction programs in the state.
WOEIP and the Bay Area Air Quality Management District (BAAQMD), the regional agency implementing AB617, then co-developed *Owning Our Air: The West Oakland Community Action Plan* (WOCAP). The plan identified 89 strategies to reduce pollution sources and limit resident exposure. Formally approved by BAAQMD, the California Air Resources Board (CARB), and the Oakland City Council in 2019, the Plan was implemented by a diverse Steering Committee of residents, businesses, community organizations, and government agencies. The wider coalition of stakeholders included tree-planting organizations, youth education groups, neighborhood associations, climate resilience activists, local churches, and schools.
\[image\]
The work aimed to actualize the community-led visions articulated in WOCAP. While source reduction—limiting pollution at its origin—remains the primary long-term strategy for improving air quality, such interventions require years of regulatory and infrastructural change. In the interim, urban greening offered a complementary approach to more immediately reduce resident exposure to existing pollution. Viable greening strategies included vegetated buffers and green walls composed of plant species proven effective at intercepting, capturing, or redirecting particulate matter before it reached residential areas, with the City of Oakland designated as a lead implementation authority.
Strategy \#12 of WOCAP explicitly called for the Air District and WOEIP to implement green infrastructure between Interstate I-880 and the Prescott neighborhood. Strategy \#10 directed the City to develop a comprehensive urban canopy and vegetation plan, identifying locations where trees could be added and maintained—including along Caltrans rights-of-way—and to protect existing trees that reduce air pollution exposure in West Oakland. Strategy \#16 tasked the City, in partnership with the WOCAP Steering Committee, CARB, and the Air District, with studying the exposure reduction benefits of requiring solid or vegetative barriers between buildings and pollution sources.
These efforts aligned with Oakland's broader climate resilience planning as well. The City's 2030 Equitable Climate Action Plan (ECAP), a roadmap to equitably reduce emissions and adapt to climate change, referenced WOCAP multiple times and incorporated several of its strategies, including Strategy \#10's urban canopy provisions.
The Metropolitan Transportation Commission (MTC) and the Alameda County Transportation Commission (ACTC) funded the project, bringing regional coordination and countywide transportation planning expertise to this community-led environmental justice intervention. Coordination with the concurrent West Oakland Sustainable Transportation Equity Project (WOSTEP) provided additional planting resources. WOSTEP, implemented through a partnership between the City of Oakland, the Oakland Parks and Recreation Foundation, and Urban Biofilter (a collaboration between Hyphae and WOEIP), focused on tree planting throughout West Oakland. OakDOT, serving as WOSTEP project manager, provided trees from that grant for use in the Prescott Project planting areas.
## Prescott Greening Projects
\[image\]
Multiple potential planting sites adjacent to the Prescott neighborhood were identified for a phased greening strategy. The I-880 freeway, a major contributor to particulate pollution in the neighborhood, presented several intervention opportunities. Preliminary modeling showed that planting buffers in the Caltrans right-of-way could effectively intercept air pollution before it reached residential areas, and initial concept designs were developed for this long-term intervention. Similarly, a "road diet" proposal for Frontage Road—reducing lane width to create space for a large vegetated buffer—was modeled using Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) as a potential future phase.
For immediate implementation, the team prioritized an existing planting area along Frontage Road within the City of Oakland's right-of-way. This site offered several advantages: open soil was already available, an irrigation system existed (though non-functional), and no extensive construction or complex permitting would be required. The area already contained some trees, but many were in poor condition. CFD modeling confirmed that revitalizing this space—repairing the irrigation system, replacing declining trees, and infilling gaps—would create an effective buffer against pollution from the adjacent freeway and high volumes of truck traffic on Frontage Road itself. The models indicated that a vegetated buffer in this location could effectively reduce the concentration of particulate matter reaching downwind neighborhoods.
\[image\]
## Using Vegetated Buffers for Air Quality
### **Scientific Rationale**
Beyond the aesthetic and temperature regulation functions of tree canopy, compelling evidence suggests that trees can also purify the quality of the air. A 2013 study published in the American Journal of Preventative Medicine, "The Relationship Between Trees and Human Health: Evidence from the Spread of the Emerald Ash Borer," demonstrated this connection at population scale. The Emerald Ash Borer, an invasive insect from Asia whose larvae kill ash trees, led to the loss of over 100 million trees since its 2002 detection in the United States. Researchers analyzed data across 15 states and found that counties where more trees died showed increased deaths from lower respiratory and cardiovascular causes. This suggested that the trees themselves had been providing health protection: their absence was associated with more than 20,000 additional deaths in affected areas.
This evidence informed the development of vegetated barriers as a targeted air quality intervention. Vegetated barriers—dense plantings positioned near pollution sources—can function as physical buffers that intercept particulate matter. The EPA has studied roadside vegetated barriers by placing sensors before and behind different vegetation types to identify design characteristics that improve pollution interception.
\[image\]
### **Design Principles for Effective Buffers**
EPA research identified several key characteristics for effective vegetated barriers. An effective barrier must be continuous, with minimal gaps, as openings allow particulate matter to pass through unimpeded. The barrier should extend long enough that wind cannot easily carry pollution around it, and should ideally reach at least 13 feet in height. Trees must have branching that extends to ground level, or be supplemented with shrubs to eliminate lower gaps. Buffer thickness also matters as denser plantings create more opportunities for particle deposition.
\[image\]\[image\]
\[image\]
Placement relative to wind patterns and pollution sources also significantly affects performance. Barriers work most effectively when positioned downwind of pollution sources, where prevailing winds carry particulates toward the vegetation. Computer models show that particulates disperse into the upper atmosphere as they move away from roadways, making them progressively harder to capture. Proximity to the pollution source thus improves interception efficiency substantially.
\[image\]
\[image\]
This consideration held particular relevance for West Oakland, where streets and highways generated 50% of total PM2.5 emissions according to the community-scale emissions inventory documented in the West Oakland Community Action Plan. Roadside vegetated buffers therefore had potential to intercept a major local emissions source.
\[image\]
*Figure from Owning Our Air: West Oakland Community Action Plan*
### **Plant Selection Criteria**
A barrier’s performance is heavily dependent on correct tree species selection. Trees function more effectively than solid barriers such as walls because leaves provide substantially higher surface area, both slowing air movement and creating more surface for particulate deposition. The key metric employed by researchers and designers is Leaf area density (LAD), the surface area of foliage per unit volume. Species with dense, clustered foliage intercept more pollution than those with open branching patterns. Trees with thick, needle-like leaves offer higher overall surface area than those with flat, simple leaves. 
\[image\]
Evergreen species are recommended for their higher leaf area density than broadleaf deciduous trees. They also offer year-round protection, with the ability to intercept pollution through the winter months. Tree form also matters: shade trees with high, spreading canopies can trap pollution near ground level rather than blocking its movement toward pedestrians, while trees with branching that extends closer to the ground provide more effective barriers.
\[image\]\[image\]
### **Shrubs and other vegetation **
Supplementary vegetation enhances buffer effectiveness. Shrubs, large grasses, and groundcovers fill gaps beneath tree canopies and add visual interest. Where trees cannot be planted due to space constraints or restrictions, dense hedges serve as alternatives. Vines on existing walls improve barrier performance beyond what walls provide alone.
In summary, the optimal vegetated buffer combines these characteristics: 
  - continuous length
  - substantial height (minimum 13 feet)
  - low porosity 
  - downwind positioning close to pollution sources
  - high leaf area density evergreen species 
Additional considerations include: 
  - dense branching structure
  - year-round foliage retention
  - Longevity
  - low maintenance requirements
# Computational Fluid Dynamics Modeling
## Scientific Rationale
Before implementing costly greening interventions, we modeled potential planting areas to test whether vegetated buffers would effectively reduce pollution exposure. A model is a mathematical way of predicting outcomes using available information about physical conditions. The most familiar example is weather prediction: meteorologists input data about solar intensity, topography, wind patterns, and evaporation to generate forecasts. While weather predictions aren't perfectly accurate, they provide useful guidance for decision-making.
Modeling pollution movement cannot replace direct measurement, but it enables testing scenarios that would be impractical or impossible to measure in advance. We used computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling to evaluate whether vegetated buffers in different locations would improve air quality before committing resources to construction.
## Digital Twin Development
\[image\]
The modeling process began with creating a “digital twin,” or three-dimensional computer model of the physical environment. Hyphae combined aerial LiDAR data with ground-based LiDAR scans from car-mounted and backpack-mounted scanners to generate a comprehensive 3D map of the Prescott neighborhood. Existing aerial LiDAR, captured from above, provided detailed roof topography, while our street-level scanning captured building facades and existing tree canopy. This multi-perspective approach ensured accurate representation of buildings, the West Oakland BART track, bridges, and vegetation.
\[image\]
*The aerial LiDAR is shot from the sky, and so is most useful for the tops of structures like roofs.*
\[image\]
\[image\]\[image\]
The resulting LiDAR scans were then simplified into a computationally manageable digital twin. Even at this reduced complexity, running these simulations required several days of processing time per scenario.
## Pollution Source Characterization
Accurate pollution inputs were critical to obtaining reliable model results. BAAQMD used the EPA’s AERMOD dispersion model to predict how pollution from local sources moves across the city and region. Their team conducted extensive inventories of pollution sources and thoroughly vetted emission estimates. Hyphae then used BAAQMD's AERMOD data to assign estimated pollution output to road segments throughout the study area.
BAAQMD's city-scale model, however, operated at a different resolution than needed for neighborhood-level intervention planning. While their regional model effectively identified pollution hotspots, it couldn't capture block-scale phenomena such as wind blocking by buildings or pollution interception by vegetation. We therefore used BAAQMD's validated emission data as a foundation but applied it within a finer-scale CFD model that reflected these local conditions. Critically, the BAAQMD model excluded vegetation, which was precisely what we needed to test.
\[image\]
### **Wind Field Mapping**
Wind patterns determine how effectively positioned buffers intercept pollution. We used data from wind monitoring stations throughout Oakland to characterize prevailing conditions. In general, Oakland's winds move from west or southwest toward the east. This information was used to generate wind field maps showing how air moves around buildings and trees at the neighborhood scale.
\[image\]
*Left wind stations. Right wind field map*
The image on the right of the figure above is a wind field map. The darker areas behind buildings show where the wind is blocked and causing the air to move more slowly, while the lighter areas show faster airflow. Arrows indicate wind direction, revealing the complex patterns that emerge from urban topography.
### **Simulation Scenarios**
With the digital twin, pollution sources, and wind fields established, we could simulate how air carries pollution through the neighborhood. The first simulations modeled existing conditions with current vegetation to establish a baseline for comparison. We then created simplified representations of vegetated buffers for each potential planting area and tested them using ENVI-met software. Each simulation revealed where adding a vegetated buffer would improve or worsen air quality relative to baseline conditions.
\[image\]
*Left Existing Vegetation. Right Vegetated Buffers Added to Digital Twin*

## Plant List
#### **Plant Choice Guidelines**
#### Project Goal:
Create a plan for a [vegetated buffer planting](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1tbX-e2s6zlMFRVrww-UFN6iMp5c7Nck3dYizGdrmF0o/edit?tab=t.0#heading=h.q5en5crb4y8n) along the frontage road, with the ultimate aim of intercepting near road air pollution. 
#### Design Brief Goal:
Provide Alana with specific plant recommendations for each zone, based on the conditions that we've defined. I am aiming for 5-6 trees and 5-10 shrubs/groundcover for each zone. Each zone will have different tree recommendations from the other zones, so overall biodiversity of the planting will remain high, even if there is repetition in the individual zones.
#### Planting/Design Priorities:
  - **Thick buffer**- plantings should be designed so that they will create the least amount of gaps. That means that an ideal planting will include:
      - Trees with branching that goes down to the ground/ not limbed up
      - Evergreen trees that don’t lose their leaves
      - Trees planted so that their branches overlap at maturity
      - Trees with thick leaf foliage rather than open branching
      - Evergreen shrubs planted at the base of trees to close off possible gaps at the bottom
  - **Ease of maintenance**- Plants should be chosen that require little maintenance. Plants should be planted in a regular enough pattern as to make maintenance easier for people not familiar with the original project plan.
  - **Biodiversity**- Plantings should have biodiversity in each zone, and across the entire planting in order to discourage disease and pest related issues. 
  - **Planting combinations**- when possible, with all of the above priorities being met, plants should be combined either because they are beautiful, or because they often grow together in natural settings and thus benefit from being planted in community, or both. Native plants that can benefit local pollinators, birds, and insects is also a priority.

### **Political Context and Community Tensions**
The project's air quality modeling revealed findings that complicated the neighborhood's longstanding environmental justice campaigns. For many years, community meetings had focused heavily on truck traffic along Frontage Road. Our data (**from field measurements or modeling?**) revealed that the majority of particulate matter pollution originated from Interstate 880, however, not from the street itself. And since Caltrans' highway right-of-way proved too constrained for vegetated buffer installation, Frontage Rd and its wide right-of-way provided the only viable space to intercept pollution drifting over from the freeway. The road diet on Frontage Rd combined with the mitigation strategy, therefore. By reducing traffic lanes to create space for planting, a sufficiently dense and wide barrier could be planted to protect residents from freeway emissions.
These findings emerged against a backdrop of two decades of land use battles in the Lower Bottoms neighborhood, a historically black community. Ms. Margaret Gordon and WOEIP had spent decades opposing residential development of the post-industrial land bordering on Frontage Rd, arguing that these defunct industrial parcels should remain undeveloped due to their proximity to I-880's pollution plume. They had simultaneously advocated for designating Frontage Rd as a formal truck route, part of a broader truck management plan that would confine heavy vehicles to major arteries and protect the internal residential streets of the Lower Bottoms neighborhood from pollution and other safety concerns. WOEIP lost that fight, and the City converted those parcels closest to the highway to mixed-use buildings housing young professionals (as well as some affordable housing units and homeless transition services). The new condo development occupied space that could have been dedicated to planting a barrier that would protect the Lower Bottoms residents from pollution. Meanwhile, the new residents, many of them white and affluent, began lobbying against designating Frontage Rd a truck route. (**So this is where the road diet concept comes in? Because the yuppies already want to constrain the space?**)    
The pollution source findings added another layer of complexity. The modeling (**modeling or field measurements?**) demonstrated that particulate matter originated predominantly from brake and tire wear from passenger vehicles on I-880 and not from truck exhaust. This finding aligned with emerging research on non-exhaust emissions, which have increased with the adoption of heavier electric vehicles as tailpipe emissions, including from trucks, have declined significantly over the past decade due to stricter regulations. The decades-long focus on truck routes, while important for addressing noise, diesel particulate, and traffic safety, had not been calibrated to address the primary source of harmful particulate matter: the friction between tires, brakes, and roadways generated by high-volume freeway traffic.

**Zone Selection and Elimination Process**
The Prescott project's immediately plantable area underwent significant reduction from initial plans due to jurisdictional, budgetary, and logistical constraints. The team originally identified multiple planting zones along both the eastern and western sides of Frontage Road. However, several factors led to the elimination of most proposed areas:
**Eliminated Zones:**
The area behind the sound wall was excluded early due to jurisdictional issues—it fell under a different city department's purview, requiring a separate permitting process beyond the project's scope.
The zone across the street on the western side of Frontage Road presented complex jurisdictional challenges. The boundary between Caltrans and City land remained unclear and did not align with existing fencing. Though this area technically fell within City jurisdiction following a 13-year-old agreement transferring the land from Caltrans, the existing irrigation infrastructure had been installed by Caltrans prior to the transfer. Most critically, extending water service across the street proved prohibitively expensive given the project's $400,000 construction budget.
Similarly, the area in front of an old train station along Frontage Road was eliminated because routing water under the road would require extensive permitting and costs exceeding available resources.
Zone 1 (the area above the trench extending along the sound wall toward 7th and Frontage) was eliminated late in the planning process. Gordon Matasa from Oakland's Tree Services Department rejected this zone because the sound wall increases significantly in height toward 7th Street, making ongoing tree maintenance impractical for City staff.
**Retained Zones:**
Zone 2 consists of an area with existing redwoods in poor condition, which will be removed and replaced with more suitable species. This zone retained access to existing water connections.
Zone 3 encompasses a large area in front of apartment buildings with established trees and shrubs. Rather than extensive new plantings, this zone will receive strategic infill planting to enhance the existing vegetation. Water access is available at this location.
Both retained zones benefit from existing water infrastructure, avoiding the permitting complexities and costs associated with establishing new service connections.

**West Oakland Sustainable Transportation Equity Project (WOSTEP)**
The STEP project is being implemented through a partnership between the City of Oakland, the Oakland Parks and Rec Foundation, and Urban Biofilter (a partnership between Hyphae Design Laboratory and West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project) to do tree planting in West Oakland. West Oakland experiences disproportionately high levels of air pollution, more than three times higher than the Bay Area average. Areas within the Prescott neighborhood are hotspots of pollution, with levels of particulate matter 150 to 300% higher than West Oakland's average. In 2018, West Oakland was thus identified as a first-year priority in a state Community Air Protection Program under Assembly Bill 617. AB 617, California's landmark environmental justice bill of 2017, mandated the implementation of community-led air quality monitoring and emissions reduction programs, placing environmental data and scientific tools into the hands of community organizers that had formerly been handled exclusively by experts. West Oakland was chosen as a pilot neighborhood for a forthcoming Community Air Action Plan, with WOEIP, supported by Hyphae, leading some of the first community-led emissions and exposure reduction programs in the state. 
WOEIP and the Bay Area Air Quality Management District (BAAQMD), the regional agency implementing AB617, then published *Owning Our Air: The West Oakland Community Action Plan* (WOCAP). The plan identified 89 strategies to reduce pollution sources and limit resident exposure. Formally approved by BAAQMD, the California Air Resources Board (CARB), and the Oakland City Council in 2019, the Plan was implemented by a diverse Steering Committee of residents, businesses, community organizations, and government agencies. The wider coalition of stakeholders included tree-planting organizations, youth education groups, neighborhood associations, climate resilience activists, local churches, and schools.
The Prescott Project aimed to actualize the community-led visions articulated in the WOCAP. While source reduction—limiting pollution at its origin—remains the primary long-term strategy for improving air quality, such interventions require years of regulatory and infrastructural change. In the interim, urban greening offered a complementary approach to more immediately reduce resident exposure to existing pollution. Viable greening strategies included vegetated buffers and green walls composed of plant species proven effective at intercepting, capturing, or redirecting particulate matter before it reaches residential areas.
The Prescott Project directly fulfilled multiple strategies identified in the West Oakland Community Action Plan, with the City of Oakland designated as a lead implementation authority. Strategy \#12 explicitly called for the Air District and WOEIP to implement green infrastructure between Interstate I-880 and the Prescott neighborhood. Strategy \#10 directed the City to develop a comprehensive urban canopy and vegetation plan, identifying locations where trees could be added and maintained—including along Caltrans rights-of-way—and to protect existing trees that reduce air pollution exposure in West Oakland. Strategy \#16 tasked the City, in partnership with the WOCAP Steering Committee, CARB, and the Air District, with studying the exposure reduction benefits of requiring solid or vegetative barriers between buildings and pollution sources.
These efforts aligned with Oakland's broader climate resilience planning as well. The City's 2030 Equitable Climate Action Plan (ECAP) and its roadmap to equitably reduce emissions and adapt to climate change, references WOCAP multiple times and incorporates several of its strategies, including Strategy \#10's urban canopy provisions.
The Metropolitan Transportation Commission (MTC) provided planning, funding, coordination, and technical assistance for the project, drawing on its regional role coordinating Bay Area transportation initiatives. The Alameda County Transportation Commission (ACTC) brought countywide transportation planning expertise and long-range infrastructure investment experience. 
Coordination with the concurrent West Oakland Sustainable Transportation Equity Project (WOSTEP) provided additional planting resources. WOSTEP, implemented through a partnership between the City of Oakland, the Oakland Parks and Recreation Foundation, and Urban Biofilter (a collaboration between Hyphae and WOEIP), focused on tree planting throughout West Oakland. OakDOT, serving as WOSTEP project manager, provided trees from that grant for use in the Prescott Project planting areas.
The Prescott project aims to actualize the community-led visions in the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan. The primary way to reduce air pollution is with source reduction, but given that this is a longer-term process, there are additional strategies to more immediately reduce resident exposure to existing pollution, such as planting vegetated buffers. Possible planting projects adjacent to the Prescott neighborhood have been identified. The goal is for these plantings to take place in phases, with the earliest planting being the proposed planting along the immediately plantable areas along Frontage Road.

## First Phase of Planting - Plant what is immediately plantable
It was determined that revitalizing the planting area along frontage road: replacing trees in poor condition and infilling the planting area could create an effective buffer for the community from traffic emissions. 
An area of opportunity is the plantable area along Frontage Road, which is within the City of Oakland’s right-of-way. ### **Trees Impact Respiratory Health**
We have reason to believe that trees have an effect on people’s respiratory health. In 2013 a study was published in the American Journal of Preventative Medicine called “The Relationship Between Trees and Human Health- Evidence from the Spread of the Emerald Ash Borer”. The Emerald Ash Borer is an invasive insect from Asia, whose larvae bore into ash trees and kill them. Since it was first detected in the US in 2002 it has led to the loss of 100 million trees. Researchers looked at data in 15 states, and found that in counties where more trees had been killed there were more deaths related to issues of the lower respiratory system and, and more cardiovascular-related deaths. In this area alone the ash borer was associated with more than 20 thousand additional deaths. 
Studies have shown that vegetation has a positive impact on people’s health in general. But planting specific trees in specific configurations can make them even more effective at stopping pollution. Vegetated buffers are thick walls of trees that can be planted near pollution sources to create a physical barrier to pollution.
The EPA has studied roadside vegetated buffers by placing sensors in front of and behind different types of vegetation to see what makes a vegetated buffer more or less effective at blocking pollution. 
—---
#### A good barrier is unbroken
In order for a vegetated barrier to function, it needs to physically block small pollution particles. Having a lot of gaps in a buffer will let the pollution particles through. Much of the things that makes a buffer effective are very intuitive. It needs to be long enough that pollution doesn’t just blow around it easily. It should be at least 13 feet high. Trees need to have branching that goes to the ground, or there should be a combination of trees with shrubs to cover the gaps below. An ideal buffer should also be thick.
#### A good buffer is downwind and close to the source of pollution
—---
Typically a buffer is most effective if the wind is blowing the pollution towards the trees. If the wind blows along the trees or away from the trees, then they won’t actually intercept the particulates. 
Computer models of how particulates move after being produced by a vehicle shows that they tend to disperse into the upper atmosphere as they drift away from the road, and become much harder to capture. So planting buffers as close to the source of the pollution as possible makes them much more effective. It’s similar to how if you stood closer to a sprinkler, you would block more of the water than if you stood far away from it. 
In the West Oakland community-scale emissions inventory, particulate matter (PM2.5) emissions from streets and highways made up 50% of the total PM2.5 emissions. This means that vegetated buffers have the potential to intercept a big source of local emissions.
\_\_\_\_\_
#### A good buffer uses thicker evergreen trees
Part of the reason that trees make better buffers than buildings or walls, is that leaves have much higher surface areas. This both slows the air carrying the particulate matter, and also gives much more area for the particulates to be deposited on to. 
Tree habit and leaf shape impacts how effective they are as buffers. A tree with open branching is going to create gaps for pollution. A shade tree will typically trap pollution close to the ground, rather than blocking it from moving towards pedestrians. 
\_\_\_\_\_
Trees with leaves that are thick and dense with a lot of little needles have a higher overall surface area than leaves that are flat and simple. Leaf area density (LAD) is the surface area of of leaf foliage per unit of volume. A tree with a higher leaf area density has lots of leaves clustered densely together, and will be better at intercepting pollution.
Evergreen trees are preferred, not only because many evergreen species tend to have higher Leaf Area Density than broadleaf deciduous trees, but because they do not lose their leaves in the winter time. They can be an effective barrier year round.
\_\_\_
Trees can grow tall, making them the most ideal for a good buffer. But other types of vegetation can supplement vegetated buffer plantings. Shrubs, large grasses, and smaller vegetation can fill in the gaps below trees, and add visual character. And in some places where trees are not permitted or are too large, a large hedge is a great alternative. Vines climbing over walls are much more effective than walls by themselves. 
The characteristics that make other vegetation good additions to a buffer are:
  - Thick dense branching
  - Evergreen
  - Longevity (doesn’t look scraggly after 2-3 years)
  - Low maintenance

## Costs
## How much does it cost to care for one tree for a year?
When labor, water, equipment, and administrative overhead are fully accounted for, the cost to care for a single urban tree is approximately $500 per tree per year on the low end, particularly during the establishment period (typically the first 3–5 years after planting).
Lower figures frequently cited in academic literature ($20–$60 per tree per year) reflect annualized municipal averages that amortize costs across large inventories and often exclude establishment watering, nonprofit or contractor labor rates, and program administration. In contrast, practitioner and municipal budget documents that model actual service delivery show substantially higher per-tree costs.
For example, San Francisco’s Urban Forest Financing Report estimated approximately $560 per tree per year when accounting for pruning, inspections, service requests, emergency response, and contractor labor, with a reduced but still substantial range of $270–$455 per tree under more efficient maintenance scenarios (City of San Francisco, n.d.). Roman et al. (2018) similarly note that full-cost accounting reveals maintenance costs far higher than those implied by ecosystem service valuation studies.
## How much does it cost to care for multiple trees on a site for a year?
When trees are maintained as a grouped site or corridor, per-tree costs decrease due to shared labor, equipment mobilization, and watering efficiencies. Even with these economies of scale, annual costs typically remain in the range of $300–$450 per tree per year when establishment care is included.
Municipal plans show that while pruning or pest management costs can be reduced through batching, watering, monitoring, and follow-up care still require significant labor inputs. The City of Hayward, for example, estimates combined annual costs for pruning, watering, and pest management exceeding $200 per tree, before accounting for removals, inspections, and administrative overhead (City of Hayward, n.d.). Roman et al. (2015) emphasize that clustered maintenance reduces costs relative to dispersed plantings, but does not eliminate the fundamental labor intensity of tree care.
## How much does it cost to care for a neighborhood or larger area of trees for a year?
At the neighborhood or citywide scale, maintenance costs are typically expressed as total program budgets rather than per-tree line items. When these budgets are divided across the managed tree population, average per-tree costs often fall between $250 and $600 per year, depending on service levels, climate, and labor structure.
San Francisco’s analysis demonstrates that sustaining a large urban forest with acceptable service levels requires tens of millions of dollars annually, translating to per-tree costs consistent with the $500/year range when emergency response, inspections, and contractor services are included (City of San Francisco, n.d.). Roman et al. (2018) caution that programs operating below this funding threshold tend to defer maintenance, resulting in higher long-term costs due to removals and replanting.
## How do economies of scale impact maintenance costs?
Economies of scale reduce per-tree costs but do not eliminate high baseline expenses associated with labor, water, and equipment. Larger programs benefit from:
  - Shared crew mobilization  
  - Scheduled pruning cycles  
  - Centralized data and monitoring systems  
  - Long-term service contracts  
These efficiencies can reduce costs by 20–40% relative to small or fragmented programs, explaining why citywide averages may fall closer to $300–$400 per tree per year under optimal conditions. However, Roman et al. (2018) and Roman et al. (2015) consistently find that underfunded maintenance programs experience higher mortality, which ultimately increases the cost per surviving tree and erodes projected ecosystem service benefits.
In effect, economies of scale shift costs from reactive emergency response to proactive maintenance, but they do not justify minimal per-tree budgets. Sustained investment remains necessary to ensure survival and long-term canopy performance.

More on CARB funding
### **1. CARB Funds Defined Deliverables Within a Defined Term**
Across AB 617, Cycle 4/5, and other incentive programs, CARB consistently requires:
  - A **clearly defined project term  
    **
  - **Fully costed scopes** tied to that term  
  - **No open-ended obligations** beyond the grant period  
This structure implicitly aligns with **sinking or fixed-term maintenance funds**, not perpetual endowments, which:
  - Extend obligations indefinitely  
  - Depend on long-term investment performance  
  - Are difficult to reconcile with CARB’s close-out and audit requirements  
**Implication:** CARB prefers maintenance funding models that can be fully accounted for, spent down, and closed out.
## **CARB’s Longstanding Position: “No O\&M Without a Plan”**
CARB guidance routinely emphasizes that:
  - Capital investments must have a **credible operations and maintenance plan  
    **
  - O\&M costs must be **identified, justified, and tracked  
    **
  - Ongoing performance must be **demonstrable within the project window  
    **
A **sinking maintenance fund**:
  - Clearly ties dollars to specific maintenance actions  
  - Allows CARB to verify that maintenance occurred  
  - Avoids ambiguity about whether funds are being “saved” instead of used  
**Key Point:** A trust that *spends* funds to achieve outcomes is easier for CARB to evaluate than one that *earns* funds over time.
## **Past CARB Awards Show a Pattern (Even If Not a Rule)**
Looking across awarded AB 617 and community air protection projects, CARB has consistently funded:
  - **Time-bound maintenance line items** (3–5 years is common)  
  - **Escrow-style or reserve accounts** tied to establishment periods  
  - **Replacement and survival guarantees**, not perpetual care  
You rarely see:
  - CARB capitalizing a true endowment  
  - CARB approving grant funds that are not expected to be fully expended within the agreement term  
**Interpretation:** CARB values **durability through proper establishment**, not perpetual financial instruments.
## **Audit and Risk Management Considerations**
From a state agency perspective:
  - Endowments raise questions about:  
      - Investment risk  
      - Fiduciary liability  
      - Long-term governance after grant close-out  
  - Sinking funds:  
      - Are easier to audit  
      - Have clearer eligible uses  
      - Reduce long-term state exposure  
**Bottom line:** Sinking funds reduce CARB’s financial and compliance risk.
## **When an Endowment *****Might***** Be Acceptable (Rare, But Worth Naming)**
CARB is not philosophically opposed to permanence—but:
  - Endowments usually require **non-CARB match dollars  
    **
  - CARB dollars, if included, are typically limited to:  
      - Establishment  
      - Early maintenance  
      - Monitoring  
If you position the endowment as **supplemental** and CARB funds as **time-bound**, you stay aligned.

#### **Prescott Greening**
#### **Project Background**
The Prescott Project is a greening project funded by the Metropolitan Transportation Commission and the California Air Resources Board. It is led by West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project (WOEIP), and supported technically by Hyphae Design Laboratory, with the goal of using air quality modeling, monitoring, and evidence based design to improve human and environmental health. To achieve this  emissions and exposure risks were evaluated, and this data was used to design and model greening interventions. 
\[image\]
#### **Context**
The Prescott Project will fulfill strategy \#16 of the West Oakland Community Action Plan (WOCAP).
West Oakland experiences disproportionately high levels of air pollution, more than three times higher than the Bay Area average. Areas within the Prescott neighborhood are hotspots of pollution, with levels of particulate matter as high as 150 to 300% more than the ambient measurements inside West Oakland. In 2018, West Oakland was identified as a first-year priority in a state Community Air Protection Program under Assembly Bill 617. This initiates community-led emissions and exposure reduction programs in neighborhoods most impacted by pollution. West Oakland was chosen as a pilot neighborhood for this program. The West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project (WOEIP) and the Bay Area Air District (BAAD) co-led the development of the West Oakland Community Action Plan (WOCAP), which includes 89 identified strategies to reduce source and exposure to pollution in the neighborhood. The plan was formally approved by BAAQMD and the California Air Resources Board (CARB), and adopted by the City of Oakland City Council in 2019. 
#### **Project Scope & Phases**
\[image\]
The Prescott project aims to actualize the community-led visions in the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan. The primary way to reduce air pollution is with source reduction, but given that this is a longer-term process, there are additional strategies to more immediately reduce resident exposure to existing pollution, such as planting vegetated buffers. Possible planting projects adjacent to the Prescott neighborhood have been identified. The goal is for these plantings to take place in phases, with the earliest planting being the proposed planting along the immediately plantable areas along Frontage Road. 
**Caltrans Planting**
The 880 freeway is a major contributor to particulate pollution in the Prescott neighborhood. In preliminary models, planting buffers in the Caltrans right-of-way was shown to be a promising way to intercept air pollution before it reaches the neighborhood. Initial concept designs are underway. 
**Frontage Road Diet **
A road diet refers to the removal and reduction of lanes in order to slow traffic and increase safety along a roadway. The Frontage Road diet is the proposal to reduce the size of Frontage Road lanes in order to use the excess space for a large vegetated buffer. The Hyphae team has used Computational Fluid Dynamic modeling to develop concept designs for this road diet. 
**Frontage Road Immediate Planting**
It was determined that revitalizing the planting area along frontage road: replacing trees in poor condition and infilling the planting area could create an effective buffer for the community from traffic emissions. 
An area of opportunity is the plantable area along Frontage Road, which is within the City of Oakland’s right-of-way. The right-of-way along Frontage Road has some existing trees already, and an existing irrigation system, but some of the trees are in poor condition and the irrigation system has not functioned for a long time. Repairing that irrigation system and replacing and infilling trees would both allow for the beautification of a city road, and create a buffer for pollution. 
Frontage road receives high volumes of truck traffic, and runs adjacent to the freeway: a major source of local pollution. Through the use of Computational Fluid Dynamic modeling, Hyphae evaluated the impact of vegetated buffers in reducing resident exposure to existing pollution. The models indicated that a vegetated buffer in this area could effectively lessen the concentration of particulate pollution downwind in the neighborhood. 
\[image\]
#### **Completing the Frontage Road Planting**
The first phase of planting along Frontage Road will be completed at the end of 2026. Due to the limitations of the existing budget, only part of the plantable space along the roadway will be planted. With additional funding, the planting area could be extended to the full area originally intended. Planting plans for that area have already been created and reviewed by the City of Oakland’s parks department. Additionally, the team is currently working to find more funding to fully cover the establishment maintenance and stewardship of the project area.
