# Initial Outline

**Hyphae Design Lab**
*Re: Nueva Street Corridor Planning Study — RFP No. 743-2026-RFP-1426*
**Tasks Hyphae can contribute:**
  - A LiDAR-based digital twin of the corridor’s existing conditions, covering tree canopy, structures, and terrain
  - Sub-meter thermal comfort and microclimate simulation (CFD / ENVI-met) for each of the required corridor scenarios
  - Highway-adjacent pollution and wind dispersion modeling to assess how the corridor is impacted by its proximity to the highway
  - Design and deployment of a field sensor network (particulate, wind, temperature/humidity) for baseline data collection
  - Ongoing data tracking, quality assurance, and analysis pipeline to validate and refine models against real-world conditions
  - Land use evaluation along the corridor, using land-cover and equity data to inform prioritization and recommendations
  - Integration of traffic-count and travel-pattern data into the environmental models, in coordination with the team's traffic engineering lead
  - Data-driven tree and vegetation placement recommendations to support the canopy and cooling-infrastructure strategy
  - Planning-level cost estimates (capital and O\&M) for recommended greening and cooling infrastructure
  - Pre/post monitoring design to measure thermal comfort and air quality outcomes across project phases
  - Concept level designs for greening and cooling infrastructure
  - GIS-native data deliverables structured for integration with UT San Antonio's own research and future design work
**Our experience:**
**Sub-meter thermal comfort and microclimate simulation: **We build 3D digital twins from aerial LiDAR and run them through OpenFOAM and ENVI-met to simulate wind, shade, and heat at a resolution fine enough to place individual trees. We used this workflow on Harry Hines Blvd in Dallas’s Southwestern Medical District, a highway-adjacent corridor with real similarities to Nueva, modeling thermal comfort and pollution transport both before and after proposed interventions.
**Sensor network planning, deployment, and data operations: **We validate our models through measurement. Our teams have designed and built distributed monitoring networks (PurpleAir particulate sensors, anemometers, drone-based transects, and LoRaWAN-connected stationary units feeding a cloud data pipeline), sited using CFD results and community input, then tracked and analyzed the resulting data to validate the models.
**Hyperlocal tree and vegetation placement: **The Green Heart Project in Louisville, which Hyphae worked on in partnership with the University of Louisville and The Nature Conservancy, used our vegetation placement method to plant over 8,000 trees across four square miles, paired with a clinical study tracking the health effects.
**Highway-adjacent pollution and wind modeling: **Nueva runs alongside a highway, which likely leads to elevated pollution exposure. Our dispersion modeling simulates how the wind transports pollutants near major roadways. On our Rodeo project we partnered with the community to help them understand the wind-driven pollution transport near their major roadways.
**Land use evaluation for greening and cooling prioritization: **On the Oakland STEP project, we combined land use and land-cover data with social-vulnerability and equity indicators to build a weighted prioritization system for which streets and sidewalks should receive greening and cooling investment first.
**Traffic-data-informed environmental modeling: ** On Harry Hines we worked with the team’s traffic engineers using traffic count data to calibrate our models and assess health impact. Accurate traffic data is a key input of a rigorous pollution model.
**Cost-informed prioritization: **By simulating outcomes before anything gets built, we can provide the opportunity to weigh capital and O\&M costs against expected benefits. We’ve provided this service on projects such as Boyle Heights and on our Prescott Greening Project.
**Project Reference Summary**
**Harry Hines Blvd, Dallas Southwestern Medical District. **Highway-adjacent corridor slated for a major streetscape and tree-planting overhaul. We built the LiDAR-based digital twin and ran the CFD/thermal comfort simulations that guided tree placement, deployed field sensors for cross-sectional and pre/post monitoring, and coordinated directly with the team’s traffic engineers, Buro Happold and Kimley Horn, to incorporate traffic data into the models. 
**Oakland STEP Project, Oakland, CA. **Citywide street and sidewalk greening prioritization program. We combined parcel-level land use and land-cover data with equity and community-feedback data to rank streets for cooling investment, and worked with city DOT staff on tree-well and streetscape engineering standards.
**Green Heart Project, Louisville, KY (with University of Louisville and The Nature Conservancy). **Led data-driven placement modeling to** **plant 8,000+ trees across a four-square-mile area and paired the greening with a clinical study of health effects. 
**Rodeo Community, CA. **Full-cycle air-quality monitoring program near industrial and highway pollution sources. We planned sensor placement using CFD results and community input, built and deployed the network (PurpleAir particulate sensors, 2D anemometers, drone-based transects), and tracked and analyzed the resulting data to characterize wind-driven pollution transport.
**Arlington Park Neighborhood Association, CA (proposed, not funded). **A proposed adaptOS deployment specifying a LoRaWAN sensor network, cloud data pipeline, and community-facing analysis dashboard, alongside CFD-based simulation of interventions like urban greening and truck rerouting. It wasn’t funded, but the proposal reflects the same combined sensor-and-modeling approach we’d bring to Nueva.
**Boyle Heights, Los Angeles. **Neighborhood-scale plan to reduce traffic and industrial pollution exposure through vegetated buffers, truck rerouting, and depaving. We provided the environmental modeling and technical coordination behind the intervention design.
**Prescott Greening Project, West Oakland, CA (with WOEIP; funded by MTC and CARB). **Freeway-adjacent vegetated-buffer project along I-880. We built a combined aerial-and-ground LiDAR digital twin and ran CFD/ENVI-met simulations to test buffer placements against real wind and emissions data, including a “road diet” concept that reclaimed travel lanes for planting. We also coordinated Caltrans right-of-way permitting and structured the project’s per-tree cost and stewardship/O\&M funding model.

<comment_start id=kix.9t67bgu7tsz5>Collaborated<comment_end id=kix.9t67bgu7tsz5> with the Texas Trees Foundation to integrate an evidence-based design framework into the redesign of a corridor in Dallas's Southwestern Medical District, a project that combines traffic flow modifications with a large-scale tree planting initiative aimed at improving public health. Using environmental sensors, Hyphae's role was to monitor health-improving interventions and set up the district as a "living laboratory" for data driven design, running thermal comfort simulations across the whole corridor using a model of existing conditions, validated through community engagement, then testing many iterative scenarios in a representative section of the corridor and for the proposed Innwood Park.
Key findings showed that replacing mature trees with younger ones increases pedestrian heat stress, while clustered groves of shade trees outperform sparse or evenly spaced plantings by generating cooling pockets that extend into downwind areas. However, near building corners where wind speeds are high, shade trees can sometimes increase heat by blocking beneficial airflow. The findings underscore mature canopy's outsized role in lowering radiant temperature and position the project as a national model for evidence-based design.
  - [**The Green Heart Project**](https://www.nature.org/en-us/about-us/where-we-work/united-states/kentucky/stories-in-kentucky/green-heart-project/)**: Pioneering Urban Greening for Public Health** (2018 – Present)  
      
    The Green Heart Project began in 2018 as a collaborative effort between the University of Louisville, The Nature Conservancy, and multiple local and national partners, including Hyphae Design Lab. With funding from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and support from The Nature Conservancy, the Green Heart Project is the first [clinical tria](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwae458)l to study the effects of urban greening on air quality and its direct impact on cardiovascular and systemic health.
Key aspects of the project included planting over 8,000 mature evergreen trees and shrubs across a designated four-square-mile area in Louisville, Kentucky. This effort targeted neighborhoods with historically low tree canopy coverage and high exposure to environmental stressors, including air pollution and urban heat.
The project implemented a **quasi-experimental design**:
  - Baseline (2018-2019): Collection of extensive health and environmental data from 745 residents, including biomarker analyses (e.g., high-sensitivity C-reactive protein or hsCRP), air quality measurements, and noise pollution monitoring.
  - Intervention (2019-2020): Large-scale greening, focusing on high-traffic corridors and residential zones.
  - Post-intervention Assessment (2020-Present): Continued monitoring of participants to evaluate health improvements linked to reduced systemic inflammation and improved air quality.
**Impact**:
  - Participants in neighborhoods receiving greenery saw 13–20% lower levels of hsCRP, corresponding to a 10–15% reduction in risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes.
  - Significant reductions in particulate matter (PM2.5) levels and urban noise pollution were documented, highlighting the multi-dimensional benefits of increased urban greenery.
Scalability and Future Research:
The Green Heart Project recently received $4.6 million in renewed funding from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) to expand its research over the next five years. The project will delve deeper into the long-term benefits of urban greening, exploring how increased tree cover affects heat stress, chronic diseases, and healthcare costs.
  - [**LA Depave Plan**](https://depave.la/)  
      
    Partnering with Los Angeles County, Hyphae sourced and compiled data from over 20 agencies to create a countywide green infrastructure strategy. This comprehensive plan integrated GIS layers for land use, transportation, and climate factors to prioritize multi-benefit solutions addressing urban heat mitigation, stormwater management, and depaving opportunities. By overlaying environmental and social vulnerability data, the strategy emphasized interventions in heat-vulnerable areas to maximize cooling effects and enhance community resilience. The initiative was part of a larger Community Forest Management Plan funded by CAL FIRE’s Urban and Community Forestry Program, with an award of $1.5 million.
  - [**Oakland STEP Project**](https://adapt-os.com/kits/step/story) (2023–2026, STEP Grant)  
      
    The Oakland STEP project integrated high-resolution parcel-level and meter-scale analyses, synthesizing remote sensing and political data to prioritize interventions for mitigating urban heat. Streetlight data for vehicle and active transportation guided the development of weighting systems that normalized diverse datasets and incorporated environmental justice and community feedback. These systems informed the equitable prioritization of streets and sidewalks for greening, focusing on heat mitigation. Hyphae collaborated with city DOT staff to improve engineering workflows for sidewalks and streetscapes, developing novel tree planting guidelines and protocols for tree well cutouts to enhance cooling and shading in historically neglected areas.  
  - **Prescott Greening Project **(2021-2024, AB 617 and Transformative Climate Communities funding):
This ongoing project is led by the West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project with Hyphae support. Part of the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan (WOCAP) under AB 617, the project received initial funding from a $150,000 Transformative Climate Communities grant by the California Strategic Growth Council. Additional funding includes $300,000 from the Metropolitan Transportation Commission's Priority Conservation Areas program, matched with $600,000 from Alameda County Transportation Commission, $50,000 from the Port of Oakland, and $100,000 from CalFire. The project integrates air quality monitoring, vegetated buffers, and tree planting to reduce pollution exposure in West Oakland's Prescott neighborhood, the highest pollution hotspot in the region. It employs phased implementation, beginning with a vegetated buffer pilot along Frontage Road, to create scalable solutions for pollution mitigation​
  - **Richmond Parkway Urban Greening Project** (2019-2022, Urban Greening Grant Program):
This project, funded by California’s Urban Greening Grant Program, addresses environmental justice issues in Richmond by enhancing the Richmond Parkway. Key interventions include tree planting, bike lane redesign, and pedestrian access improvements to buffer neighborhoods like Atchison Village and the Iron Triangle from pollution and traffic hazards. The project integrates technical design, community engagement, and job training through partnerships with Groundwork Richmond. Its innovative “Treefense” strategy involves planting dense vegetative barriers to reduce pollution exposure, enhance safety, and improve thermal comfort, leveraging $184,600 in matching funding from the City of Richmond for maintenance and additional in-kind support from CalFire and local stakeholders​.
  - **Stockton AB 617 Urban Forestry and Vegetation Barrier Project** (2021–2025, $2 million):
This multi-year initiative, funded through AB 617 and California’s Transformative Climate Communities (TCC) program, focuses on enhancing Stockton's green infrastructure to mitigate air pollution and urban heat impacts. Key activities include planting and maintaining over 1,750 trees within disadvantaged neighborhoods, creating vegetative barriers to shield residents from pollution, and implementing a prioritization plan for future greening efforts. ## Firm Experience
Hyphae Design Lab brings extensive experience delivering innovative, data-driven solutions for urban resilience, environmental justice, and heat and pollution mitigation, pairing technical rigor with robust community engagement to give communities actionable data and scalable design strategies tailored to their specific needs. This includes a strong track record of collaborating with university research teams on air quality and heat mitigation; among them the Greenheart Project and University of Louisville (KY), the Marin City (CA) project with Dr. Ivey, UC Berkeley with Tina Chow, UC Merced with Asa Bradman, UCSF with Neeta Thakur, and a new NIH cancer grant with Texas A\&M, giving Hyphae the experience needed to collaborate effectively with UT San Antonio's staff, researchers, and students. 
  - [**Greenspine/Southwestern Medical District Streetscape**](https://texastrees.org/swmdtransformation-design/) (Current, Dallas, TX)  
      
    Collaborated with the Texas Trees Foundation to integrate an evidence-based design framework into the redesign of a corridor in Dallas's Southwestern Medical District, a project that combines traffic flow modifications with a large-scale tree planting initiative aimed at improving public health. Using environmental sensors, Hyphae's role was to monitor health-improving interventions and set up the district as a "living laboratory" for data driven design, running thermal comfort simulations across the whole corridor using a model of existing conditions, validated through community engagement, then testing many iterative scenarios in a representative section of the corridor and for the proposed Innwood Park.
Key findings showed that replacing mature trees with younger ones increases pedestrian heat stress, while clustered groves of shade trees outperform sparse or evenly spaced plantings by generating cooling pockets that extend into downwind areas. However, near building corners where wind speeds are high, shade trees can sometimes increase heat by blocking beneficial airflow. The findings underscore mature canopy's outsized role in lowering radiant temperature and position the project as a national model for evidence-based design.
  - [**The Green Heart Project**](https://www.nature.org/en-us/about-us/where-we-work/united-states/kentucky/stories-in-kentucky/green-heart-project/)**: Pioneering Urban Greening for Public Health** (2018 – Present)  
      
    The Green Heart Project began in 2018 as a collaborative effort between the University of Louisville, The Nature Conservancy, and multiple local and national partners, including Hyphae Design Lab. With funding from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and support from The Nature Conservancy, the Green Heart Project is the first [clinical tria](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwae458)l to study the effects of urban greening on air quality and its direct impact on cardiovascular and systemic health.
Key aspects of the project included planting over 8,000 mature evergreen trees and shrubs across a designated four-square-mile area in Louisville, Kentucky. This effort targeted neighborhoods with historically low tree canopy coverage and high exposure to environmental stressors, including air pollution and urban heat.
The project implemented a **quasi-experimental design**:
  - Baseline (2018-2019): Collection of extensive health and environmental data from 745 residents, including biomarker analyses (e.g., high-sensitivity C-reactive protein or hsCRP), air quality measurements, and noise pollution monitoring.
  - Intervention (2019-2020): Large-scale greening, focusing on high-traffic corridors and residential zones.
  - Post-intervention Assessment (2020-Present): Continued monitoring of participants to evaluate health improvements linked to reduced systemic inflammation and improved air quality.
**Impact**:
  - Participants in neighborhoods receiving greenery saw 13–20% lower levels of hsCRP, corresponding to a 10–15% reduction in risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes.
  - Significant reductions in particulate matter (PM2.5) levels and urban noise pollution were documented, highlighting the multi-dimensional benefits of increased urban greenery.
Scalability and Future Research:
The Green Heart Project recently received $4.6 million in renewed funding from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) to expand its research over the next five years. The project will delve deeper into the long-term benefits of urban greening, exploring how increased tree cover affects heat stress, chronic diseases, and healthcare costs.

By overlaying environmental and social vulnerability data, the strategy emphasized interventions in heat-vulnerable areas to maximize cooling effects and enhance community resilience. The initiative was part of a larger Community Forest Management Plan funded by CAL FIRE’s Urban and Community Forestry Program, with an award of $1.5 million.
