# **Hyphae Description**
## **From CMEJ-MPCA Grant**
Hyphae Design Lab is a national leader in evidence-based design. In partnership with communities and academics, they have received funding from the National Science Foundation and the National Institutes of Health to develop an adaptive management platform named adaptOS. This software-enabled platform synthesizes aerial, satellite, and ground-level data to create high-resolution, hyperlocal maps that model local risks to air, water, and soil. In consultation with communities, the platform can then generate adaptive environmental design interventions to maximize human health and biodiversity, as well as mitigate other threats such as extreme heat and flood risk. With the University of Louisville, Hyphae is presently leading the first and largest clinical trial designed to uncover the effects of urban greening on air quality at a neighborhood scale. 
## **From CMEJ Google Data Grant**
We selected Hyphae as our technical partners for their expertise in leveraging advanced technology to support community-based organizations (CBOs) fighting environmental racism. Partnering with leading scientific institutions and academic researchers, Hyphae equips grassroots organizations with the data and tools needed to drive meaningful environmental change. In 2020, they received National Science Foundation funding to develop adaptOS, a platform that synthesizes aerial, satellite, and ground-level data into high-resolution environmental models, enabling communities to design and implement data-driven interventions. Hyphae is currently leading a clinical trial with the University of Louisville to study the [effects of urban greening](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/heart-health/living-tree-filled-areas-may-reduce-heart-disease-risk-study-shows-rcna168214) on air quality and is working with Climate Resolve and NASA’s Jet Propulsion Lab in Los Angeles on a [de-paving plan](https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2023-09-08/heat-waves-los-angeles-reflective-coating-streets-cool-pavement) to reduce urban heat island effects and pollution. Similar projects are underway in West Oakland, Stockton, Richmond, Marin City, and beyond.

### Community-driven Design
Our engineering partner, Hyphae Design Laboratory, furnishes community organizers with the data and scientific tools they need to practice evidence-based environmental justice advocacy. The firm has fifteen years of experience coordinating activists, other technical assistants, academic researchers, air districts, city governments, and federal agencies in To redressing unfair pollution burdens faced by low-income communities and communities of color. Acting mainly through the legislative framework of AB 617, their partners have transformed their built environments and achieved significant policy changes in pollution reduction, green infrastructure, and climate resilience.
In Boyle Heights, Climate Resolve and Promesa are partnering with Hyphae to plant vegetated buffers, implement a truck re-routing plan, and depave a section of the neighborhood. \[What is the funding source for this grant?\] Through a NASA Grant, Coordinating with the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, the project integrates aerial and satellite imagery with Hyphae’s LiDAR scanning and Climate Resolve’s ground-level heat sensors to identify heat stress at a submeter level. Trusted Promesa messengers will then carefully track where sensitive receptors gather, collecting door-to-door canvassing information that cannot be gleaned from maps. Hyphae will then combine all four data sets to map the area and generate a series of extreme heat mitigation scenarios. 
### Evidence-based Design
Max’s text: 
Using hyperlocal mapping and measurement tools developed with funding from the National Science Foundation and the National Institute of Health, Hyphae helps CBOs identify air, soil, and water contamination in their communities, as well as other risks to human health such as extreme heat, flood risk, noise pollution, wildfires, and vector-borne diseases. Partners can then deploy Hyphae’s software-based adaptOS platform to simulate, design, and implement environmental mitigation solutions in response. Such interventions range from urban greening to truck-rerouting to the design of software applications protecting sensitive receptors during extreme heat events. 
The adaptOS platform iteratively cycles through a five-stage process of measurement, simulation, implementation, assessment, and refinement. After mapping a given geographical area, the platform models potential mitigation scenarios prior to construction. Once design solutions are implemented, the same tools are used to evaluate and assess the net environmental impact of those designs, providing empirical evidence of their effectiveness and sustainability, and allowing for corrections as needed. 
Brent’s text:
The community-led design and implementation process is also grounded in an evidence-based, scientific,  adaptive management framework. Evidence-Based design utilizes scientific basis for community adaptation. It goes beyond doing research with or for a community, which can often occur adjacent to the community empowerment work they are doing to embedding scientific within our community design decisions. 
In the Adapt evidence based design process, we do analysis and design, then do measurement and modeling to inform the design, prioritize implementation locations and measure their effects. It also de-imperializes science, as a tool that can be used by all, and not subject to academia and PHDs. Our core philosophy is in fact that community members are often the best scientists, as they inherently experience, observe and evaluate their community more than academics. 
### The Science Team
As Technical Consultant, Hyphae Design Laboratory will spearhead the evidence-based design process. bridging the collaboration between the community groups and the scientific and technical and agency specialists. They will help the team do the environmental measurement, modeling, engineering and design required to optimize infrastructure solutions, pedestrian and vehicle infrastructure optimization, as well as regarding tree planting and cool pavement installation (inclusive of Strategies 1,2,3,5,11, and 12). Hyphae will also lead coordination with the various research partners, including Altro Status, and Jet Propulsion Laboratory, and other partners. Hyphae will ensure that the science conducted is in alignment with the community goals and implementation projects and is shared with community members throughout the grant period. 
With support from NSF SBIR Grant \#2218499, Hyphae Design Laboratory, a mission-driven social enterprise, has developed software pipelines to build 3D digital twins, computational fluid dynamics models, and sub-meter microclimate simulations using open source data inputs like aerial lidar data, using openfoam CFD and UMEP simulation tools, to develop models for prioritizing locations and optimizing designs for various heat island mitigation interventions, such as tree planting, vine planting, cool surface treatments and pedestrian route planning. 
## **From Endless Frontier Proposal**
[Endless\_Frontier\_Labs\_2024-25](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1VbClNxKanMJkyuGnWte03IYg7T_UUYtY3T0Kr691eQ4/edit?tab=t.0)
**Q: Describe what your company does in 100 words or less:**
A: Hyphae Design Laboratory is an environmental engineering firm operating at the intersection of research and
design, focused on environmental health and climate adaptation. Our platform, adaptOS, revolutionizes green
infrastructure projects by integrating high-resolution environmental analytics, automated design, and project
management tools. Leveraging advanced machine learning, adaptOS analyzes data from various sources to
generate actionable insights for improving the quality of the air, water, and soil, as well as mitigating flooding and
heat islands. By combining data-driven design and management, adaptOS makes green infrastructure more
accessible and impactful, delivering measurable health and environmental benefits to communities.
**Q: What is your company's long-term vision and why is it important to you as a founder?**
A: Starting with our short-term ambitions to transform built environments in support of environmental health, Hyphae's long-term vision is to integrate environmental health into epidemiology. We hope that our tools can help shift our society’s reactive healthcare paradigm towards preventative environmental interventions in the built environment, where illness can be prevented before it occurs. Such a holistic approach would address not only genetic but also environmental causes of illness, leading to more effective prevention and treatment strategies in a world of healthy Cities.
This vision of a built environment that sustains human health was shaped by my experiences in the West Oakland
environmental justice movement. Witnessing the unfair pollution burdens placed on industrial fenceline
communities, I realized how reversing these trends at a local level could be scaled up to support health and
sustainability at regional and national levels.
**Q: Describe the customer pain point you are trying to solve. Why was it not solved earlier?**
A: Cities are complex heterogeneous ecosystems, and low resolution data can miss critical detail that dramatically impacts ecosystem service value and health. A difference of one single block in an implementation plan can have dramatic differences.
Despite these health impacts of our built environment decisions, the vast majority of clients, cities, agencies and
regulators lack a coordinated comprehensive approach to much of our urban planning, and don't require
businesses, urban planners, designers and engineers to do quantifiable, evidence-based design for anything
related to ecosystem services, let alone air quality and health. When an agency requires environmental review for a
high-profile project, the assessment doesn't actually significantly impact or shift design, but rather, the review is only
for regulatory compliance. None of these reviews consider other critical human environmental health factors like risk
and exposure assessment at either the project or master planning level.
**Q: Describe your target customers \[For therapeutic solutions, specify target indication(s).\]**
A: Our first target customers are municipalities, regulatory agencies, environmental consultants, urban planners, and community-based organizations. Assisting them to focus on improving environmental health outcomes through strategic urban planning and green infrastructure projects. These customers seek data-driven solutions to meet environmental standards, public health goals, and sustainable urban development. They need high-resolution analytics, design tools, and evidence-based interventions to advocate for and implement changes.
For therapeutic solutions, we target air quality improvement, heat island mitigation, and pollution reduction. Our adaptOS platform provides high-resolution environmental data, advanced modeling, and actionable insights for effective interventions. By addressing these areas, we enhance community health, reduce disease prevalence, and promote sustainability. Our scalable and adaptable solutions meet diverse urban planning and environmental health needs.
**Q: Describe your company's product or service and what it does, or will do.**
A: Hyphae Design Laboratory's adaptOS is a green infrastructure operating system that provides a comprehensive,
end-to-end project delivery process. It integrates environmental analytics, design, implementation, monitoring, and
evaluation in one platform. Using advanced machine learning, adaptOS analyzes data from street-view images,
satellite data, and meteorological information to produce high-resolution estimates of environmental conditions.
This allows for strategic, site-specific interventions that improve community health outcomes. By bridging the gap
between data and actionable solutions, adaptOS enables municipalities, regulatory agencies, and community
organizations to implement effective and efficient environmental health initiatives. Its holistic approach addresses
both genetic and environmental factors of health, promoting preventative measures and sustainable urban
Development.
**Q: Please highlight how your technology is novel, differentiated and/or transformational.**
A: Our adaptOS platform offers a comprehensive, end-to-end solution for environmental health that sets it apart.
Unlike competitors, adaptOS integrates high-resolution environmental analytics, design, implementation,
monitoring, and evaluation in a single platform. It uses advanced machine learning to analyze street-view images,
satellite data, and meteorological information, producing precise environmental condition estimates. This allows for
strategic, site-specific interventions that traditional methods cannot match.
The novelty of adaptOS lies in its ability to bridge the gap between data and actionable interventions, ensuring
projects deliver measurable health outcomes. Its transformational impact is evident in how it shifts urban planning
from reactive to preventative, addressing both genetic and environmental health factors. By combining automation
and evidence-based design, adaptOS not only identifies environmental risks but also implements and monitors
effective solutions, promoting healthier communities and sustainable development.

adaptOS provided high-resolution environmental condition estimates, leading to improved urban planning and
community health outcomes.
In Louisville, the Green Heart project marked the first NIH funded clinical trial with urban greening interventions,

Louisville. Our work on extreme heat in Dallas showcased the platform's effectiveness in mitigating urban heat
islands and enhancing public health. Additionally, our ongoing work in Boyle Heights continues to validate
adaptOS's capabilities in real-world settings, providing actionable insights for urban planning and green
infrastructure projects.
These projects, backed by measurable health and environmental improvements, confirm adaptOS's effectiveness
and scalability. Continuous monitoring and feedback from these initiatives have further refined and validated our
platform's capabilities.

### NSF SBIR
**NSF SBIR Phase I and II Grants: AdaptOS Development **(2020–Present)  
  
Hyphae’s AdaptOS platform, developed with funding from NSF SBIR Phase I and II grants, enables community-based organizations (CBOs) and municipal partners to address environmental risks such as extreme heat, air pollution, and flooding with precision and scalability. This platform bridges gaps in traditional environmental planning by combining hyperlocal data collection, modeling, and design to ensure interventions are both cost-effective and impactful.
The AdaptOS framework is iterative, cycling through measurement, simulation, implementation, and assessment. Modeling is at the core of this process, serving two essential functions. First, it optimizes sensor placement by predicting pollutant dispersion patterns, ensuring accurate monitoring and data collection. Second, it allows communities to simulate the effects of proposed interventions—such as urban greening, truck rerouting, and shade structures—before implementation, providing clarity on which solutions will yield the greatest impact and benefit-to-cost ratio.
This approach addresses a persistent challenge faced by many environmental initiatives, as exemplified by Hyphae’s work on the West Oakland Community Air Action Plan (WOCAP). While WOCAP mandated significant regulatory concessions and outlined 89 pollution mitigation strategies, decision-makers lacked the empirical tools to determine which interventions would have the most significant impact at the hyperlocal level. AdaptOS fills this gap, demonstrating how interventions like tree canopy, vegetation, and traffic patterns can be configured to protect environmental health effectively.
By combining rigorous modeling with actionable design strategies, AdaptOS transforms environmental data into solutions tailored to community needs, ensuring measurable public health and environmental benefits. Hyphae has successfully deployed this platform in projects across Oakland, Stockton, and Dallas, delivering scalable frameworks that can guide future environmental resilience efforts.
### Greenheart
**The Green Heart Project: Pioneering Urban Greening for Public Health** (2018 – Present)  
  
The Green Heart Project began in 2018 as a collaborative effort between the University of Louisville, The Nature Conservancy, and multiple local and national partners, including Hyphae Design Lab. With funding from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and support from The Nature Conservancy, the Green Heart Project is the first clinical trial to study the effects of urban greening on air quality and its direct impact on cardiovascular and systemic health.
Key aspects of the project included planting over 8,000 mature evergreen trees and shrubs across a designated four-square-mile area in Louisville, Kentucky. This effort targeted neighborhoods with historically low tree canopy coverage and high exposure to environmental stressors, including air pollution and urban heat.
The project implemented a **quasi-experimental design**:
  - Baseline (2018-2019): Collection of extensive health and environmental data from 745 residents, including biomarker analyses (e.g., high-sensitivity C-reactive protein or hsCRP), air quality measurements, and noise pollution monitoring.
  - Intervention (2019-2020): Large-scale greening, focusing on high-traffic corridors and residential zones.
  - Post-intervention Assessment (2020-Present): Continued monitoring of participants to evaluate health improvements linked to reduced systemic inflammation and improved air quality.
**Impact**:
  - Participants in neighborhoods receiving greenery saw 13–20% lower levels of hsCRP, corresponding to a 10–15% reduction in risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes.
  - Significant reductions in particulate matter (PM2.5) levels and urban noise pollution were documented, highlighting the multi-dimensional benefits of increased urban greenery.
**Scalability and Future Research:**
The Green Heart Project recently received $4.6 million in renewed funding from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) to expand its research over the next five years. The project will delve deeper into the long-term benefits of urban greening, exploring how increased tree cover affects heat stress, chronic diseases, and healthcare costs.

In the Oakland Hills neighborhood, which does not share West Oakland’s history of redlining or similar discriminatory policies, residents tended to live up to seven years longer.
In 2017, [California Assembly Bill 617 (AB617)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=201720180AB617) identified communities with a high cumulative pollution exposure burden, and directed regulators to work with those communities to develop solutions. The [West Oakland Community Action Plan (WOCAP)](https://www.baaqmd.gov/community-health/community-health-protection-program/west-oakland-community-action-plan) was one of the state’s first projects in this endeavor. After leveraging existing pollution measurement and modeling data, the WOCAP established a steering committee of residents and agency representatives that identified more than 80 intervention strategies related to land-use, bike/pedestrian, transit, port/freight, health, and urban greening. 
[Hyphae Design Laboratory](https://www.hyphae.net/collaboration) became a member of the WOCAP steering committee and has been closely involved throughout the development of the community action plan. Hyphae is also the technical lead on two of the initial intervention projects that have been funded: the previously-mentioned road diet (lane removal) and greenbelt for air pollution reduction as well as pedestrian improvements and truck rerouting.
 As technical assistants, we trust our partners to set their own community outreach agendas. But we actively participate, when called upon, to present findings and take questions at community meetings. We also rely on CBOs as trusted community messengers to suss out citizen concerns through door-to-door canvassing, and other methods of polling the community, either through in-person surveys, social media campaigns, email blasts, and flyering. 
In a recent de-paving and extreme heat mitigation project in Los Angeles, we relied on the *Promotoras* of Promesa Boyle Heights as trusted community messengers. These local educators and citizen scientists helped us collect extreme heat data from sensors and assisted us in drawing up maps of neighborhoods affected by extreme heat that we were measuring and modeling. The Promotoras helped us identify where sensitive receptors, especially children and the elderly, lived and gathered, in ways that would have been difficult to discern without their assistance. Though we were developing maps combining highly sophisticated aerial and ground-level data with NASA’s Jet Propulsion Lab, the locally sourced Promotora maps were essential complements to the data provided by our academic research partners.

**Task 2: 28 month  Community Engagement Plan for Refinery Corridor Air Coalition (RCAC) (8/15/25 - 12/20/27)**
**Overview: **
Community engagement and technical tasks will proceed simultaneously from the beginning, and both the outreach and the analysis will inform each other and direct the inquiry and timeline of our programmed activities. Community members will begin engaging landowners from Day 1 so as to secure locations for sensor placement in a diversity of conditions. Their effectiveness will determine how quickly partners can begin monitoring. Regarding modeling, Hyphae will similarly defer to the wisdom of the community in choosing the desired scale of the AOIs, including those areas chosen to produce more granular models. Furthermore, the AOI and scientific methodology will be reviewed by academic and agency experts, and further refined, along with some details of the methodology. This methodological feedback and process of iterative technical and community refinement is integrated into a scope and timeline that assumes a baseline of adaptive flexibility. 
The CSAC is directed to residents of refinery corridor communities, beginning in Rodeo, Crockett, and Vallejo, and extending to Martinez, Bay Point, Pittsburg, and Antioch. All of our communities experience high levels of pollution, some refinery-related, and some emanating from other industrial as well as near-road sources. Community members long suffering from asthma, respiratory ailments, cancers, and birth defects have grown disenchanted with both the persistent difficulty in obtaining clear information about pollution sources and a history of perceived regulatory neglect. We are pleased to learn that the AD is taking a renewed interest in our area, and we earnestly support its efforts to enhance monitoring, regulatory oversight, and community engagement to address these longstanding environmental health concerns.
We cannot, however, afford to wait indefinitely for AD action. Independent data collection, such as that provided by PurpleAir sensors, is essential for empowering our community with leverage in advocating for timely regulatory responses. ## **Community Co-Design Approach**
Our environmental justice approach aims to empower communities to advocate for themselves using data, science, and evidence. For over a decade, Hyphae Design Laboratory has supported grassroots organizations by providing technical assistance, environmental monitoring, and design solutions that empower impacted communities. By collaborating with activists, researchers, government agencies, and local stakeholders, Hyphae has helped these groups achieve significant policy advancements in pollution reduction, climate resilience, and green infrastructure.
Using advanced tools for hyperlocal mapping and data collection, Hyphae identifies environmental threats such as air, soil, and water contamination, as well as health risks like extreme heat. The evidence gathered through this monitoring and modeling will drive hyperlocal design interventions, ensuring that solutions are precisely tailored to the needs of the community. The Arlington Park Neighborhood Association will utilize Hyphae’s adaptOS platform to design and simulate environmental interventions—such as urban greening, rerouting heavy vehicle traffic, or building digital tools to protect vulnerable populations. Insights from the community will form a crucial part of the evidence base, integrating local knowledge and lived experiences into the scientific analysis to ensure solutions are both effective and community-driven. This approach puts scientific tools and data directly in the hands of the community, shifting the balance of power from regulatory agencies to the people most affected by pollution.
However, challenges persist, particularly in modeling and simulating urban air quality. Tools like Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) are still underdeveloped for community-level use, making it challenging for grassroots groups like the Arlington Park Neighborhood Association to provide definitive data to influence policy effectively. Estimating dynamic air quality conditions in complex urban environments remains a methodological gap. Addressing this gap is crucial for making informed decisions about where and how to intervene. Hyphae's approach demonstrates that, with the right tools, communities can hold decision-makers accountable and compel industries and agencies to implement necessary changes.
This proposal seeks to enhance air quality modeling capabilities, enabling the Arlington Park Neighborhood Association to better predict the outcomes of their advocacy and environmental actions. Strengthening these modeling tools could set a national precedent by expanding this participatory environmental justice framework. With improved tools, the Arlington Park Neighborhood Association and other local groups will be better positioned to evaluate the costs and benefits of interventions, thereby accelerating their efforts to secure environmental improvements.
Hyphae's success is rooted in strategic partnerships—connecting community groups like the Arlington Park Neighborhood Association with local governments, academic experts, and other stakeholders. Their track record includes collaborations with various environmental justice organizations committed to reducing pollution exposure in vulnerable areas. Coalition-building is a fundamental aspect of this strategy: it creates a comprehensive ecosystem where grassroots data collection directly informs government policy and regulatory action.
The proposed strategy involves using successful pressure campaigns as templates for new regions. By building coalitions that unite community members, environmental scientists, and local governments, Hyphae has demonstrated how to push for stricter emissions standards, more effective air quality monitoring, and increased investment in green infrastructure. Empowering communities like the Arlington Park Neighborhood Association to collect their own data and advocate for change—backed by advanced modeling capabilities—can facilitate significant environmental justice achievements nationwide.

**Task 4: Monitoring **
As illustrated in the Workplan Table, the monitoring task will take place between August 2025 and August 2027. The task begins with the design of the monitoring framework, which is peer-reviewed and informed by preliminary modeling results (Task 4.1). Stationary low-cost sensors, including PurpleAir PM sensors and anemometers, will be deployed across identified hotspots based on modeling outputs and community engagement (Task 4.2). To complement these ground-based sensors, drones will be flown multiple times a year and during the day to capture LiDAR, video, and wind vector data around key areas, such as the Phillips 66 refinery (Task 4.3). Co-location with government reference monitors (Task 4.4) and the establishment of robust data validation methods (Task 4.5) will ensure accuracy and regulatory alignment. Additional analysis of the baseline data (Task 4.6) will inform future sensor deployments and refine the digital twin. Equipment purchases (Tasks 4.7–4.9), including specialized anemometers, telemetry supplies, and drone rentals, support the technical infrastructure needed to carry out these tasks effectively over the multi-year deployment period.
Monitoring will focus on two main analytes: wind vectors, measured by anemometers, and air pollution, which in this phase will be performed with optical particulate sensors which measure the mass of particles below 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 2.5, 5.0, & 10 μm, as well as particle counts, air temperature, humidity and barometric pressure. The wind sensors will be used to validate the wind vectors produced in the wind modeling task 3, and provide additional reference for understanding how the built environment interacts with meteorology to impact pollution transport from refineries and other sources. The air pollution sensors can be used to both seek spatiotemporal trends in community exposure with respect to pollution sources and refinery release events, and also to potentially validate pollution transport simulations. Both the wind and the air pollution sensor data will be a core piece of the community engagement efforts described in Task 2.  
The monitoring data can also be used to estimate the boundary conditions of the simulations in task 3. Because the PurpleAir sensors are ±10μg/m³ for PM2.5 at 0 to 100μg/m³, and PM2.5 background levels can fluctuate rapidly in response to wildfires in Canada, salt-spray from the ocean, and any of various other seemingly random dust producing events, we will also gather MAIA and TEMPO satellite imagery to supplement estimations of the boundary conditions for the the simulations described in Task 3, and to aid in interpretation of the PurpleAir data. 
We will employ both mobile and stationary sensors. Stationary sensors will be placed according to community discussions (described in task 2) which will be informed by preliminary wind and pollution modeling studies to seek the highest impact monitoring sites. Mobile monitoring will be conducted with drone flights to sample the vertical components of wind vectors (and air pollution metrics) up to and above the height of the refinery stacks. 
All data will be ingested by Hyphae’s PostgreSQL and times-series databases and made available to the community. 
Available air quality data from different sources have distinct spatial and temporal support structures and uncertainties. For instance, measurement sites often provide point measurements that are irregularly located in space at regular intervals, with reference networks providing sparse but high-quality (low uncertainty) data and low-cost sensor networks providing spatially dense data with higher uncertainty. Conversely, models and satellites typically provide data averaged over spatially and temporally regular or irregular space-time grids of different resolutions, with uncertainties often estimated. Additionally, different datasets measure various pollutants or other air quality-related entities, such as temperature and relative humidity, which cannot be directly compared. However, by leveraging the well-known Kalman filtering technique (Grewal and Andrews, 2014) as well as other data processing approaches, we can utilize the uncertainty structures, such as covariances in the spatial, temporal, and pollutant spaces, to synergistically combine these diverse types of data. This approach allows for integrating multiple datasets into a unified state estimate, improving the overall accuracy and reliability of air quality assessments. The unified state estimate can be leveraged for further understanding the relationships between pollution sources, meteorology, the built environment, and the community’s health outcomes. 
If possible all air pollution sensors (at least 20 PurpleAirs) in the stationary outdoor air monitoring network will be temporarily collocated with a regulatory monitor or portable sensor verification system (SVS) in a secure outdoor location for a minimum of 2 weeks. Near-reference PM2.5 data from the SVS will effectively support the assessment of sensor performance against EPA’s new performance targets for Fine Particulate Matter (PM2.5) air sensors (Duvall et al. 2021) for Non-Regulatory Supplemental and Informational Monitoring (NSIM) applications. 
The PM will review the data before deployment and summarize collocation results in the progress reports. If a sensor does not meet the respective monitored pollutant criteria, the PM and CPM will be informed, and maintenance will follow the instrument manufacturer’s SOPs will be conducted, or monitor replacement will be performed by the appropriate manufacturer after discussion. 
## **Type of Monitoring Proposed: **
The proposed air monitoring approach includes a hybrid of monitoring platforms around the refinery and surrounding community to ensure comprehensive spatial and temporal data coverage, meeting CARB Blueprint's community air monitoring requirements. The hybrid system will comprise:
1.  **Drone Platform Monitoring**: Equipped with reference 3D sonic anemometers, drones will be used to measure wind vectors at different altitudes around and above the Phillips 66 refinery (P66). This platform will help the community understand the potential for vertical transport of pollutants and assess locations to investigate pollutant concentration, in line with CARB's requirement for spatial pollutant analysis. The drones will be equipped with RTK GPS, real-time clocks, inertial measurement units, pairs of Plantower PMS-6003 particulate sensors (same as PurpleAirs) and a light-weight sensor-fusion system to produce high spatiotemporal resolution wind fields that can be compared with CFD simulations.  These detailed vertical profiles are of great interest to the community as the refineries are equipped with fenceline monitors, but the emissions stacks are quite elevated above the fenceline, leaving little imagination required to worry about pollution wafting down on the communities beyond the fence. 
2.  **Fixed Transect Arrays at Urban Scale**: Two orthogonal transects will be oriented along the prevailing wind directions to monitor air movement and dispersion across urban areas. These transects will feature fixed monitoring stations positioned to capture the spatial extent of wind vectors and particulate emissions as they disperse away from P66, addressing CARB's need for community-specific data collection. There will be 20 PurpleAir sensors and 20 anemometers on these transects. 
3.  **Distributed Low-Cost Transient Data Collection**: This includes both mobile and stationary units for measuring wind vectors in various locations. It will use low cost 2d anemometers to assess horizontal wind direction and speed. These low-cost, distributed sensors will be used to monitor areas that may not be covered by fixed stations or drone monitoring, ensuring accessibility and broad data coverage as outlined in CARB's community involvement guidelines. The network will include 20 PurpleAir sensors to monitor fine particulate matter (PM2.5). This network will be strategically informed by both community input and CFD modeling performed by Hyphae to ensure comprehensive and relevant PM2.5 data collection throughout the area.

### Expected Needs and Benefits:
Rodeo is immediately adjacent to a large refinery which is permitted by EPA to emit thousands of tons of air toxics per year. This monitoring study will provide a reliable data foundation for tracking air quality changes over time and informing community outreach on pollution transport from potentially problematic refineries and other pollution sources. It will help identify when and where pollution emissions are likely to accumulate under realistic meteorological conditions, to enable future monitoring studies to distinguish refinery emissions from other pollution sources, such as ambient background levels or regional events like wildfires. ### Previous, ongoing, and proposed air monitoring and gaps that this community air monitoring will address: 
The Phillips 66 refinery has 2 open-path UV, FTIR and TLD  fenceline monitors set up along north and south fencelines that monitor and report 5 minute and 1 hour averaged ammonia, H2S, O3, Nitrous Oxide, SO2, BTEX, MTBE, Ethanol, Mercaptan, CS2, 1,3-butadiene, CO, COS, Ethylene, Methane and Hexane, as well as 6 organic gas detectors for total hydrocarbons throughout the refinery property. While this provides helpful information about what is happening along the fenceline, there are several limitations to this approach:
  - Open path optical equipment like this provides data over a large area, but fails to  distinguish between a high concentration in a narrow plume and a low concentration along the entire path. 
  - Open path optical sensors don’t work during rain, fog, strong winds, or dust/particulate events; including those generated by the refinery itself, such as sulfate aerosols generated by permitted sulfuric acid emissions, secondary aerosols from permitted VOC emissions, and combustion products from normal operations or flaring events. 
  - The fenceline monitoring path is at a fixed elevation above ground but below the level of the emissions stacks. Thus the possibility of air pollution floating over the fenceline monitoring path and then coming down on the community is unaddressed by the fenceline monitors. 
There are also regulatory monitors in place throughout the bay area, with figure 4.5 in the attached workplan figure document. below showing the Federal EPA map of north bay sensors in their purview. The nearest SO2 / air toxics sensor location is in Crockett, with no sensors in Rodeo all the way to San Pablo, thus any emissions coming from refineries southwest of Rodeo will not be detected.
The Bay Area Air Quality Management District also runs an ambient air network, and has a single H2S sensor in Rodeo at 326 3rd street. The Bay Area Air Quality Management District is planning to add more VOC monitoring station throughout the surrounding community, however these installations take a long time to be installed, and will likely by spready sparsely throughout the community and so will not inform in great detail about spatiotemporal interactions between the built environment, meteorology and pollution transport. 
There is also publicly available PM data from the PurpleAir network. However, as shown in the map (figure 4.6), there is only a single such sensor in Rodeo, and a large hole in the network between Crockett and Pinole.
Our community monitoring program can fill some of these gaps by providing detailed vertical wind profiles and a relatively dense grind of wind and air pollution measurements that can provide spatial resolution that can be used to infer built environment effects on pollution transport, and to trace the path of potential stack emissions as they move above and beyond the fenceline. Our approach of using a combination of drone anemometers and CFD simulations will provide a greater understanding of both vertical pollution transport and the influence of buildings, terrain, vegetation and meteorology on community exposure.

**Task 3: Computational Modeling **
Computational fluid dynamic (CFD) simulations have proven invaluable for analyzing the dispersion of pollutants in urban flows (Blocken et al., 2013; 2015; Tominaga & Stathopoulos, 2016; Di Sabatino et al., 2013; Pantusheca et al., 2022;  San Jose et al., 2021; Buccolieri et al., 2011). Meso-scale CFD of urban areas considers the urban heat island effect, regional wind patterns, and pollutant dispersion across broad urban regions. These simulations typically derive boundary conditions from mesoscale meteorological models (Lucas et al., 2016). Micro-scale CFD simulations of urban areas delve into the detailed flow dynamics around individual buildings, streets, and trees. These simulations are crucial for understanding local variations in pollutant dispersion and the impacts of urban design features such as buildings, street canyons, and vegetation (Blocken et al., 2016). 
In urban pollution studies, ground-level and elevated emission sources play critical roles, particularly in areas near industrial facilities like refineries. Ground-level vehicle emissions contribute significantly to air quality issues, especially in high-traffic areas, while elevated stack emissions from rooftops or industrial activities disperse pollutants differently, impacting downwind areas and potentially causing high ground-level concentrations (Pantusheva et al., 2022; Chavez et al., 2012; Bijad et al., 2016; Guo et al., 2019; Toja-Silva et al., 2018; Bahadori, 2022). 
## **Approach**
The work plan for the CFD modeling process begins with a foundational phase focused on simulating wind flow to identify optimal sensor placements for monitoring wind fields and eventually pollution concentrations. Modeling the wind flow patterns is essential, as it provides insight into local airflow dynamics, which are critical for understanding pollutant transport before advancing to detailed pollution dispersion simulations. 
The modeling process relies on three primary inputs: a domain model (digital twin), meteorological data, and traffic and pollution emission data. With these inputs, we configure the simulation by establishing the computational domain and mesh using the digital twin, accurately representing complex terrain and physical structures. Meteorological data provides critical information for setting boundary conditions (i.e., atmospheric wind forcing conditions). The digital twin integrates GIS, LiDAR, land cover, and tree survey data to create 3D models of the urban environment, assessing design impacts on wind and pollution.
The modeling phase will begin by defining the type and scale of CFD simulation appropriate for the community. Two primary simulation types are under consideration: (1) complex terrain simulations, which cover a broader area (e.g., 10–25 km across) to resolve topographic influences such as hills, valleys, and coastal effects. (2) Urban-scale (microscale) simulations, which focus on a smaller area (e.g., 1–3 km radius) at meter to sub-meter resolution, resolving detailed flow interactions with buildings, vegetation, and streets.** **Each simulation type presents trade-offs in terms of computational cost and spatial detail. Complex terrain simulations provide regional flow context but coarser resolution, while microscale simulations offer high-fidelity local detail within a limited extent. These trade-offs will be presented to the community during public meetings, where participants will guide final selections based on monitoring priorities, exposure risks, and community interests.
Selecting areas of interest (AOIs) will be driven by the community by focusing on regions highly vulnerable to air pollution, such as industrial zones near refineries or highways, areas with at-risk populations (e.g., children, the elderly, or those with health conditions), and sites with historical air quality and health data for longitudinal analysis. A preliminary review of compiled data will help identify well-covered locations with pollution readings, geospatial information, and compatibility with models like AERMOD and WRF. Urban sites will be chosen for sensor data and CFD studies based on factors such as population density and pollution levels. With input from the community, we will pinpoint high-pollution impacted areas. 
### **Wind Modeling **
CFD simulations will be performed with OpenFOAM (Weller et al., 1998), an open-source CFD solver based on the finite volume method commonly used in research and applied publications of air pollution dispersion modeling in urban environments (Pantusheva et al., 2022). OpenFOAM’s flexibility enables users to create tailored models for complex fluid dynamics, heat transfer, and multiphase flow, while allowing custom integration of environmental sub-models for atmospheric, traffic, and pollution dispersion conditions. The wind flow equations will consist of the unsteady Reynolds Averaged Navier-Stokes (URANS) with the k-epsilon turbulence model (Launder & Spalding, 1974; Versteeg & Malalasekera, 2007). These equations consist of the conservation of fluid mass, conservation of momentum, and conservation of mass of each pollutant species to be considered (i.e., PM2.5). 
For complex terrain simulations, a near ground refined mesh (\~10 m around topographic features like hills and valleys) will be used to resolve topographic effects in the broader Carquinez Strait, based on methods established by Blocken et al. (2015). Distinct roughness lengths for land and sea surfaces will be applied to improve boundary layer realism across surface transitions.
Urban scale (i.e., microscale) simulations will resolve neighborhood-level features including buildings, trees, and traffic corridors with sub-meter resolution , serving as localized zoom-ins within the broader area covered by the complex terrain simulations. These domains may partially or fully overlap, depending on the region identified by the community for detailed analysis. Built structures disrupt wind flow through effects like downdrafts, upward deflection, and reduced downwind speeds which are challenging to predict without CFD. Vegetation also impacts airflow by causing lofting, enhancing vertical mixing, and creating windbreak effects that slow winds (Janhall, 2015; Bowker et al., 2007). Urban vegetation can be modeled with porous media models, like Darcy-Forchheimer, using wind resistance data (Holzmann, 2021; Kastner & Dogan, 2022). Additionally, vehicle-induced turbulence (VIT) impacts near-road pollution dispersion by enhancing turbulent mixing (Hashad et al., 2022).
 Both simulation types (complex and microscale) will consider two boundary condition strategies: (1) ABL-based boundary conditions: using standard atmospheric inflow formulations, such as the logarithmic wind profile proposed by Richards and Hoxey (1993), suitable when computational efficiency is prioritized. (2) Mesoscale derived boundary conditions (spatially varying): incorporating output from mesoscale models like WRF to reflect realistic meteorological inputs shaped by topography, coastal effects, and urban heat island dynamics (Rodrigues et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2021; Leng et al., 2022). These boundary condition approaches are not mutually exclusive nor tied to a specific simulation type. Depending on community guidance, technical feasibility, and data availability, either method may be applied to either the complex terrain or microscale simulations. This flexibility ensures that the final modeling approach aligns with local needs while enabling robust scientific interpretation.
The computational domain and mesh are established following standard guidelines by Franke and Baklanov (2007), Blocken et al. (2013; 2015), and Kastner and Dogan (2020; 2022). OpenFOAM simulations will use customizable grids that adapt to complex geometries. Mesh creation involves two tools: (1) blockMesh for a hexahedral background mesh and (2) snappyHexMesh to fit this mesh to complex terrain, buildings, and other structures in STL format. 
### **Pollution Modeling **
Air pollutant concentrations result from a combination of regional and local sources. Regional pollution may include transported emissions from distant highways, industrial zones, or wildfire smoke, while local pollution originates from sources such as traffic, refineries, industry, and port operations. The proposed modeling framework reflects this multi-scale nature of pollution. The complex terrain simulations cover a broader geographic extent and are capable of capturing both regional background transport and emissions from large local sources, such as refineries or major industrial facilities. These simulations help characterize how pollutants move over complex terrain and across communities. The microscale simulations complement this by resolving fine-scale pollutant dispersion from sources such as on-road vehicles, building-scale exhausts, and neighborhood-level infrastructure. These models provide detailed insights into how air pollutants behave near receptors like schools, homes, or monitoring stations.
While regulatory models such as AERMOD or CALPUFF are often used to estimate background concentrations, CFD allows for spatially explicit modeling of local and regional emissions under realistic meteorological and urban conditions. External datasets, such as NASA’s SEDAC PM2.5 concentration maps can support regional baseline integration. Together, this dual-scale modeling approach delivers a comprehensive understanding of pollutant dynamics, enabling community-informed exposure assessment and supporting targeted mitigation strategies.
In this scoping phase, we aim to integrate existing pollution studies that used regulatory-grade models (e.g., AERMOD) and CFD, with guidance from the community, academic partners and regulatory experts, including BAAQMD. Initially, AERMOD will establish a baseline with computationally efficient assessments of pollutant dispersion, using EPA’s Gaussian framework to identify hotspots and distribution trends based on emission rates, meteorology, and terrain. We will advance CFD-based pollution dispersion model by developing the technical methodology and approach to do this work in future phases of funding from CARB or other grants. This dual approach combining AERMOD’s regulatory standards with CFDs detailed, high-resolution analysis will improve predictive accuracy, enabling more targeted air quality management and refined analyses. 
Pollutant dispersion in the CFD model can be simulated using a passive scalar transport framework governed by the mass-conserving advection-diffusion equation. This approach allows modeling the spread of pollutants such as PM without chemically reactive terms, focusing on physical transport and mixing. Dry deposition onto vegetative surfaces can be incorporated as a sink term, modeled as a function of pollution particle size and local leaf area density (LAD), following established formulations from Zhang et al. (2001), Steffens et al. (2012), and Tong et al. (2015). 
Emission sources for the simulation can include traffic corridors, refinery stacks, and other relevant sources identified during a literature review and community input phase. To ensure consistency with prior regulatory analyses, particularly those conducted using AERMOD for refinery permitting and CEQA documentation (e.g., the Phillips 66 Biofuel Conversion Project), we will align the emissions and source characterization with those assessments. The existing  AERMOD studies can inform the initial setup of the CFD model, particularly in establishing consistent emission rates and source locations. This ensures that the CFD simulations are grounded in regulatory precedent while extending analytical detail to finer spatial resolutions.

The project aims to use evidence-based design to inform the development of interventions that benefit the environmental health of the community. Modeling plays a crucial role in this process, serving as a bridge between data collection and the design of effective interventions. The modeling process leverages data collected from various sources to simulate the impact of different design choices on the environment, allowing for a more informed and strategic approach to community improvement.
#### Data Collection: The Foundation of the Model
The accuracy and effectiveness of the modeling process rely heavily on the quality and scope of the data collected. The project employs a diverse range of tools and techniques to gather comprehensive information about the existing environmental conditions in Arlington Park. These include:
  - **LiDAR**: This technology creates highly detailed 3D maps of the area, capturing the shape and elevation of the terrain, buildings, and vegetation.
  - **Google Street View 360 cameras**: These provide a street-level view of the neighborhood, documenting the existing infrastructure and urban design elements.
  - **Aerial thermal imagery**: This data helps in understanding the distribution of heat across the area, identifying hotspots and areas susceptible to the urban heat island effect.
  - **Fixed and mobile air quality monitors**: These measure the concentration of various pollutants in the air, providing insights into the sources and patterns of air pollution.
  - **Microclimate sensors**: These record localized weather conditions such as temperature, humidity, wind speed, and direction.
This data is then used to create a "digital twin" of the physical environment, a virtual representation that serves as the basis for the simulation models.
#### Modeling: Simulating Interventions and Predicting Impacts
The modeling stage involves using specialized software and computational techniques to simulate how different design interventions would impact the environment. This involves:
##### 1\. Developing a Range of Models
Different types of models are used to capture various aspects of the environment and provide a comprehensive understanding of the potential impacts of the interventions:
  - **Mesoscale models**: These models cover a larger geographical area and simulate regional weather patterns and air pollution dispersion. They provide context and background information for the more localized microscale models.
  - **Microscale models**: These focus on a smaller area, like the Arlington Park neighborhood, and provide a more detailed analysis of specific interventions. They can simulate the impact of tree planting, cool pavement installation, and traffic flow modifications on factors such as air quality, heat distribution, and pedestrian comfort.
  - **Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) models**: These simulate airflow patterns around buildings and vegetation, helping to understand how these elements affect pollutant dispersion and heat transfer.
##### 2\. Calibrating and Validating the Models
Before being used to test scenarios, the models need to be calibrated and validated using the collected data. This involves adjusting the model parameters and inputs to ensure that they accurately represent the existing conditions in Arlington Park. The ongoing monitoring data is crucial for this validation process, providing real-world feedback on the model's performance.
##### 3\. Testing Different Scenarios
Once calibrated and validated, the models are used to test various design scenarios and evaluate their potential impacts. This allows the design team to compare different approaches and identify the most effective solutions for achieving the project goals.
  - **Impact of tree planting**: The models can simulate the effect of planting different types and sizes of trees in various locations on factors like shade provision, air pollution removal, and wind patterns.
  - **Effectiveness of cool surfaces**: The models can evaluate how cool pavement and roofs would mitigate the urban heat island effect and improve thermal comfort.
  - **Optimization of pedestrian corridors**: The models can help in designing pedestrian-friendly streets that provide shade, reduce exposure to traffic pollution, and create a safe and comfortable walking experience.
#### Outcomes
This modeling approach provides several benefits for the Arlington Park project:
##### 1\. Evidence-Based Decision-Making
The models provide a scientific basis for design decisions, going beyond intuition and assumptions. This helps in selecting interventions that are most likely to be effective in addressing the community's environmental health challenges.
##### 2\. Optimization of Interventions
The models allow for fine-tuning the design of interventions to maximize their positive impacts and minimize any potential negative consequences. For instance, they can help determine the optimal placement, size, and density of trees to balance air quality improvement with shade provision and wind flow considerations.
##### 3\. Cost-Effectiveness
By testing various scenarios virtually, the modeling process can help identify the most cost-effective solutions. This allows for allocating resources efficiently and achieving the greatest impact within the project budget.
##### 4\. Community Engagement
The modeling process can be used to communicate the potential impacts of the project to the community in a visually engaging and understandable way. This fosters transparency and encourages community involvement in the design process.
While the modeling approach provides valuable technical insights, it's crucial to integrate it with community engagement to ensure that the proposed interventions align with the community's vision and needs. The sources emphasize the importance of involving the Arlington Park community throughout the project, from the initial planning stages to the final design development. This can be achieved through:
  - **Community meetings and workshops**: Providing opportunities for residents to share their concerns, priorities, and ideas for improving the neighborhood's environmental health.
  - **Participatory mapping**: Using mapping tools and activities to allow residents to identify areas of concern, propose locations for interventions, and visualize the potential impacts of the project.
  - **Feedback mechanisms**: Establishing channels for ongoing communication and feedback, allowing residents to provide input on the evolving design and ensure that their voices are heard.
The combination of robust data collection, sophisticated modeling techniques, and meaningful community engagement forms the foundation for the evidence-based design approach used in the Arlington Park project. This holistic approach aims to create a healthier, more sustainable, and resilient community that reflects the aspirations of its residents.

## **Active Multimodal Connectivity Planning**
A key component to reduce GHG’s and increase community wellbeing and social cohesions is to have a community that has active transportation which include multimodal means of circulating through and between neighborhoods including walking, biking, and public transportation.
In meetings about Greenspine park and corridor improvements, the Arlington Park community members have communicated that it is unsafe to walk and bike within their own community and also to to get from their community to the planned medical district greening improvements, even though it is only a few blocks away. A preliminary connectivity study was conducted and is included here and as the basis of the proposal. These preliminary studies will be further developed in partnership with community ambassadors and surveys and meetings with the community to prioritize the most critical locations for initial interventions. These will then be coordinated agencies and designs developed with the relevant agencies, including parks and recreation, Public Works and other agencies.
Existing connectivity in this region of Dallas is really bad. Freeways and lack of historic pedestrian design provide a barrier along these corridors and to the medical district from neighboring communities. Much of Dallas and the Arlington Park community specifically has poor pedestrian infrastructure. Sidewalks start and stop midblock. Many critical corridors don't have sidewalks at all or large scale infrastructure like channelized flood protection streams freeways block access. Many corridors don't have adequate tree cover over sidewalks that do exist.
Record Crossing Road, and Inwood Drive flank the community of Arlington Park and are used by people getting to and from the Medical district and the freeway, through the community, but the safety of the residents and activation of these corridors for the people who actually live there is overlooked.

## **Showcase greening projects**
These limited shovel-ready interventions aim to demonstrate innovative and impactful greening solutions specifically tailored to the unique challenges of under-resourced neighborhoods like Arlington Park. The projects will focus on creating tangible improvements while also serving as a model for future greening efforts in similar communities. This approach aligns with the EPA's focus on community-driven solutions and creating replicable models.
### Key Strategies Inspired by the Boyle Heights Project
This project draws inspiration from the successful strategies employed in the Boyle Heights project:
  - **Complete Streets Analysis**: Conduct a thorough analysis of the existing street infrastructure in Arlington Park, focusing on pedestrian safety, accessibility, and connectivity. Identify areas for improvement, such as adding sidewalks, crosswalks, and traffic calming measures. The Boyle Heights project demonstrates the value of such analysis.
  - **Sidewalk Improvements**: Prioritize the development and implementation of sidewalk improvements to enhance pedestrian safety and encourage walking. This aligns with the emphasis on pedestrian connectivity in the Boyle Heights project and the overall goal of safe access to green spaces and essential services.
  - **Tree Planting Areas**: Identify strategic locations for tree planting, focusing on areas with high heat exposure, limited shade, and potential for air quality improvement. The Boyle Heights project successfully integrated tree planting as a key component of its greening strategy.
  - **Prioritized Greening Locations**: Utilize data analysis and community input to identify and prioritize locations for greening interventions. Consider factors such as heat vulnerability, air pollution levels, pedestrian traffic, and community needs. The sources highlight the importance of data-driven prioritization in both the Boyle Heights and Arlington Park projects.
### Addressing Unique Challenges and Community Needs
The Showcase Greening Project will go beyond replicating the Boyle Heights strategies by incorporating novel elements specific to Arlington Park:
  - **Showcase Greening Projects**: This project will highlight innovative greening solutions tailored to the specific challenges of the community. For instance, where traditional street trees might not be feasible, explore options like hedgerows, car canopy trellises, living walls, and shrub trellises. This approach addresses the need for adaptable solutions in communities with limited space and resources.
  - **Community Participatory Design**: Integrate the Arlington Park community into every stage of the design process, from identifying priorities to selecting interventions. This participatory approach ensures that the project reflects community needs and values and fosters ownership and long-term sustainability.
  - **Creek Access Improvements**: Given the presence of creeks in Arlington Park, prioritize improvements that enhance access to and enjoyment of these natural assets. This could involve developing trails, creating seating areas, and improving the ecological health of the creek corridors.
### Measuring and Evaluating Impact
To demonstrate the effectiveness of the Showcase Greening Project, implement a robust monitoring and evaluation plan:
  - **Pre- and Post-Intervention Data Collection**: Collect data on key environmental indicators, such as air quality, temperature, and pedestrian activity, before and after the interventions are implemented.
  - **Community Surveys**: Conduct surveys to assess community perceptions of the project's impact on their health, well-being, and quality of life.
  - **Publicly Available Reports**: Compile the findings into publicly available reports and presentations to share the lessons learned and inspire other communities to adopt similar approaches.
### Integrating with the Green Spine Project
This project can be integrated with the ongoing Green Spine Project to create a more comprehensive approach to improving environmental health in the broader area. The Showcase Greening Project in Arlington Park can serve as a model for expanding greening efforts to adjacent neighborhoods and demonstrate the benefits of integrating community-scale interventions with larger-scale infrastructure projects.
